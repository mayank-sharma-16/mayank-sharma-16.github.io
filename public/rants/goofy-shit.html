Goofy Shit

Goofy Shit

Aug 31, 202619 min read

Life is absurd. Sometimes, it’s goofy and absurd. Recovery circles have some of the goofiest and most absurd shit I have ever seen, so here’s a bunch of them in no particular order.

Case Zero

The specific incident that motivated me to start sharing some of the goofy shit in these circles is when I had to roleplay as a passive-aggressive rapist in a group session.

Within the confluence of events that led to this moment, not a single person must have imagined that this would be the outcome:

  • The idea of using group sessions to build a sense of community and mutual support among those in recovery is a good instinct.

  • The idea of writing a letter to someone you’re angry at or sad about just to vent is one that works for a lot of people; almost everyone who goes through treatment will end up writing or reading the goodbye letters to a personification of an addictive substance or process. It can help to apply this to people and relationships we would like to leave behind as well.

  • Someone using this opportunity to vent anonymously about someone who deeply traumatized them in the past is engaging entirely in the spirit of the exercise. They did also write that they wanted to kill me, but I didn’t take that personally.

  • The two-step process of first writing the letter and then getting a response back through roleplay might be a little tricky, but it might be able to bring closure to people who would never (or can’t) voice the letter to the actual person.

  • The exercise’s stipulation that we use a communication style we try to avoid led to me being “passive-aggressive” (if you think I actually am passive-aggressive, well, everyone’s entitled to whatever opinion helps them sleep at night). In any case, it can definitely push someone into the headspace of a different style of communication which is arguably helpful.

All of this in combination led to me writing a passive-aggressive postcard while roleplaying as someone’s rapist. It reminds me of a software project where each module is doing exactly what it was intended to do, but the way they operate together at scale can produce some unforeseen and bizarre events. In this case, I have no idea if the author of the postcard found it helpful or not.

I will say though that roleplays seem to be a real hit or miss. Someone once shared that in one group roleplay session, a facilitator assumed the role of her deceased grandfather and tried to hug out the pain. It did not help them process the grief, it just added a goofy absurdity to the memory of loss.

Case One

I once called comedian Mark Lundholm a “fucktard” to his face, and then I got a free copy of his book Shortcuts: Better Ways to Better Days.

Case Two

I’m not taking advice about ambivalence from someone who spells it “ambivalance.” I’m sorry, but if you’re standing in front of a whiteboard ready to lecture me for an hour and cannot spell the topic, I am mentally checking out at best. At worst, I’m questioning the credentialing processes that allowed you to be lecturing. I’m not saying this to nitpick spelling or grammar as I make plenty of mistakes myself on this site alone, but if you’re lecturing on a subject, you have to at least care enough to spell it right.

Case Three

“I’m angry that my government is ignoring people’s rights and just pulling people out of their homes and off the streets.”

“But how does that affect you though?”

A group facilitator and a client got into a long argument over this point, and though they didn’t express the disagreement in philosophical terms, they were having a deeply philosophical disagreement that I’ve touched on through this site before.

What happens when “you can control nothing except yourself and your faith that a Higher Power will shape the world outside of you” meets “democracy requires a deliberative collective of citizens willing to think critically to hold their leaders and systems accountable?”

By the end of the conversation, I was fairly fucking horrified, because “I can’t control anything except myself” ends up being a wiser-sounding version of “I was just doing my job.” This isn’t a new revelation; psychologists, political theorists, and commentary from the recovery community have all connected this strand of hyperindividualism to complicity with authoritarian structures. What was really horrifying though was seeing how an enormous philosophical gap between authoritarianism and self-empowerment was condensed into a group facilitator yelling at a client that they wouldn’t be sober if they kept their beliefs, all started by a disagreement about a 10-minute motivational video off YouTube.

By the end, both were only more entrenched in their worldviews. The facilitator took the exchange as confirmation that clients have to be made to doubt their self-will more in order to humble themselves to receive the serenity from their Higher Power. The client, while still motivated to find recovery, grew more skeptical of institutional interventions and their capacity to understand the real world outside the facilities they maintained.

Once again, if AA works for you, great, but taking the AA philosophy as a baseline framework for recovery is inherently a sociopolitical stance, not just a spiritual one. The author of the Serenity Prayer, Reinhold Niebuhr, was famously a pro-union Socialist Party of America leader who collaborated with Martin Luther King Jr. on the issues of class warfare, international relations, and civil rights. He also hated the premises of the Oxford Group as being fundamentally fascist and delusional about producing change in the world in a way that only the most privileged people in society could embrace. William Wilson (Bill W.), the founder of AA, credits the Oxford Groups for directly inspiring the 12 steps.

So, you cannot take the political out of the spiritual or the religious. Even the distinction between “spiritual” and “religious” is fundamentally a political phenomenon. If you mean to introduce that premise into a psychoeducation class or facilitated discussion session, you’re going to run into deeply held beliefs about politics and society.

Case Four

I have had multiple classes in which the facilitator turns on a soundbowl ASMR for an hour and takes a paid nap. I have had others where we watch a Ted Talk, go around the room to answer how we felt about it (“I agree, we should make choices that improve our happiness” or something equivalently shallow), and then watch another Ted Talk. I cannot see the therapeutic value in this process, and I do not blame half the class for ignoring the rules about not being on their phones. An hour of Sudoku or texting a friend seems more therapeutic than being lectured by prerecorded “happiness coaches” rehashing corporate propaganda from a decade ago. To quote a facilitator, “I get paid either way.”

Case Five

I walked into a doctor’s office and explained how I had been managing recovery, medication, and other health issues, hoping to get advice on what works in the long-term. Instead, after telling the doctor about my recovery approach, they took out a notepad and asked me to repeat it so that they could recommend it to other patients. I came to get an expert’s evaluation, and I left having given the expert my self-evaluation. In that moment I truly felt the old adage land, “No one is coming to save you.”

Case Six

I once had a therapist attempt to be culturally sensitive by handing me a worksheet describing “Indian-American values” to, supposedly, help me discover the value system from which I could find the strength to apply coping skills. I, an actual Indian-American, am sitting in front of this person and can answer questions about my relation with my culture and the negotiation of values involved in applying those to a changing world. I have no idea what therapeutic manual recommended that was a good way to go. Admittedly, it’s not a reasonable expectation for a therapist to know about cultures around the world, but neither is it reasonable for me to explain air to a fish that’s only known water.

Case Seven

If you didn’t walk into a treatment facility using nicotine, you almost certainly will walk out using it. You might as well with all the secondhand exposure you’re guaranteed to inhale. I have been given an absurd amount of free vapes. I feel like a kid in a candy store.

Case Eight

Someone named their kid Justin. Middle name Hale. Justin Hale. Just inhale. I know I’ve brought up the “No True Scotsman” fallacy when it comes to discussions about being a “True Addict”, but this is one of those incidents that makes me reconsider that position.

Case Nine

If you’ve seen Better Call Saul, you might recall that there’s a story beat where Mike Ehrmantraut attends a support group for people who lost loved ones at the request of his daughter-in-law. While it represents a turning point for Mike when he turns away from the group and the prospect of processing his grief, the scene also depicts a real phenomenon of someone faking stories in peer support circles to elicit sympathy, compassion, and attention. Sometimes this is through a clinically-recognized Factitious Disorder, historically referred to as Munchausen syndrome, though the show doesn’t explicitly use this label. Many peer recovery groups begin with a premise of unconditional support rather than cross-talk and questioning in order to create a safe space to share their stories. Anonymity and rigorous honesty form a complex paradox, a fine line of enabling or disabling each other. People who seek attention can readily get it from these groups without the years of gradual intimacy it can take to share deeply personal traumas.

Anyways, it was surreal the first time I saw someone fake stories in a real support group. I didn’t notice it at first because you hear a lot of genuinely crazy stories when you put a group of people who live in subcultures on the margins of broader society into a single room. Also, I just struggle to pay attention when people who are not me are talking longer than 15 seconds. Over time though, I begin to notice details that don’t add up, that don’t seem like they can just be chalked up to the ordinary messy process of memory retrieval and reconsolidation that people always undergo.

Their cadence seems rehearsed, not in the way that someone with social anxiety might try to prepare their thoughts before speaking, but in the way that a standup comedian plans to pause for audience feedback. If and when it doesn’t come on some nights with a tougher crowd, a panicked expression flickers across their face as if they missed a step walking down the stairs. If they try nervously to recover, they can end up slipping between associated concepts. An ex-girlfriend becomes a fiance becomes an ex-wife, who cheated with a best friend who becomes a random Tinder hookup who becomes a sugar daddy after they drove her away through a DOC that changes each time the story is recounted. One gets the impression that the speaker is giving a new improvisation of the same rejected mad libs script.

But creating and remembering stories is exhausting, so lies and sarcastic jokes are often wrapped around kernels of truth. The most sincere part of the story this person expressed was their repeated ostracization from groups they joined, oblivious to how they were the common denominator in each of these instances, but especially oblivious to the way even their own description of events raised eyebrows. Complaining that people don’t get your sense of humor, especially when we’re talking about people in recovery who have an unusually high tolerance for dark humor, edgy jokes, digusting rock bottoms, and tough love, is a major red flag.

An interesting contradiction I noticed though is that this person, despite their vocal and enthusiastic sharing in the context of group, had almost no interpersonal dynamics outside of the meeting structure unless it was part of an emotional rollercoaster that factored into the stories of trauma they shared at meetings. In fact, they seemed to keep to themselves when the spotlight was off, so to speak. I wouldn’t make this claim if I was the only one who felt this way. It was echoed numerous times by others in private conversations, yet no one dared to break the trust and culture of the recovery group by suddenly accusing someone else of making up their story. To do so would be to put everyone on their toes for an accusation as if giving testimony on a witness stand. So we all carry on agreeing to the polite fiction that we’re all practicing rigorous honesty underneath the anonymity.

Case Ten

The idea of having to hit a “rock bottom” before you can change is really dangerous. I hear it said very often in recovery circles that someone didn’t feel they could change until they hit a rock bottom where they lose everything but achieve some “gift of desperation” that permits transformation or a “real” desire to engage in recovery. There’s an almost admirable self-awareness people can express about their ambivalence towards the cognitive dissonance behind knowing they should change but don’t think they can until they hit a point of no return.

I’d like to help by offering my own two cents. There are only two real rock bottoms: death and a state worse than death. In neither case can you be certain that you get to return.

A lucky few are resuscitated after an overdose, an unlucky fewer are resuscitated only to end up hooked to machines in constant pain and intermittent lucidity while loved ones accumulate massive debts to sustain whatever quality of life your organs are sputtering out. Families can’t just decide not to pay either; in states like North Carolina, Virginia, New Jersey, Texas, California, Arizona, Washington, and others, families can be legally on the hook for costs. Hospitals can sue to garnish wages and place a lien on shared property, especially in the cases of a healthy spouse with income or adult guardians who are entirely liable for minor dependents. Sometimes, in the chaos of emergency operations and admissions, families will quickly sign paperwork that might have them acknowledge their role as legally-bound financial guarantors for a patient’s healthcare costs. There are also some old laws that are still technically valid in over two dozen states that state adult children are liable for supporting elderly parents, though in practice government funding steps in before hospitals pursue those avenues. No guarantees though. And while all this is happening, you might have no ability to communicate that you’d like to refuse further treatment and accept whatever dignity remains in choosing death.

So, you can pick any moment to decide to change. You can declare where you are right now as a rock bottom you have no interest in breaking down to find an even deeper hole. You are condemned to have the freedom to choose to change. I recommend choosing it sooner rather than later.

Case Twelve

I’d like to break this up with something more positive than the last. The diversity of people you can meet across recovery circles is unbelievable.

I’ve met people whose families own vineyards and yachts, lawyers at major corporations and public agencies, undocumented immigrants who are the first in their families to attend a university, reformed felons who survived multiple shootings, disowned teenagers who killed traffickers in self-defense and earned thousands street-fighting, lab techs who created and tried experimental tranquilizers, strippers turned DJs, military soldiers whose butt-chugging would put every college fraternity to shame, and a lot of very sweet dogs. Granted, there’s also the occasional neo-Nazi who pops off about interracial marriage or the homophobe who loses their mind about rainbow pencils being given to kids in June, but recovery circles are decentralized enough that you can avoid a lot of those nutjobs.

It’s genuinely inspiring to see how people can form networks to take care of each other in these environments when they’re facing common problems that they can actually talk about. I can ask someone “how are you?” and it’s not just a polite formality born out of cold intimacy as the beginning of a small talk ritual, where someone feels compelled to respond with “I’m fine” or “I’m good” through a fake affect. That allows a lot more support and resources to be exchanged between people once they’re not feeling obligated to repress their experiences to maintain the polite fiction that the world is an absolute mess that feels suffocating to handle alone. I will give credit where it’s due, Alcoholics Anonymous is an incredible mutual aid network that embodies an anarchist model of human organization and autonomy.

Case Fifteen

There’s a Round Table Pizza in Lake Forest, California that, for some reason, provokes the strangest racist rants from people. I haven’t been there a single time without experiencing someone having something of a manic breakdown over racist conspiracies.

The first time, some kid in his 20s was losing his mind over the concept of interracial marriage as he couldn’t understand why someone didn’t want kids that didn’t look like they did. He then launched into a pseudoscientific rant about how scientists can infer your race based on your skeletal structure so that “race is bone deep, not just skin deep, man.”

For the record, that’s not what the science says. What researchers have actually found is that humans have undergone natural selection based on the locations they live in, so that we can make an educated guess about what part of the world one’s ancestors might be from based on some physical characteristics. For instance, someone with narrower nasal openings is more likely to be from a population that lived in cold, dry climates while wider openings correspond to warm, humid climates. So, we might guess that someone with the narrower openings is more likely to be from the region of present-day Norway than present-day Venezuela. That’s it. That’s the entire finding, which should be unsurprising to anyone who learned the first thing about Darwin’s observations of local wildlife at the Galapagos Islands in 1835. The researchers are tracking geographic correlates, not anything comparable to racial essentialism. In fact, genetic studies consistently show that there is more genetic and physical diversity within racial groups than between them.

Yet, this kid insisted he knew a lot about this science, so much so that he was regularly asked “are you a neo-Nazi or something?” He did not actually clarify that he wasn’t, he just laughed at the question being asked of him. One gets the sense that he was all too unfortunately inspired by TikTok edgelords. And also, he claimed to be white while also claiming to be descended from Italians. Apparently his research did not inform him that Italians were not considered white or equals to Northern Europeans and Anglo-Saxon tribes for centuries, and many still do not consider them as such. In the American South, Italian immigrants experienced segregation and lynchings alongside Black Americans, not to the same degree but for the same underlying reasons. FDR’s politics and calls for national unity in WWII moved the needle and brought Italian-Americans into the fold of White suburban neighborhoods distinct from Black and brown communities.

My armchair psychologist headcanon is that his insistence that he inherited great White traits from his mother is compensation for not having inherited enough traits from his father to feel masculine. It’s a shame, he’d make a halfway decent femboy if he just gave in.

The second racist rant didn’t go on as long but was equally passionate. A gentleman took great issue with how “the Jews own everything. A Jew owns PornHub, a Jew owns OnlyFans-”

One kid interrupted to save this gentleman from himself: “you mean Israeli billionaires right? There’s a difference between a government and everyday people.”

Alas, the gentleman did not take the helping hand. “I mean it’s all Jews, there’s always a Jew in charge behind the scenes.”

The anti-Semitism isn’t remotely surprising, but the gentleman’s emphasis on porn kind of was. Does he imagine that a secret cabal of Jews are trying to get Americans addicted to porn to decrease the birth rate as part of the White Genocide conspiracies? Is he speaking out of sexual frustration and dissatisfaction with his own porn habits? Is he upset about the rejection he received when he tried asking out a young woman half his age? All of a sudden, I’m no longer sympathetic to his complaints that his ex-wife moved across the country with their daughter.

People like this are why I cannot stomach the AA standards about having no opinion on outside issues. I am not opening up about the intergenerational trauma and structural differences in my lived experiences resulting from colonialism, imperialism, racism, and xenophobia in a room with people like that. And if I’m perfectly honest, I don’t much care to keep them sober either when their sobriety directly contributes to movements that produce the trauma that makes sobriety harder for others. If they insist on making recovery a zero-sum game, I insist on meeting that energy.
