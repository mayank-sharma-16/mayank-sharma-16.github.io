Natural defense: Unlocking the brain’s hidden ability to suppress addiction

Researchers at the University of Texas at San Antonio (UTSA) have made significant breakthroughs in their focus on using midbrain astrocytes as a natural mechanism for the brain to suppress addictive compulsions. Matthew Wanat, a professor and co-principal investigator (PI) of a grant provided by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), has led a project titled “Targeting the midbrain astrocyte epigenome to regulate drug-dependent behavior.”

Astrocytes are cells in the central nervous system (CNS) that support and protect neurons, and they help control the molecules that move across the blood-brain barrier. While they were initially assumed to only play a supportive role in maintaining the health and structure of neurons, newer paradigms study astrocytes as having direct relevance to neuronal activity. Specifically, Wanat’s team has focused on the way astrocytes interact with neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the midbrain that house the primary mechanisms for releasing dopamine impacted by the conditioning of chronic substance use. In brains that haven’t been exposed to habitual drug use, promoting astrocyte activity is associated with a stronger reward-seeking drives, but they do the opposite in drug-experienced brains by turning down the excitability of neurons that produce dopamine. Activating the Gq G-protein coupled receptor signaling pathway through epigenetic changes can release a steady stream of GABA and adenosine that dampen the mechanisms that produce intense cravings preceding a relapse.

Traditional neuropsychiatric drugs in substance use disorder treatments target neuronal receptors directly, which can lead to severe side effects or even transferring a chemical dependence to the drug itself. Medications that attempt to blunt the sensitivity of dopamine pathways and intense cravings can produce anhedonia and lower the rewards or pleasure experienced from any activity one engages in. Through collaboration with the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) and Astrocyte Pharmaceuticals, Wanat’s team is exploring the potential of a drug referred to as AST-004, which works as an agonist of adenosine receptors on the surface of astrocytes. This mimics a distress signal that triggers a defensive response from an astrocyte cell’s mitochondria and produces a metabolic boost through increased ATP production, which in turn triggers a series of supportive functions like cleaning up toxic levels of neurotransmitters and counteracting edema from the bodily stresses of substance use. The cumulative effect is in stabilizing neural pathways that suppress the compulsions to relapse that occur when astrocytes are drained of energy from substances that forced neurons to overwork.

Although AST-004 is undergoing active preclinical evaluation as a treatment for substance use disorders, it’s already been approved for clinical research in other neurological conditions like Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) and Acute Ischemic Stroke (AIS). Overall, this work signals a departure from the common assumption that addiction is first and foremost a dysfunction of the neurons themselves, such that pharmacological interventions must either promote neuronal connections or substitute for the mechanisms of a particular exogenous substance on which an individual is dependent. Working through astrocytes is almost like having access to a new organ that can address addictive compulsions across the board rather than studying the particular mechanisms of drug-specific dependencies. Both stimulants like cocaine and opioids like fentanyl could be addressed through focusing on the astrocyte-neuron interactions of pathways shared across addictive substances.

Northwest researchers develop vaccines to prevent opioid overdose and support addiction recovery

The Pravetoni Research Group at the University of Washington (UW) have made breakthroughs in developing a fentanyl vaccine that could teach the body’s natural immune system to not only block the effects of fentanyl and close analogs like carfentanil but reduce the intensity and likelihood of cravings, dependency, and overdoses.

Normally, fentanyl molecules are haptens, or molecules too small for the immune system to recognize. The vaccine works by attaching a molecule structurally similar to fentanyl to a carrier protein, roughly 500 times larger, that forces the immune system’s B-cells to activate and find a configuration capable of interacting with the shape of fentanyl. Later, when real fentanyl molecules enter the bloodstream, the B-cells produce antibodies capable of trapping them inside a much larger moleculer cluster that neutralizes their potential to interact with the brain. Due to the drug’s potency (studies indicate fentanyl might be 50 to 100 times stronger than heroin and morphine), the vaccine is administered alongside immune boosters and cutting-edge nanoparticle delivery systems. The kidney and liver safely break down the antibody-fentanyl clusters and toss them out through urine.

Although the idea of using pharmacokinetic immunological responses isn’t new, this breakthrough is the first opioid vaccine to ever reach Phase I human clinical trials in the United States. Unlike naloxone (Narcan) which wears off within an hour or two and leaves open the potential for re-narcotization, the vaccines last for days at full effectiveness and may even work up to a year. If it passes trials and is rolled out as a treatment protocol, it would be aimed at providing extra voluntary security for those actively trying to quit, since the vaccine would interrupt the high from a potential relapse and overdose.

Vaccines to limit the influence of other substances are actively being researched as well. A breakthrough for methamphetamine or cocaine would be especially significant since there are no FDA-approved medications to alleviate the symptoms of dependence on those substances. Unfortunately, it’s unlikely we would ever see one developed for alcohol since ethanol, the molecule that actually produces the psychoactive effects in alcoholic products, is too small for even a highly-boosted immune system to detect as a distinct marker and manufacture antibodies for.

What role does society play in making recovery from addiction harder, and how can we shift this to be more supportive?

Within recovery spaces and academic circles, there’s ongoing intense debate about how many social factors are at play in substance use disorders.

For instance, it’s incredibly common for those with substance use disorders to also have another co-occurring health issue. Studies have found that as many as 60% of those with a substance use disorder experience another mental health condition; that number rises to 80% when looking at adolescent demographics. Only 50% of adults with mental health conditions of any kind receive treatment. These numbers get worse when looking at communities of color where healthcare access is underdeveloped, strong cultural stigma against psychiatric care exists, and/or intergenerational trauma makes members reluctant to trust medical institutions. The trend towards dual diagnosis healthcare programs in recent years reflects a growing acknowledgment of this co-occurrence of mental health disorders.

If we accept the core premise of the self-medication hypothesis, that people try to choose substances that regulate some underlying issue rather than becoming dependent on whatever’s randomly available, we might interpret this data as suggesting that we as a society are systemically failing to address health issues in a majority of our population, and some people will attempt to deal with that by self-medicating with substances. Even when people do receive care, it takes an incredibly long time to adapt evidence-based modalities into institutions, especially where it concerns the insurance industry. The emphasis on CBT and derived approaches in recovery programs is in no small part attributable to incentives that insurance companies have towards manualized approaches that can be easily standardized and operationalized. Interventions with comparable or higher effectiveness like combinations of Contingency Management, MOUD, and Housing First policies) require allocating much more capital to treatments and specialists that insurance companies find difficult to calculate profit margins for covering. Yet, we live in a time when states like Minnesota have just closed 3 of their 5 CARE network facilities which were dedicated to people presenting with complex cases of substance use disorder and co-occurring disorders, due to budget considerations.

Basically, we need a lot more money to train a lot more people to try many more interventions that research has found effective, and that means we need to have the political will to overhaul major aspects of our healthcare system that put barriers in the way of connecting people who need help to solutions that work. Unfortunately, far too many people still believe that punitive measures must be the center of addiction policies, and almost no culture in the world tends to value those with illnesses as much as those who are healthy. I wish I had more optimism to share about the prospect of change, but the more I’ve looked into this problem, the more I’m convinced some type of crisis point that breaks the system will have to happen before there’s political will and public appetite for major changes.

Sponsorship without any (or at least without strict) adherence to the 12 steps

I have a strong preference for Recovery Dharma (or Refuge Recovery) meetings that still have a structure to support mentorship, which can mean either a one-on-one relationship or a circle of mentors. It’s very common for RD attendees to also attend AA meetings, so you might have a way of using an RD mentor in the place of a pure AA sponsor, who has flexible alternatives to the traditional AA approaches but still can walk you through the Steps. The main issue you might run into is that AA is far more widespread than RD, so you may not have in-person access depending on where you live.

The Minnesota Star Tribune - Minnesota closed three addiction centers, leaving dwindling treatment choices

The Community Addiction Recovery Enterprise (CARE) network is managed by the Minnesota Department of Human Services (DHS) that used to maintain five 16-bed inpatient facilities for those requiring more specialized care for more complex care than other programs might provide, especially private sector programs.

Why 16? Because the Social Security Amendments of 1965, which signed Medicaid into law, excluded institutions for mental disease (IMD) from receiving federal funds for serving patients between 21 and 65. The idea was to disincentivize institutionalizing mass numbers of people into asylums that provided poor care while simply taking unwell people out of public view while forcing states to finance their own healthcare rather than shifting operational costs onto the federal government.

Whatever the merits of this argument at the time, there is widespread criticism of this policy in the present. Recovery treatment has advanced substantially since the 1960s and includes all kinds of evidence-backed therapeutic modalities and technologies that never existed before. State officials, whose local tax dollars are insufficient to implement the most effective interventions, are caught between a rock and a hard place when deciding where bottlenecks for psychiatric care should exist. The closure of CARE facilities was framed as a necessary step towards consolidating services in regional state facilities for all types of urgent high-acuity psychiatric care, where shortages also exist, but this means more individuals with specialized dual-diagnosis needs will bounce between emergency rooms and local jails since no private sector option exists for them.

Unions like the Minnesota Association of Professional Employees (MAPE) and AFSCME Council 5 launched public campaigns to argue against the disclosure, pointing out that they were given assurances from DHS management that the facilities would be expanding their staffing and improving their quality. Their resistance preserved the Carlton faciltity that was originally planned for closure would stay open, as it was the only all-women facility among the CARE network centers. (Source) Still, after the closures and flooding that sliced one facility’s available beds in half, the capacity of the CARE network went from 80 to just 24 for the entire state. As of writing this, there are 53 people on the waitlist for the two remaining facilities. Studies cited by American Addiction Centers claim that 45% of those on waitlists end up not seeking treatment because the wait was too long and that the likelihood of someone attending rehab after having to wait before admittance is less than 50%. (source). The number of those admitted into a CARE program in 2022 were 333, which fell to 194 last year. In fairness, part of this may be due to a decline in overall cases of civil commitment for someone struggle with chemical dependence or a substance use disorder, but there was also an increase within the last year of recommitted patients. Either way, it’s not safe to declare a long-term falling demand for the services the CARE network provides.

Carolyn D. Gorman, a fellow at the Manhattan Institute, presents a report arguing for the reform, if not repeal, of the IMD exclusion:

“Analysis of hospital data and prior reforms suggests that further modest modifications of the IMD exclusion will not suffice to increase bed supply. Full repeal would be ideal, but if that’s not politically feasible, IMDs should, at a minimum, be defined as facilities with more than 108 beds (up from the current 16 beds). This would allow as many as 332 existing psychiatric hospitals (with more than 20,000 beds) to receive Medicaid reimbursement for their services.” (Source)

Quel Health launches telehealth platform for addiction treatment using GLP-1s

Quel Health, a New Mexico-based startup, announced the “the first national telehealth company built specifically around the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists for addiction,” with the nonprofit Caron Treatment Centers as a Founding Strategic Partner. The platform is available in 41 states and the District of Columbia right now with plans to expand nationally by the end of the year. Marlene C. Lira, the CEO and Co-Founder of Quel Health explains the premise of the platform:

“We live in a society in which our dopamine networks are sold to the highest bidder… We’ve built entire industries on the deliberate exploitation of the reward system, and then we treat the resulting conditions as separate diseases in separate clinics with separate specialists. Quel exists because we think that framing is incomplete, and because the science is finally filling in the gaps.”

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1), the most popular version of which is Ozempic, has only been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for Type 2 diabetes and chronic weight management, or obesity. Quel acknowledges that using GLP-1 for substance use disorders is an off-label practice, but also that it’s common to prescribed drugs off-label for substance use disorders given the complexity of these cases. Plus, there is evidence to suggest GLP-1s modulate dopamine release in a way that acts directly on the “hijacked” dopamine pathways that result from chronic substance use. A 2026 study of 600,000 veterans across three years published in the British Medical Journal (the BMJ) and authored by researchers at Washington University in St. Louis found that GLP-1 users had a reduced risk of developing substance use disorders of any kind. There was a 14% reduction in risk across the board, with 14% for cannabis, 18% for alcohol, 20% for cocaine, 20% for nicotine, and 25% for opioids. This also led to 25% fewer hospitalizations, 30% fewer visits to an emergency room, 40% fewer overdoses, and 50% fewer drug-related deaths. Overall, this is a very exciting finding that opens the door to directly addressing the “hijacked” pathways of dopamine networks that facilitate all kinds of addictive and compulsive behaviors, and GLP-1s are inherently non-addictive without causing physical dependence that substitutes for an existing one. Over 50 large-scale Phase III trials are registered with ClinicalTrials.gov specifically examining GLP-1s for addiction, and smaller randomized controlled trials have already tested low-dose semaglitude for a decrease in cravings for alcohol with those diagnosed with Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD).

That being said, delivering drugs off-label in a telehealth format can understandably raise eyebrows. Quel seems to require more manual interventions than other platforms that can use automated questionnaires to quickly screen and prescribe GLP-1s to patients, but experts still raise reasonable concerns that telehealth programs aren’t capable of the continuous monitoring and testing capabilities of in-person clinics. Personally, I think telehealth can solve a lot of problems with healthcare access that we struggle to establish with in-person clinics (especially when the article mentioned just above shows the finicky nature of funding and staffing) but I imagine this works best as a supplement than a substitute. The nature of private sector operations is that they compete with other services for the sake of profit, which means that a private sector healthcare provider has incentives to market themselves towards patients who would be better serviced by an in-person provider. As with most things, this means that the most complex cases among the most vulnerable populations might not find the care that’s truly best suited to their needs.

Why is it that even professionals like doctors and lawyers struggle to seek help for addiction, and what can be done to change this stigma?

The occurrence of substance use disorders (SUD) among the physician population is about 10–15%, which is about the same rate as the general population. About 50% of adults with any mental health condition receive treatment of any kind, while only 13 to 36% of physicians seek out treatment for mental health disorders, including SUD.

Part of this disparity has to do with the cultural perception that medical providers should be free of health conditions themselves in order to help others be free of them, despite many cultural traditions of the “wounded healer” who uses their own experience battling a disease or illness to teach others how to manage them. This creates both external pressures to present the picture of perfect health and internalized pressures that lead one to believe that one is less effective a physician if they have chronic health issues. Substance use disorders carry even more stigma than mental health disorders like anxiety and depression, as it’s easier to imagine that there’s some kind of “broken brain” leading to an inability to experience pleasure or motivation than it is to imagine the reward system breakdown between what we want and what we like that accompanies addiction. For doctors, who are expected to have developed extraordinary self-mastery after the grueling gauntlet of medical training, admitting to substance use disorders can feel like admitting to have failed in a core tenet of their education.

It doesn’t help that many state regulatory boards interrogate clinicians about their history of mental health conditions, which leads to professionals worrying that disclosing their conditions or seeking treatment could seriously impact their careers. Massachusetts and other states are beginning to dial back these probes into clinical history to only ask about present mental and physical health conditions that might affect their work.

In my opinion, recovery communities have always thrived on peer support away from the judgment of institutions, so one of the best ways to reach people is to provide non-judgmental resources delivered by anonymous peer-led formats, particularly for Physician Health Programs (PHPs) that give confidential support. Anything that avoids labels and institutional records will go a long way to working around stigma. As for addressing the stigma itself, mental health activists and advocates have historically leveraged media to tell stories about addicts that humanize them and promote empathy for the context of their struggles. I imagine we’ll just have to wait for more of those to move the needle on stigma.
