If It Works, It Works

If you like this book and feel it has been helpful in your life, don’t read the rest of this review.

I will always preface everything I have to say about life advice or self-help or spirituality with the caveat that if it works for you, you don’t need me or anyone else to approve it. I don’t know you or your life’s circumstances. I don’t know what works or doesn’t for you.

This review is for the people it didn’t work for, because they might still be looking for something that does, and understanding why this didn’t work can be helpful. I’ll tell you why it doesn’t work for me, and why I find it an issue that this book is so widely celebrated as a spiritual reading in addiction and mental health recovery programs.

This is also not an article. In fact, I will mention multiple times that YOU SHOULD NOT TREAT THIS AS AN ACADEMIC SOURCE OR A WIKIPEDIA SUBSTITUTE. You Can Judge This One By Its Cover

Bluntly, I consider this just another one of the many pop psych books out there turning a profit by appropriating the aesthetics and philosophies unfamiliar to mainstream Western discourse in order to market itself as battle-tested ancient wisdom. I say this as someone whose family’s oral storytelling traditions would date us back to Proto-Indo European groups - who noticed Deepak Chopra of all people quoted on the back cover. To many of us in the Hindu community, he’s comparable to a televangelist or a daytime TV doctor. I know they say not to judge a book by its cover, but this book is literally asking you to judge it based on the people associated with the cover. I will do commentary on Deepak Chopra’s works eventually. His latest personal controversies make him less of a figure people might look to in the future.

But okay, let’s set this aside and not just assume Don Miguel Ruiz is a Mexican or Toltec parallel to Deepak Chopra. Perhaps Ruiz genuinely does appreciate Chopra’s works, or was unfamiliar with his controversial positions, or just thought a big author delivering an endorsement would help him raise awareness for a book he believed was worth distributing. It’s not fair to totally condemn an author based on who endorses them, even if the author publicly appreciates the endorsement enough to attach it to their work. I’m willing to give people the benefit of the doubt they clearly don’t deserve. What else do we see?

The book’s cover says “A Toltec Wisdom Book.” I grew up in the American public school system, so naturally I had no idea when the Toltecs were around. If you asked me if they came before or after the Aztecs, it would be a 50/50 toss-up from my side. Luckily, I can still read, so I did some reading. The Toltec empire arose in what would be Central Mexico today from the 10th to 12th century, but its capital city was looted by Aztecs after it fell to the Chitimecs and other groups. It has been a long time since they’ve been around, and most of what we know seems to come from other peoples and cultures that took note of them as well as archaeological evidence. They don’t have much in the way of written works we can reference to understand them on their own terms.

Zuzana Marie Kosticova is a Czech scholar at Charles University in Prague who specializes in contemporary spirituality derived from Mesoamerican traditions. She published a paper funded by the Czech Science Foundation which traces the historical development of contemporary Toltec spirituality.

On the basis of his most celebrated bestsellers, Carlos Castaneda has usually been analyzed as a precursor of the emerging pre-Harnerian Neo-Shamanism, one that was chiefly inspired by the cultures of the so-called Oasisamerica and the spirituality of the Great Plains. Nevertheless, in his transitional period (the 1970s and the early 1980s), Castaneda also explored ancient Mesoamerican symbolism. The fruit of this new inspiration mainly consists of three concepts – the Tonal/Nagual, the symbolism of the cardinal points, and the Toltec. The term “Nagual” as well as “Toltec” persisted long after Castaneda’s death and proved to be one of the most popular traits of the author’s doctrine. Such contemporary celebrity authors as Miguel Ruiz owe their idea of the Toltec to Castaneda.

The term itself acquired new meanings – “Toltec” now tends to be translated as “shaman”, “wise man”, “sorcerer”, “sage”, etc. At the same time, the link to the actual Toltec archaeological culture that flourished a thousand years ago in Central Mexico gradually weakened. Toltec spirituality thus became a fully postmodern, contemporary phenomenon; its original Mesoamerican core (if there ever was one) was interpreted and re-interpreted to suit the needs of humankind in the present, with its exhausting lifestyle and constant search for awe-inspiring spiritual experience

So she straightforwardly rejects the notion that Ruiz is invoking a tradition that goes back thousands of years. In fact she very explicitly lays out that “Toltec” in reference to spirituality has been “re-interpreted to suit the needs of humankind in the present.” A harsher critic might say that Toltec has become a buzzword to invoke spiritual aesthetics detached from our available evidence about the actual populations called Toltec. “If there ever was one” implies we have never known enough to even conclude that a single Toltec culture is a coherent construct.

Kosticova traces the modern reinterpretations of Toltec to Helena Petrovna Blavatsky of the Theosophical Society. She wrote that the Toltecs were part of an ancient continent called Atlantis which was technically and intellectually advanced until it collapsed due to abuses of the elites and European imperialism. The indigenous communities in the Americas were remnants of those who fled while the supposedly superior white Aryan race now dominated the planet. Blavatsky’s claims were never adopted into mainstream historical academia, and early Mesoamerican scholars who tried to find evidence for her claims didn’t get far. The interest that was ignited into Mesoamerican cultures was primarily archaeological, so Carlos Castaneda took up the public-facing spiritual side.

Castaneda’s works have been the subject of lots of scholarship, and there’s a consensus that contemporary ideas of Toltec spirituality can be traced strongly back to his conceptions of indigenous American spiritual traditions and aesthetics. Ruiz has claimed his father was a Nagual; the term has mostly been associated with Castaneda’s popularization of Toltec spirituality and speaks to the influence Castaneda had on Ruiz’s premises. Aside from Kosticova making passing references to Ruiz as the most vocal part of Castaneda’s legacy, it seems Mesoamerican historians and anthropologists have not really commented on The Four Agreements in an expert’s capacity, which is itself significant. You can find articles written by mental health professionals that will say the book is a good primer for those seeking to explore or supplement the ideas behind Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, but they express skepticism regarding the connection to ancient Toltec wisdom. Articles written by others with Mesoamerican heritage who have engaged in public discourse routinely condemn Ruiz for taking concepts out of context for the purposes of adding the aesthetics of mysticism. Academics simply do not think the book presents serious enough claims to merit a response except to comment on the virality of it as a downstream consequence of someone else’s works.

“In the tradition of Castaneda, Ruiz distills essential Toltec wisdom, expressing with clarity and impeccability what it means for men and women to live as peaceful warriors in the modern world.” — Dan Millman, Author, Way of the Peaceful Warrior

But let’s be fair: Ruiz says he’s drawing on his own heritage and his familial oral traditions, not making a claim intended to be evaluated by academics or necessarily describing Mesoamerican culture aside from his little corner of it. It is absolutely possible that his family has real heritage they trace back to the Toltec Empire, and we do have evidence of continuous cultural and religious traditions that have survived for thousands of years. Even if the exact details don’t conform to the standards of evidence we use in science and academia, ideas like values, principles, and philosophies can persist in new forms that have evolved for new circumstances.

Consider a family recipe. We might not have evidence to say that any particular recipe existed in that form a thousand years ago, but we also might not want to challenge our grandmothers on cultural authenticity. Every family might do something differently, and we will never have the evidence to capture all the variation in human lifestyles and experiences. I would be a hypocrite myself if I said I checked my family’s oral histories against the academic archaeological and anthropological evidence available before sharing or acting on them. There’s plenty of very good reasons to be skeptical of perspectives shaped by limited or skewed evidence, such as the asymmetry of evidence preserved in European state archives by destroying other records they didn’t understand or care about.

Also, you might just not care about whether it’s verifiably authentic or not. It’s fair to apply a “death of the author” approach to take Ruiz entirely out of the picture and consider what lies between the covers on their own merits. Maybe the details don’t matter at all as long as the core truths and patterns are successfully conveyed. After all, we’re presumably interested in how to navigate life going forward where the details will never align 1-to-1 with any previous situation, and we as people also have notoriously unreliable memories that are very easily swayed through suggestions and context switches. When we get to the actual Four Agreements, we will dive into that, but I’m not done exploring the premises yet.

The Toltec

Anthropologists have spoken of the Toltec as a nation or a race, but, in fact, the Toltec were scientists and artists who formed a society to explore and conserve the spiritual knowledge and practices of the ancient ones. They came together as masters (naguals) and students at Teotihuacan, the ancient city of pyramids outside Mexico City known as the place where “Man Becomes God.”

It’s true that anthropologists have historically framed the Toltecs as an empire based around Tula, though they seem to be more nuanced now with the understanding that Toltec as a term was used also to refer to an idealized ancestor or a title for skilled masters. There isn’t really evidence to support the idea that the Toltecs were anything like an academic or intellectual network dedicated to preserving old knowledge. It’s not clear either what scientist or artist mean here either. Astronomers and artisans were certainly present in Mesoamerican cultures, but we don’t have evidence to support something like a Platonic Academy or chapter of the Templars at Tula with people dedicated to an exploration of philosophy and knowledge. So Naguals as a term for an official master who teaches students, like a guru, also doesn’t seem to hold up. It would be one thing if Ruiz had said “according to my family’s traditions,” but he takes direct aim at serious academics to contradict a consensus.

Also, Teotihuacan as a city was thriving from 100 BCE - 550 CE. The Toltecs were thriving from 900 CE - 1150 CE. That’s a gap of about 400 years. If we give the benefit of the doubt, it’s possible that Ruiz is referring to some settlement that survived where Teotihuacan was before or the ancestry of the Toltecs. In family traditions, it wouldn’t be uncommon for these details to blur.

Over the millennia, the naguals were forced to conceal the ancestral wisdom and maintain its existence in obscurity. European conquest, coupled with rampant misuse of personal power by a few of the apprentices, made it necessary to shield the knowledge from those who were not prepared to use it wisely or who might intentionally misuse it for personal gain.

Toltec knowledge arises from the same essential unity of truth as all the sacred esoteric traditions found around the world.

Though it is not a religion, it honors all the spiritual masters who have taught on the earth.

It is true that European invasions destroyed a lot of culture, and many Mesoamerican communities went underground with their practices and records to avoid targeting. But the rest of it is a very standard archetype found in spiritual communities claiming esoteric knowledge. I mean, he openly says as much. He applies perennialism, or the idea that within all religions and spiritual traditions are recurring patterns that point to universal truths.

You can find lots of New Age “spirit science” type authors and content creators using this formula: ancient esoteric knowledge from a lost group allows one to achieve unique powers, but it can be abused both by insiders and outsiders, so a special class of insiders preserved it away from the institutions that are responsible for your current suffering. Now, these special insiders believe that the present moment is the ideal time to reintroduced this esoteric knowledge an power in a way that won’t be abused. In this case, 21st century America - after the European conquerors they were hiding from consolidated even more precise control over the world. And invented nuclear weapons and drone warfare and mass media platforms. But hey, I’m not a Nagual, so perhaps they work in mysterious ways, and there’s a good reason to bring back this esoteric knowledge at this moment in a short self-help book that has gone mostly unnoticed by those who dedicated their life to the study of Mesoamerican cultures and peoples.

When I discuss Hinduism and sects that have been around for thousands of years, I very intentionally try to demystify it and avoid the term “esoteric”. These are traditions that have evolved across thousands of years through lived experiences, not in some secret cave or ashram even if that’s where the founding myths go. Different generations adapt concepts for different sociopolitical circumstances. Hindu belief systems are not ancient esoteric wisdom; just a different formulation of life than Western Eurocentric Abrahamic perspectives that have held international hegemonies for the last few centuries and counting. The lack of a central canon or authority in Hinduism makes it seem indecipherably complex, but if you just start with one sect and make an honest attempt to internalize it, you can start to understand the other branches in terms of the differences. You just need to start with one sect to acquire the vocabulary. I might even recommend starting with Buddhism as an easier way to unlearn assumptions from an Abrahamic culture.

Lots of ancient Hindu discourses are written as critical commentaries that pick at specific verses of older texts and dialogues between a teacher and a student, with the teacher often being very forward about the limitations of their own knowledge at the time so that the student may choose their next step. Gurus and religious leaders may exercise political authority in an area, but there’s no Pope who delivers a final word or determines the heretics to be excommunicated are. In many stories, you can find a deity or wise sage remark that it’s improper for someone to explain their own history and background. There should be others who will be your historians.

There’s a noticeable instinct in the West to take the perennialist stance because it supports the idea that there are some common denominators in the belief systems of cultures around the world that can be operationalized by existing Western institutions. The binaries like Heaven and Hell, good and evil, angels and demons, God and Satan, etc., are aspirationally seen as universal. That frames the West as having a kind of liberalized operating system that can accommodate every other system because it exists transcendentally above other systems as the ultimate rational discourse. After all, if you admit you don’t have the knowledge or background to understand something, how could you hope to control it? I think there’s an element of denial that the Western American way is not THE way of progress that the world must or should follow, even if we love what America has been and want to preserve it.

Perennialism is nothing new though. It predates the West in any way we know it now. Early Christian scholars in the first few centuries AD were doing the same thing with Christianity and Greek philosophy in order to argue for greater legitimacy in elite circles. Some made arguments that Plato’s conception of a divine madness or guidance, including the daemon Socrates claimed he had as a voice that would intuitively guide him away from bad decisions, was an incomplete observation of the presence of the Holy Spirit. The Bible is reasoned to be a way for God to introduce these lost truths to humanity. Renaissance elites and scholars like Leibniz took a renewed interest in this line of reasoning when seeking to reinterpret classical Greek and Roman works on new human-centric terms.

However, the current strain of perennialism that’s common in Western nations stems back to American transcendentalism, and the perception among American authors like Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau that the “Oriental” cultures of the East held ancient spiritual insight that was lost to a radically material and rational Western society. This isn’t really about understanding Hinduism, Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, or any other tradition at all but reinforcing the image of the West as suffering from the success of their own hyperrationality and scientific materialism. An alternative interpretation would have been recognizing that these Eastern traditions have very sophisticated logical and scientific philosophies of their own that are closely interwoven with ideas from spiritual traditions.

As a result, they flattened the “Oriental” or “Asiatic” schools of thought into a set of “Oriental” stereotypes that focused on direct experience and personal awakenings through mysticism rather than rationality. I would argue these ideas are actually quite close to Protestant revivalism traditions of experiencing some kind of conversion as a result of an encounter with God or Jesus, and that this framework was imposed on a diverse set of traditions that do include spiritual awakenings, but also a lot more. Many traditions have very nuanced and regimented approaches to meditation, rituals, prayer, yoga, contemplation, inquiry, tutelage under predecessors, and the proper time and space to internalize these concepts into experience. Logic, religion, spirituality, philosophy, etc are not necessarily separable into disciplines or institutional authorities the way the West has imagined it.

The American transcendentalists may have engaged in Orientalism to construct an Other out of the East, but it was out of ignorance rather than malice or deliberate condescension. Where it became a problem is, as we discussed above, Blavatsky and the Theosophical Society. She actually began articulating a system of ideas behind perennialism. Weird ideas though.

Blavatsky proposed a cosmology for humanity that inspired a lot of pseudoscientific beliefs about races. Specifically, she proposed that humans involved out of a set of root races. The first two were the Polarian and Hyperborean races that had no physical form. The third were the Lemurians who were giants that existed alongside the dinosaurs. The fourth were the Atlanteans who sank into the ocean, including the Toltecs, because they were technically and scientifically advanced with psychic powers but suffered spiritual corruption. The fifth are the Aryans who represented the peak intellectual and physical development of the human species so far with a new spark for higher understanding. The term “Aryan” was really referring to the family of languages associated with Indo-European groups, where my own family links its heritage according to oral storytelling tradition and records as far back as we can tell. You can still find people in India, especially elders, who will use the term “Aryan” to describe their origin. Blavatsky repurposed it as a singular race, and existing indigenous non-European communities across the world were leftovers of the “half-animal” races that came before.

During the 1960s with countercultural New Age movements, the anthropologist Carlos Castaneda claimed to have received apprenticeship under a Yaqui shaman and began reintroducing Mesoamerican concepts into the alternative spirituality scene. Castaneda has inspired actual interest from academics, not for his claims about Toltecs but for introducing the contemporary conception of Toltecs as a mystical society rather than just an ethnicity or name for a network of peoples around a particular polity. If anything, archaeological evidence suggests the Toltecs were highly militaristic in their expansion.

The closest comparison I can think of to this situation is Sparta. The Spartans were militaristic in their expansion over the Greek city-states as well, and they weren’t very interested in creating a body of literature as a legacy. Most of what we know comes from others who encountered them, and those others always had some kind of an incentive to promote a certain picture. A historian who was critical of Athenian democracy might play up the efficiency of Spartan hierarchies and militarism; a historian who favors Athenian democracy and diversity might play up the rumors about barbaric practices in Spartan training systems. Sparta itself enjoyed leaning into the reputation that they were trained as children to be fearless warriors who would die in a battle before they ever surrendered, even though most Spartan citizens were landed gentry with a slave underclass through Helots. The Toltecs seemed to have undergone a distortion in the other direction: they’re seen as the stewards of ancient wisdom and truths rather than the brutal expansionist empire represented by Spartans.

Castaneda encouraged his followers to “erase their personal history” as a means of freeing themselves from the attachments that hold back warriors from living entirely in the present without the definitions or restrictions of a society around them. This includes family history. In the early 1970s, Castaneda achieved celebrity status and subsequently, celebrity scrutiny. His claims of being born into a high status Brazilian family was challenged by records showing he was a Peruvian immigrant who left a family behind and changed his name. His response to these contradictions in the claims about his background: “to ask me to verify my life by giving you my statistics is like using science to validate sorcery. It robs the world of its magic and turns us all into milestones.” Like many New Age movements, Castaneda eventually used his ideas about erasing your identity to convince a small group of women to completely cut off their “energy vampire” families, legally change their names, and burn all identifying documents. When Castaneda died in 1998, these women drove into Death Valley and, based on the remains found, appeared to have committed mass suicide.

Ruiz has framed himself as a contemporary peer of Castaneda, a “great teacher” he credits for introducing the “Toltec wisdom” of their shared oral storytelling traditions to Western discourse. But as we saw above, the actual Mesoamerican scholar Kosticova has very explicitly said Ruiz conception of the Toltecs and use of the term Nagual implies he was directly inspired by Castaneda. We can at least say that his New Age characterization of the Toltecs is incomplete at best if it doesn’t make any mention of their militarism. In fact, some critics argue that there’s a kind of commodification of culture happening that allows the sale of aesthetics or a sanitized version of history without any of the substantive engagement with the “European conquest” that drove it underground. Erasing the past is certainly a very convenient framework for those who may not want to take accountability for their past.

Ruiz claimed he was a Western medical doctor and neurosurgeon who had an out-of-body near-death experience during a car crash in the 1970s, after which he began a new journey to understand his family’s Toltec history and spirituality. It seems that journalists who tried to check his medical degree, residency paperwork, or hospital affiliations did not find evidence that he was a practicing neurosurgeon. Nevertheless, he charged tourists thousands of dollars to take them on a “Power Journey” through Teotihuacan over the objections of actual residents and elders who feel their sacred architecture is being commodified. In 2002, after a heart attack, Ruiz indicated his sons would be taking over as Toltec guides to manage his businesses under the claim that they were uniquely qualified because of the family’s oral tradition. Setting aside that he had indicated others like Castaneda existed who carried the same oral traditions, there have been criticisms that his sons have lived mostly Westernized lives. To put it crudely, if Castaneda was like Steve Jobs, Ruiz is like Jeff Bezos; less of a cult based on a personality, more of an efficient corporate structure that can be handed off.

So if all Ruiz wanted to say is that he shares is his own family’s oral traditions, this review wouldn’t exist and a lot of skeptics challenging it would probably be far less critical. But he’s making historical and philosophical claims about other peoples and cultures in the process. That’s hard to look past, especially given how commonly the book shows up in mental health and recovery spaces that have been underserved by traditional institutions that fail to address complex health conditions, and the lack of academic engagement with anything in the book aside from a nod to its celebrity status. That leaves a very wide space for unfalsifiable or unverifiable literature to opportunistically fill in the gaps. Many Hindus would put Deepak Chopra in the same class of opportunists.

Smoke and Mirrors

Three thousand years ago

Yes, there’s a problem already. Again, the archaeological evidence we have suggests the Toltecs emerged around 900 CE, about 1100 years ago. So we’re going back 2000 years prior to the existence of the Toltecs, closer to the era in which the Sanskrit Vedas were composed and distributed. The Vedas actually were transmitted orally and through family lineages for centuries with an extremely rigorous set of practices and rituals ensuring its transmissions. Contrary to what many expect, successful oral storytelling is a sign that a culture has allocated a lot of time and effort to ensuring knowledge would be transmitted. It takes a group of experts who would study everything from mnemonics, hand gestures and choreography, grammar, vocabulary, music, fashion, natural resources, performance spaces, and much more with a cohesive goal of preserving a set of values and norms. For centuries, there were rules against trying to write down the Vedas to establish a canon outside of a group of experts dedicated to it. Eventually the Vedas would form the foundation for universities that attracted people from many different communities to participate in the preservation and development of this knowledge. So I have no problem following some of the esoteric formulaic beats of the Toltec narrative, but we might reasonably expect to see archaeological evidence of an effort to preserve these traditions from a culture this old and once-powerful. Oral storytelling does not just mean telling stories at night to your kids or showing up to a festival once a month where the community’s youth prepare a dance.

The only way a Toltec society could have existed 3000 years ago is if you adopt the timeline proposed by Blavatsky about the hierarchy of Atlantean races and its survivors.

One day, as he slept in a cave, he dreamed that he saw his own body sleeping. He came out of the cave on the night of a new moon. The sky was clear, and he could see millions of stars. Then something happened inside of him that transformed his life forever. He looked at his hands, he felt his body, and he heard his own voice say, “I am made of light; I am made of stars… Everything is made of light… and the space in-between isn’t empty.”

Then he realized that although he was made of stars, he was not those stars. “I am in-between the stars,” he thought. So he called the stars the tonal and the light between the stars the nagual, and he knew that what created the harmony and space between the two is Life or Intent. Without Life, the tonal and the nagual could not exist.

Apparently, that’s not what tonal and nagual mean outside of Castaneda’s repurposed usages.

Remember that The Four Agreements is not a peer-reviewed academic book that has had Mesoamerican scholars review his usage of these terms. It was published by Amber-Allen Publishing, which grew steadily in the 1970s after acquiring the rights to publish The Seth Material by Jane Roberts. Roberts said she would become possessed by a spirit named Seth who told her to tell humans “you create your own reality” not limited by a measurable material world. In 1994, the company published a hit with a small 100 page book called The Seven Spiritual Laws of Success by a physician named Deepak Chopra. Well, that might help us answer how Chopra’s glowing recommendation ended up on the back cover of Ruiz’s book. Just two years later, the company’s owner Janet Mills met Ruiz through a friend and attended one of his “Power Journey” tours. She helped streamline the beliefs that Ruiz held into something closer to Chopra’s hit book.

At the same time, in the celebrity Hollywood world, Ellen DeGeneres had been blacklisted after coming out as gay. She got involved in the Southern California spiritual healing communities where Ruiz was building his business with Amber-Allen’s growing network. She and her circle eventually endorsed the book avidly on her own show, then brokered a deal with Oprah to interview Ruiz for a magazine. This catapulted the book into popular culture, but at no point did it seem to get a stamp of approval from anyone with subject matter authority over Mesoamerican cultures. Terms like tonal and nagual became associated with Ruiz’s definitions, borrowed from Castaneda’s repurposing, and bypassed the understanding we have from other evidence.

Fray Bernardino de Sahagún’s Florentine Codex (1577) and Fray Alonso de Molina’s Vocabulario (1571) both pre-date Castaneda by several centuries in their use of the terms tonalli and nahualli, both of which are specific terms in the classic Aztec language (or language group) Nahuatl. Tonalli refers to a kind of force of human vitality granted by creator deities in utero, linked to the heat of the sun through a link with an animal companion based on the solar calendar. Nahualli seemed to refer to a class of magic practitioners who could detach their soul to shapeshift their bodies into animals, although European friars often translated this just to “witch” or “necromancer.”

Life is the force of the absolute, the supreme, the Creator who creates everything. This is what he discovered: Everything in existence is a manifestation of the one living being we call God. Everything is God.

Ruiz seems to use these words to mean that there is a dualism between the material world and an energy that holds that material world together. He uses tonal to mean the first and nagual to mean the second, but also that Intent or Life is the the substrate that connects both. This initially appears to be a kind of non-dualism that suggests the interconnectedness of all things. On the surface, this sounds a bit like Spinoza: “Deus sive Natura” (God or Nature) is how he referred to the universe. Many Hindu non-dualist belief systems, including the ones I lean on, make a similar framing for a single consciousness pervading the universe of which matter and space are both made. Thus, the mind (immaterial) and body (material) dualistic system are different expressions of the same single mode of a transcendental substance. In this case, the material tonal and immaterial nagual are bound together with Intent or Life, and all of these things are considered to be of Deus sive Natura.

As we go forward, we’ll see that this is actually just window dressing for an extremely radical dualism. A critical perspective might argue that he wants the aesthetics of non-dualism that is more common to non-Western conventions and traditions, but he doesn’t want to actually change the radical dualism and individualism that forms the basis of “mind over matter” arguments.

Let’s throw an olive branch out here though. As long as people are tapping into their empathy to try and see parts of themselves in others, it’s arguably a net improvement to someone’s approach or worldview, The ins and outs of dualism or non-dualism are perhaps just academic details for philosophy majors and religious scholars to quibble over. It still encourages people to relate to each other rather than make another Other, right? To a point. Because right away Ruiz writes:

In those few moments he comprehended everything. He was very excited, and his heart was filled with peace. He could hardly wait to tell his people what he had discovered. But there were no words to explain it. He tried to tell the others, but they could not understand. They could see that he had changed, that something beautiful was radiating from his eyes and his voice. They noticed that he no longer had judgment about anything or anyone. He was no longer like anyone else.

He immediately sets up a justification to tune others out. If you have some moment of enlightenment through which you learned something you have difficulty explaining to someone, you can just declare them to be in a dream state while you have unique awareness. After all, if you’re God, you can declare your own truths for the universe. You can define cause and effect any way you want. So this starts with the notion of unity and universality as is common for post-New Age perennialist spiritual movements, and then quickly proposes that you adopt a system of knowledge that insulates itself from challenge and dialogue.

For contrast, I’ll share a parable from many Hindu sects that grapple with the same core themes about recognizing your nature as a divine impermanent Self and communicating it to others, but without creating a contradiction where

In the Chandogya Upanishad, both the king of Devas and the king of Asuras sought the truth of the Self from their father and creator Prajapati, who said to know the Self was to have all your desires satisfied. Devas and Asuras get mapped to gods and demons, but that’s forcing it onto a moralistic Abrahamic duality. Instead, the Devas were born of Aditi, associated with the order of the infinite cosmos, and the Asuras were born of Diti, associated with the limitations of material reality. Both are required in order to keep the universe in a state of constant evolution; when the Asuras challenge the Devas for control of the cosmic order, the Asuras are forced to evolve their administration of that order to counter a new challenge.

When the kings of both factions met their father, for 32 years he said absolutely nothing to them. The kings instead presumed the role of students and performed chores until Prajapati chose to ask why they visited him. When they asked to learn how to find their true Self, he instructed them to look into a pan of water with their own reflection, and he said that is the Self (I’m simplifying a much longer and more poetic dialogue). The king of the Asuras interpreted this as the Self being the material form looking back at him, and so those who are materialistic and chase the manipulation of material form are said to have an Asuric disposition of the Self. Faith, charity, and insights into the infinite nature of reality are lost on the materialist Self. He was satisfied with his answer and returned to share his answers with the other Asuras.

Indra, king of the Devas, began his return to his faction until he was struck with doubt. With this materialist conception of the Self, the Self changes and dies alongside the body. But if knowing the Self is to have all desires in all worlds, then a mortal material Self is too finite to be the complete Self. So, unsatisfied, Indra returned and raised his objection to Prajapati, who was happy that Indra observed this and instructed him to stay another 32 years with him. This time, Prajapati said that the Self that moves about in dreams is the immortal and permanent. Indra once again began his return to the Devas only to doubt the answer again on his way. The dream-Self still suffers from pain, from fear, from distress. It too cannot be the complete answer to understanding the Self. Indra returned and spent another 32 years in austere contemplation.

Now after a total of 96 years, Prajapati said the Self that is completely asleep with no dreams or experiences is the true immortal and permanent Self. And yet again Indra walks away to have doubts that lure him back. The Self that is completely asleep is indistinguishable from total annihilation. There is no awareness, which an immortal and infinite Self should have of its Self. This answer still did not satisfy Indra, who returned to the instruction of only a 5 year wait this time. After 101 years, the king of Devas was finally shown the complete answer: the real Self is the Silent Witness - pure consciousness that only dwells inside a body for a while to observe its infinite nature from a finite one. When this Self no longer identifies with the body, it no longer identifies with the suffering of the body. Life will still involve pain and sorrow, but the psychological suffering that comes from identifying with the body can be eased. The anxiety, the shame, the resentment, the stress of maintaining an image or reputation, the self-blame for mistakes or bad choices, etc. can all be eased by identifying with the Self in its infinite capacity and separating oneself from the impermanent finite ego. From there, even pain is an opportunity to experience healing and develop empathy for more people. As is often said in Buddhist-inspired recovery groups, great pain does not erase goodness but informs it.

My interpretation of Shaivism as a result is not about removing storms from your life. It’s about recognizing that an immortal Self sits in the eye of the hurricane while you weather it. The identity of the observer and the observed collapse into the same continuous reality. Intense emotions like grief, love, anger, and sorrow are instances of the universe contracting itself from its immortal Self to create an experience that only a finite Self could have. Even something as painful and awful as the grief of losing a child to illness or accident is a deliberate creative design to produce a new experience. Creation is not an act of generosity nor punishment. It doesn’t confer authority to the creator. It is pure creative expression detached from the outcome, mischeivous and reckless, awesome and awful. I pursue meditation with the goal of internalizing this awareness.

These are not necessarily easy concepts to absorb, even if the practice to absorb them is simple. But that’s the point: even the kings of deities must repeatedly pursue the truth with a singular focus in order to understand it and the Self. The parable is a guide to what you should do if you want to internalize complex and difficult ideas. It is a call to humble yourself in pursuit of knowledge. It is not a distillation of truth about God and human nature that has just been established to be inarticulable into four cliches that are shorter than weekly reading assignments for a standard undergraduate class. It is not a promise of Heaven on Earth or 100% control of your life that Ruiz literally promises later on. It took Indra 101 years to understand his own nature while humbling himself to a student who scrubs floors and waits patiently and silently for the master to share wisdom, and the opportunity to discover one’s nature was available to the Asuras as well. There are none who are included or excluded from knowledge on the basis of any essentialist conclusion you might extract from an impermanent world. As humans, 101 years of focus seems daunting and unattainable for most within a single lifetime, but luckily we are not always starting from scratch thanks to thousands of years of discourse from our traditions.

So when someone claims to have uncovered their true nature in a single dream in an exotic cave or bright light induced by hallucinogens but can’t actually articulate what they actually went through to anyone else, I am skeptical that this is meaningful universal insight instead of a cool personal experience. When there is no discourse or evidence of discourse that relates at all to claims about an oral storytelling tradition going back millennia and the actual vocabulary in the story appears only decades ago, I have to remind myself to keep an open mind because there was a lot of chaos introduced into the intellectual traditions of indigenous Mesoamerican communities. When scholars of the community that this oral storytelling tradition comes from either completely disregard it as celebrity authorship from a New Age publishing company or point out that the other prominent author claiming this oral storytelling tradition formed a mass suicide harem, both my eyebrows are very much raised while I squeeze out the last drops of charitability I’m capable of. When the author has a profitable company that sells trips to sacred sites with the promise of experiencing a similar recognition of your nature as his Smokey Mirror parable over the objection of actual residents, and that he seems to have set up his sons to inherit that business and keep it going, I would start firmly identifying as a skeptic of every claim this person makes given their undeniable conflicts of interest. When I see that the path to its popularity went through celebrities like Ellen and Oprah and that the New Age publishing company behind it just happens to do most of its business in Southern California communities known for picking up celebrities with mental health struggles, I think we have a lot of dots that we can start connnecting about the motivations behind this book.

The story I shared also sets a precedent for transmitting knowledge through experiences that cannot be articulated easily: the master-student relationship. Many 10th century Shaivist sects I enjoy following discussed their spiritual philosophies in private within their own homes in a small circle of peers or family rather than isolated monasteries. The master’s role was not just to impart knowledge or guide experiences, but to evaluate the psychology of their students as well. Sometimes there were standard initiation phases which still included something resembling a spiritual awakening and virtue-building tasks that make one of service to their community. If a master felt that a student was learning to feel superior to others or used their knowledge for materialist or egoistic reasons, they would withhold further knowledge until their behavior and attitude were adjusted. Much of these characteristics are shared by some Zen Buddhist traditions where monks in initiation ceremonies would go through a rigorous interview over a koan. If they recited a prepared answer rather than engaging fully with original argumentation about the koan, they could be ferociously reprimanded. To maintain this kind of personalization and ensure the passage of knowledge to someone emotionally and mentally equipped to pass it on themselves, a small cohort or individual relationships would be necessary. Scaling it through lectures and textbooks would be impossible. People might just take whatever they need to rationalize their whims without experiencing a real challenge that forces them to question themselves and change.

So, this is a long way to say: esoteric traditions that discuss passing on the knowledge stemming from direct experience or spiritual awakenings do exist. They existed at the same time as the Toltecs even according to Ruiz’s bizarre timeline. They were also driven underground by not just European invaders but a whole host of occupying forces from around the world, both competing regional powers and distant invading empires. This is a problem that has had many solutions in many places across the world, and we have evidence of how they attempted to address it. We have no such thing for the Toltecs, save for Ruiz’s claim to an oral tradition that was also Castaneda’s oral tradition. The parable Ruiz shares is much closer to the radical individualism of modern Western institutional premises than the ideas we might expect for societies like the actual Toltecs and their Mesoamerican successors. Militaristic societies might be more likely to preach about collectively sacrificing for the greater good rather than encouraging everyone to do whatever they want as if in some free flowing utopian commune.

We Live Inside a Dream, But Who is the Dreamer?

What you are seeing and hearing right now is nothing but a dream. You are dreaming right now in this moment. You are dreaming with the brain awake.

This isn’t inherently objectionable to me. As the parable above showed, Hindu sects commonly perceive a Self that exists through multiple levels of consciousness. Being awake, dreaming, and having dreamless sleep are distinct stages just within Indra’s 101 year journey alone to understand the Self. “Nothing but a dream” seems like it’s expressing a value judgment though, as if something isn’t real or is less real just because it’s a dream. This distinction will come up again later.

Before we were born the humans before us created a big outside dream that we will call society’s dream or the dream of the planet. The dream of the planet is the collective dream of billions of smaller, personal dreams, which together create a dream of a family, a dream of a community, a dream of a city, a dream of a country, and finally a dream of the whole humanity. The dream of the planet includes all of society’s rules, its beliefs, its laws, its religions, its different cultures and ways to be, its governments, schools, social events, and holidays.

We are born with the capacity to learn how to dream, and the humans who live before us teach us how to dream the way society dreams. The outside dream has so many rules that when a new human is born, we hook the child’s attention and introduce these rules into his or her mind. The outside dream uses Mom and Dad, the schools, and religion to teach us how to dream.

Attention is the ability we have to discriminate and to focus only on that which we want to perceive. We can perceive millions of things simultaneously, but using our attention, we can hold whatever we want to perceive in the foreground of our mind. The adults around us hooked our attention and put information into our minds through repetition. That is the way we learned everything we know.

There are some good claims and some bold claims here.

Ruiz argues that objective reality is really composed of individual subjective interpretations that he calls “dreams”, which is perhaps stretching the term to encompass opinions, beliefs, inferences, information, knowledge as justification, preferences, and imagination all at once.

Insofar as Ruiz is asserting that many of our behaviors and concepts are conditioned into us by others since we were children, this is a fine assertion. I could not agree more that we have institutionalized and operationalized so much of our lives that children are taught to conform and memorize rules rather than engage with the world with their own faculties. Children know this too, which is why so many will resist the idea of sitting in a classroom for 8 hours with strange adults who grade them like livestock. We routinely fail to educate our children on the value of education. They just learn the rules they individually need to follow in order to avoid consequences in the institutions they find. We incentivize them this way, and they react accordingly. We have no right to blame children for cheating if we show them a society where cheaters get ahead as an unspoken rule just because we add aspirational spoken ones on top. They are conditioned to our conditions.

That’s not the only way we learn things though, or else every generation would look the same as the last. Doesn’t the The Four Agreements itself rely on the premise that it has knowledge that will cut through these conditioned rules? How else could humans have learned anything to begin with in the first place to pass down through the oral storytelling traditions Ruiz proposes? And what about rebellions and radical uprisings? Certainly the American Revolution or Civil War or World Wars were not the product of rules and conditioning. People are obviously capable of having original thoughts as the result of processing a unique environment through a unique brain. You might have agency over what you do once have those thoughts, but those thoughts emerge because our brains are active inference organs that constantly filter and synthesize inputs. You can choose actions no one else has and experience a reward or punishment all on your own. You’re not a computer program with hardcoded instructions based on your childhood.

Perhaps this is only a critique of Western institutions from a specific Toltec perspective, so this rhetoric is merely exaggeration intended to establish a contrast, but the sweeping nature of these claims is a problem. Again, if this was just a personal entry about family history, this would not be an issue. Ruiz is making claims about history and knowledge themselves with the goal of having millions of people apply it who are looking for alternative approaches to life than what they grew up with. It is wrong at best to broadly claim “That’s the way we learned everything we know.”

In fact this already contradicts the Smokey Mirror parable he told just a couple pages ago. That person received a dream in which they received a spiritual awakening and new knowledge he could not transmit (nor could anyone else until Ruiz apparently). So he both acquired knowledge independently and then failed to hook the attention of others into understanding it, even though this knowledge made him see his nature and everyone’s nature as God. The only implication I can think of that doesn’t lead to a contradiction is the premise that some people are simply capable of receiving awakenings to realize their nature’s relation to God while others are not. That has a certain predestination quality to it.

The outside dream hooks our attention and teaches us what to believe, beginning with the language that we speak. Language is the code for understanding and communication between humans. Every letter, every word in each language is an agreement. We call this a page in a book; the word page is an agreement that we understand. Once we understand the code, our attention is hooked and the energy is transferred from one person to another.

It was not your choice to speak English. You didn’t choose your religion or your moral values — they were already there before you were born. We never had the opportunity to choose what to believe or what not to believe. We never chose even the smallest of these agreements. We didn’t even choose our own name.

As children, we didn’t have the opportunity to choose our beliefs, but we agreed with the information that was passed to us from the dream of the planet via other humans.

The only way to store information is by agreement. The outside dream may hook our attention, but if we don’t agree, we don’t store that information. As soon as we agree, we believe it, and this is called faith. To have faith is to believe unconditionally.

That’s how we learn as children. Children believe everything adults say.

This is a claim called Doxastic Voluntarism, which assumes humans have complete control of what they choose to believe or hold as information. This is not at all a consensus found in the works of epistemologists. Let’s take a walk down the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

We have versions of this argument dating back to Epictetus of the Roman Stoic movement who framed opinions as a type of agreement to an impression that is given to us. Aristotle sharply disagreed, reasoning that our imagination is limitless but our opinions are forced into subjective decisions about how true a statement is likely to be, and that subjectivity depends on our beliefs. Early Christian writers like Clement of Alexandria and St. Augustine of Hippo began interpreting voluntarism very differently with a moralistic component; you had to have the courage to choose to believe in God before you could come to an understanding of reality. It also meant that maintaining faith was a more important goal than having the literacy to interpret the Bible. As Anselm of Canterbury said: “I believe so that I may understand.” Some medieval era writers like St. Thomas Aquinas went even further to suggest that all belief is voluntary and separate from knowledge. Others completely disagreed. Robert Holcot pointed out that it’s actually quite unreasonable to expect a human to deny all their sensory experiences and evidence-based reasoning skills in favor of one side of a contradictory belief system, especially if both seem open for questioning:

I argue that it is not in the power of man to make something appear to be in reality completely as a proposition denotes. Therefore, it is not in the power of man to make a proposition appear to him true; nor, as a consequence, that he believes that to be true. For man cannot believe some proposition to be true unless it appears to him true; nor something appear to him true unless it appears to him, thus, to be just as that proposition denotes. If, therefore, it would be in the power of man to believe a proposition proposed to him, for which, also, he does not have a compelling reason, it would be in man’s power to make a thing appear to him just as he wants. And, thus, there is a contradiction: I believe that this proposition is true, and that proposition does not appear to me to be true.

Or to put it very bluntly, voluntarists might say “you can’t understand anything until you first make the choice to believe in God as the first cause of all things,” and involuntarists might reply “I find it hard to make myself believe I’m not on fire when it seems I am on fire.”

Rene Descartes

The entire field would be dramatically simplified if this was the whole extent through which the human brain assembled a model of reality. I would argue this is an incredibly radical position. Motivated by the work over Plato’s Theaetetus, Western epistemologists have commonly used the concept of a “Justified True Belief” (JTB) as a starting point to frame knowledge as a proposition. So for any proposition of knowledge P:

  • you must (B)elieve it so that your internal psychological state is aligned with the proposition
  • the proposition must be aligned with T(rue) external reality
  • you must have (J)ustification for accepting the proposition as a True Belief, which in theory excludes luck

Gettier argued in 1963 that you can have a Justified True Belief that still does not count as knowledge through lucky guesses. If you have a True Belief and a Justification, but the Justification was tied to luck or an unverifiable mechanism, then it’s possible to be correct with a JTB but not actually operate on knowledge.

Ruiz has radically rejected the parts about Truth or Justification here, arguing solely that an Agreement (forged out of attention and reptition) to a Belief constitutes Dreams which constitutes Reality framed as the Dream of the Planet. And this is not as a result of rejecting Westernized radical materialism or anti-mysticism, which I would have a lot of sympathy for. For that, look again at Hinduism and Buddhism to see alternative epistemological frameworks.

The Nyaya school of logic, which falls under “orthodox” Hindu sects in contrast to the “esoteric” Shaivist-specific sects I lean on, fully embraces the notion that an understanding of objective reality is possible but dependent on the integrity of instruments through which information is obtained. This is often called Causal Reliabilism, since we believe we can ascertain knowledge of causes if our instruments are reliable. A child does not need to pay attention repeatedly to an agreement that fire is hot; they learn that the moment they stick their hand in it and feel the heat on their skin. If the sensory instrument of touch is reliable, then the knowledge fire is hot (or specifically that it causes pain through heat) can be obtained. If sensory instruments are not reliable, you cannot know whether or not you are in pain. I have a feeling though that you’re not going to go stick your hand in a fire now and claim that you don’t agree to the burning of your skin. The four Pramanas, or instruments of knowledge, are Pratyaksha as sensory perception, Anumana as logical inference, Upamana as analogies, and Shabda as expert testimony.

Some Buddhist epistemologists were radical pragmatists who argued that true knowledge, which they also referred to as Pramana, is defined by Casual Efficacy or Arthakriya. In fact, the Buddhist logician Dignaga made arguments quite close to the idea of language as a shared construct and sought knowledge without the use of language. His successor Dharmakirti and the Yogacara-Sautrantika school ran with the premise of a constantly impermanent world in which language, beliefs, truth, and justification cannot exist statically. Instead, they leaned entirely on the capacity to produce an effect as the determinant of reality. A small child incapable of articulating a belief, a standard for the truth, or a justification beyond their feelings can still learn quickly that putting their hand in the fire burns them. The effect of the burn is the reality of the fire. Whatever words or constructs they learn afterward to communicate about the fire doesn’t change the reality they experienced. Ironically enough, under this perspective, the truth content behind The Four Agreements is as real as the ability someone gains from using its knowledge to practically navigate the world, so it would be far more charitable than I’ve been so far. However, there would still be a discord since a patient replacing “I am helpless” with “I am empowered by The Four Agreements” is an improvement as far as medical providers and recovery programs are concerned, but epistemologically it’s just replacing an uncomfortable illusion with a comfortable one. There are still static concepts being used that don’t necessarily bring the patient or the reader closer to an understanding of impermanence and the ego’s nature within it.

I wouldn’t have as much of a problem with this if Ruiz’s framework was one of many offered in clinical settings to someone seeking to explore spirituality as part of evobiopsychosociospiritual health (or wherever we are now in this patchwork approach that keeps adding bandaids to bullet wounds). But this book is treated as a gold standard, and attempts to reconcile this book with other epistemological frameworks fall flat. In the best case scenario, medical professionals and recovery staff will make a good faith appeal to perennialism or encourage you to take what works while leaving the rest. In the worst case scenario, it reads as stubborn, resistant, or anti-social to someone who is just there to do a job. I once asked if there was anything within a center’s library more evidence-based to understand the mechanics of addiction, and this was the book recommended to me.

Domestication Nation

I call this process the domestication of humans. And through this domestication we learn how to live and how to dream. In human domestication, the information from the outside dream is conveyed to the inside dream, creating our whole belief system. First the child is taught the names of things: Mom, Dad, milk, bottle. Day by day, at home, at school, at church, and from television, we are told how to live, what kind of behavior is acceptable. The outside dream teaches us how to be a human. We have a whole concept of what a “woman” is and what a “man” is. And we also learn to judge: We judge ourselves, judge other people, judge the neighbors. Children are domesticated the same way that we domesticate a dog, a cat, or any other animal. In order to teach a dog we punish the dog and we give it rewards. We train our children whom we love so much the same way that we train any domesticated animal: with a system of punishment and reward. We are told, “You’re a good boy,” or “You’re a good girl,” when we do what Mom and Dad want us to do. When we don’t, we are “a bad girl” or “a bad boy.”

Here, Ruiz seems to transition from proposing his own epistemological framework and towards concepts from psychology and sociology. He references the idea of operant conditioning from BF Skinner where positive reinforcement can lead to long-term habits, although modern psychologists have also noticed an overjustification effect where an actor is solely focused on the reward of the outcome without internalizing any reward from doing the action itself. Without the reward, the behavior also collapses.

There’s also another interesting human tendency that throws a wrench in this from Anthony Giddens’ Theory of Structuration, which proposes an agency-structure duality where the structure can be likened to the way Ruiz describes the Dream of the Planet, but it leaves agency on the table. To Giddens, humans are inherently knowledgeable and structures exist as a result of humans making the individual choice to replicate or modify the society they’re in. If every human agreed tomorrow to abolish gender, or at least gender roles in any institutionally prescribed or categorized sense, we wouldn’t have it anymore. Dogs, unfortunately, can do no such thing. Dogs cannot unionize. So while Ruiz proposes The Four Agreements as a path to liberating yourself from previous agreements we were domesticated into, Giddens argues we have always had agency. We have always been bending, breaking, questioning, renegotiating, iterating, and reaffirming every aspect of society in every moment we’re in. We even observe a tendency in people who suspect their agency is being engineered to choose the opposite of the optimal suggestion from the system as a form of restoring their control. That’s often why teenagers will deliberately choose to make a situation worse when given an out; they’re suspicious about whether they really even rebelled if the out allowed them to act rebellious without consequences. It’s not rebellious to have a drink when you’re a teen if your dad gives you a beer. It’s rebellious to sneak one to your younger siblings behind his back. It’s the second that actually might allow the teen to feel independent, even if the first allows the teen to feel more mature.

The domestication is so strong that at a certain point in our lives we no longer need anyone to domesticate us. We don’t need Mom or Dad, the school or the church to domesticate us. We are so well trained that we are our own domesticator. We are an autodomesticated animal. We can now domesticate ourselves according to the same belief system we were given, and using the same system of punishment and reward. We punish ourselves when we don’t follow the rules according to our belief system; we reward ourselves when we are the “good boy” or “good girl.” The belief system is like a Book of Law that rules our mind. Without question, whatever is in that Book of Law, is our truth. We base all of our judgments according to the Book of Law, even if these judgments go against our own inner nature. Even moral laws like the Ten Commandments are programmed into our mind in the process of domestication. One by one, all these agreements go into the Book of Law, and these agreements rule our dream.

So it’s clear now that Ruiz really wants us to think in terms of “domestication.” As my own opinions have made clear, I certainly think we treat children like we do livestock on factory farms, but Ruiz uses this loosely enough that it blurs the line with “socialization.” That’s a far more neutral term than “domestication”, which has the implication of changing a fundamental nature of a thing. You can use both terms in the same breath for sure, but you then have to delineate domestication from socialization in the acquisition and sharing of knowledge.

Let’s refer to the parable I shared before about passing esoteric knowledge. Indra was not “domesticated” by his father Prajapati. The kings of the Asuras and Devas alike sought knowledge because they sought the fulfillment from knowing the Self in recognition of their own finite experiences. They sought new knowledge because of an inherent desire for it, then sought stubbornly to pursue it after receiving answers he found unsatisfying all on his own, then had to spend a lot of time quietly internalizing it with no external reward in the process. You could say his father “socialized” him and that would be more true to the spirit of their interaction, but that’s not quite right either. “Education” or “Awakened” or “Guided” might be better words. And if the parable motivates you to learn, then you’ve begun a search for knowledge yourself without needing a reward. You simply recognize that as an intrinsic desire. You don’t even have to sleep in a cave and change your name to Smokey Mirror.

Even the Asuras who walked away with incomplete knowledge leading to a material emphasis continued to explore knowledge in that direction. Rawan was an Asura whose devotional austerity impressed Shiva, who would grant boons to anyone regardless of their status as Devas or Asuras, even if it might be abused later to control the cosmic order administered by the Devas. It’s actually kind of amusing to imagine the Devas frustrated with Shiva for giving power and knowledge to anyone who might abuse it while he retorts that they haven’t abused it yet. He’s not a believer in prior restraint. At best, the Asuras would eventually be “defeated” or “pacified” perhaps, but not “domesticated.” It is their nature to eventually challenge the order again in a new way and force evolution. Some Asuras even challenge other Asuras while praying to Devas.

The belief system is like a Book of Law that rules our mind. Without question, whatever is in that Book of Law, is our truth. We base all of our judgments according to the Book of Law, even if these judgments go against our own inner nature. Even moral laws like the Ten Commandments are programmed into our mind in the process of domestication. One by one, all these agreements go into the Book of Law, and these agreements rule our dream.

Ruiz is kind of reinventing Freud’s superego with the Book of Law, down to the way parental authority becomes an internal guiding authority. The guilt and shame given by parents for violating the norms of society eventually become internalized as a part governing our own sense of social morals and ethics. Set aside what you think of the merits of Freud’s work for a moment; we can hopefully appreciate that Freud spent a lot of time writing and researching his case for the id-ego-superego trinity, as well as cultural variations, neuroses, and edge cases. We have ample record of his clinical work. We don’t have anything like that from Ruiz except a suggestive appeal to personal intuition and experience.

There is something in our minds that judges everybody and everything, including the weather, the dog, the cat — everything. The inner Judge uses what is in our Book of Law to judge everything we do and don’t do, everything we think and don’t think, and everything we feel and don’t feel. Everything lives under the tyranny of this Judge. Every time we do something that goes against the Book of Law, the Judge says we are guilty, we need to be punished, we should be ashamed. This happens many times a day, day after day, for all the years of our lives. There is another part of us that receives the judgments, and this part is called the Victim. The Victim carries the blame, the guilt, and the shame

There are modern therapeutic approaches like Internal Family Systems (IFS) which absolutely involve conceptualizing the mind as a system of parts with a core Self, as if there are multiple subpersonalities that might conflict or cooperate in complex ways that can be difficult to describe without the family system model. The typical model categorizes these parts into Exiles (suppressed wounded parts that hold overwhelming emotions like shame or guilt), Managers (protective parts that try to maintain functional control to keep exiles from surfacing), and Firefighters (reactive parts that try to counteract surfacing exiles even through maladaptive mechanisms like substance abuse). These parts are all assumed to have positive intent but lack the ability to work together productively on strategies. Studies so far seem to indicate that it helps with self-compassion and emotional regulation.

What Ruiz proposes here with the Judge and the Victim as parts of ourselves is not lacking in merit at a surface level, but attempting to map his model to IFS can reveal some interesting objections. The Book of Laws might function as a Manager working to develop strategies to comply with rules, the Judge might function as a Manager using self-criticism to ensure that one won’t be rejected by the people around them, and the Victim might be an Exile holding onto powerful feelings associated with rejection or abandonment that could lead to fears of falling into complete dysfunction if surfaced. But rather than see these parts as inherently limiting or destructive, we might try to see them with positive intent and dynamically responding to the environment. If you suddenly learn about a different culture with different norms, the system of your Book of Laws, the Judge, and the Victim might all be working to make sure you didn’t do something offensive. The Book of Laws might be rapidly indexing the environment in a way that feels like overthinking or hypervigilance, The Judge is trying to make sure you conform to the standards or norms you’ve picked up on, and The Exile might replicate the safety mechanisms associated with avoiding punishment in the past like withdrawing or reflexively apologizing.

The reason a distinction like this matters is because Ruiz then goes on to say:

Just as the government has a book of laws that rule the society’s dream, our belief system is the Book of Laws that rules our personal dream. All these laws exist in our mind, we believe them, and the Judge inside us bases everything on these rules. The Judge decrees, and the Victim suffers the guilt and punishment. But who says there is justice in this dream? True justice is paying only once for each mistake. True injustice is paying more than once for each mistake.

Every time we remember the mistake, we blame them again and send them all the emotional poison we feel at the injustice, and then we make them pay again for the same mistake. Is that justice? The Judge in the mind is wrong because the belief system, the Book of Law, is wrong. The whole dream is based on false law.

The Judge, the Book of Laws, and the Victim are framed inherently as problems to be addressed. There is barely even discussion of how an adaptive strategy became maladaptive, much less acknowledging the possibility of positive intent.
