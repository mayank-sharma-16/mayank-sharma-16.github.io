Yes, I’m Going There

Look, it’s 2026. It’s been almost nine decades since it came out. We should have a conversation about the Alcoholics Anonymous book, or The Big Book as it refers to itself.

This is not a judgment on AA meetings or the community. As with all things relating to life advice, if it works for you, you don’t have to engage with any of my crap. Take what works, leave what doesn’t. I don’t know you, your life, your meetings, your sponsor, your medications, your fellowship, your social network, your medical history, your healthcare access, or anything else relevant to whether AA is helpful or not for your particular life. I could never recommend nor recommend against AA for anyone.

The only thing I have to say is that it is generally religious, not just spiritual, and that’s okay. Just be aware that you are not an “unfortunate” who is “constitutionally incapable” of improving your life if you feel a clash between your beliefs and the program’s belief system. There are other recovery programs and communities that take the moralizing and emphasis on finding His will out for those who have different etiological perspectives about the nature of illness and suffering.

This is solely about a book that is a staple of treatment centers addressing substance abuse disorders and the most common piece of addiction literature recommended around the world. I am not interested in taking away a method that has worked for your life. I’m interested in what this book does for the culture of addiction prevention, perception, and treatment. Most importantly, I’m interested in who it fails for and why, because I refuse to believe it’s just a problem of moral character, personal willpower, divine predestination, or knowledge gaps that lead to irrational decisions. That’s all far too easy to blame.
