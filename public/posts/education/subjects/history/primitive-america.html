The Primitive West

The Primitive West

Jul 25, 202619 min read

A Radical America

Many American students of history are introduced to the subjectivity of historical narratives through the question “Was the American Revolution radical?”

From the perspective of the British monarchy and aristocracy, certainly. The colonies had started in part because wealthy Protestant families hosted in a commune at the Earl of Lincolnshire’s estate took the physical legal charter of their company to America so the king could not prosecute and confiscate assets at will. A few centuries later, these families and those who came after (second-born sons out of the line of succession, indentured servants that included children, enslaved Africans, non-Anglo Europeans escaping prosecution or poverty, etc.) announced their intent to create a constitutional democratic republic of their own rather than follow Britain’s monarchy - and in the process producing anarchic interims where local committees and social networks managed daily life with no central authority. Industrious scholars who studied at elite institutions and managed large portions of the economy came together to build a new form of government using the knowledge of empires that came before. They separated power into three branches that were to check each other and ensure balance. Regular elections and trials would be held to keep the government confined to laws instead of whims of tyrants. Each state then continued experimenting on its own as a laboratory of consitutional democratic republics. Although mostly Protestant, there were very distinct denominations within that from Ann Hutchinson empowering women to lead religious groups to the pacifist Quakers of Pennsylvania to Harvard College’s Puritan donor network. Through a coalition of different states with these different groups, the American army was able to successfully employ guerilla tactics against a global superpower. An estimated 100,000 Tories were expelled from the United States or killed with their property confiscated.

From the perspective of indigenous nations centuries ago or enslaved Africans, it might not have seemed like much of a difference at all. The same project of expanding an imperialist Eurocentric Christian empire through genocide and slavery continued, just under a more fragmented leadership than before. The Massachusetts Bay Company had been modeled after the Dutch East India Company, which was both one of the first examples of the modern shareholder corporation and one of the first pioneers of accounting fraud. The transition to shareholder capitalism persisted in the post-colonial economy. Treaties were still ignored and courts were blatantly biased, no matter how much the virtues of the rule of law and due process were extolled within the lands the colonists captured. The same applied to enslaved Africans and their descendants who were excluded entirely from the bit about “all men are created equal.” In fact, very confusingly, owning a slave gave an additional 3/5ths of a vote to the owner. There would eventually be a whole Civil War over the fact that half the country could not conceive of a society without a racial underclass as their slaves. The half that opposed slavery did so in part because it was difficult to compete with freely exploited labor, so they still favored segregation and discrimination to some degree. In fact, Britain abolished slavery before America (although they often compensated through offshore imperialism and exploitation so that they didn’t have to see the atrocities).

The answer remains dismal if we look at other historically marginalized groups in America: women were treated as men’s property, anything other than cisgender heterosexual orientations were unacceptable, the smattering of Asians who went to America already posed problems for a black-white racial hierarchy, indentured servants who were taken as child laborers but survived to freedom didn’t necessarily come into land which was necessary for suffrage, and many states excluded other Christian religious sects they disagreed with from voting. Many of the members of these who fought in the Revolution hoping to prove they deserved rights too were ignored or outright treated as expendable. Arguments made from Christian ethics about compassion and God creating all men as equals were met with similar justifications used by missionaries that argued non-white cultures needed a white Christian patriarchy to civilize them.

Neither version can be said to be wrong per se, but they certainly rely on different methods of picking and presenting evidence that lead to different conclusions. If it was radical, then we might say that America was built on radical immigrants who were willing to work hard for freedom instead of accepting a monopoly’s control over their life. If it wasn’t radical, then we might say that America was just a different branch of Eurocentric imperialism that developed on a separate continent- a fork of the same project spearheaded by the same classes of elite families. It can also be both and more to varying degrees, although the more nuance you add, the harder it is to create propaganda for the present based on claims about the past.

A Primitive West

In the spirit of the question above, what if we asked whether the West is more primitive than the East? I don’t actually believe it makes sense to call any culture or nation or state “primitive” for the reasons any modern Anthropology 101 course will give you, but we can see what happens when we start with a conclusion and work to find evidence supporting the associated narratives. From transcendentalists to New Age perennialists, there are lots of problems with regarding the East through an Orientalist lens about ancient exotic immaterial wisdom in contrast to a industrialized material rational West. To someone without background knowledge to push back, we might make a very compelling case that the West never learned sustainability or accountability, so under a polished veneer of sophistication lies a fairly barbaric approach to the world that understands very little of what it seeks to control.

Prior to the settlement of America, indigenous nations had a diverse and sophisticated network of trade, war, coalitions, kinships, and diplomacy that was wiped away for a standardized Puritan model of society that relied on hierarchical institutions expanding their control over others in the pursuit of monopolies.

There was the Haudenosaunee Confederacy (or Iroquois Confederacy) consisting of a willing coalition of united nations: Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora. They had a dual sovereignty system where each nation handled its own internal affairs while the confederacy handled affairs between member nations. The federal council consisted of 50 hereditary seats belonging to matrilineal clans, where the clan mothers nominated, judged, and removed chiefs from power if they weren’t serving the clan’s interest or became power-hungry or arrogant. Rather than majority voting, the council’s decisions required consensus so that no clan ends up marginalized or dismissed. Civil chiefs and war leaders were distinct.

The Muscogee Confederacy was composed of talwas, or small town-like settlements, as the main political unit. Each talwa had its own square ground for ceremonies, council houses, leadership structures, ritual calendars, and legal authorities. There was no central state or bureaucracy. The Confederacy was like an emergent property of the sophisticated diplomatic networks between talwas when they needed to coordinate action. Similar to the Iroquois Confederacy, there was a distinction made between war leaders and civil leaders that would be embedded in their cosmological beliefs.

The Cherokee Nation consisted of autonomous towns bound together by kinship and diplomatic relations. Clans structured obligations and justice such that responsibility for an individual fell collectively on the clan they belonged to.

The European model of a single patriarchal king commanding a nation, often surrounded by wealthy aristocrats who have agendas of their own, seems quite chaotic and simple. One person’s whims determines whether a war happens, what the state religion is, how the economy should grow, which books are published and burned, how laws are applied to individuals and markets? What if he stops acting in the interests of his subjects? What if he has a mental breakdown or throws himself into hedonism? What if he leaves no succession plan, or an idiot prince, and the kingdom becomes weak to invasion or civil strife? The American government seems to have a President rather than a King whose executive authority is to be checked by Congress and the Supreme Court, but the President also seems to have unlimited pardon power to exempt anyone from federal laws, no Supreme Court Justice can assume their office without the President’s approval, and nothing stopped factions from forming across branches that immediately ruined the intended structure. You can find many contemporary American legal scholars and historians who argue that this imperial executive was the intended outcome all along rather than a civil administrative state of qualified appointments.

And why the radical insistence on erasing the role of women? We can certainly make an argument that we shouldn’t build institutions on the basis of sex or gender, but many indigenous structures were matrilineal or had institutions of power dedicated to women. One has to work very hard to imagine a way to convince a matrilineal society to adopt Eurocentric-style patriarchy that forbids women from even voting as part of the general population. In the year 2026, America has still not had a female President or female Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. We have had a female Speaker of the House, but not a female Senate Majority leader. In fact America still struggles with accepting there could be diversity within gender, sex, and sexual orientation, much less the idea that societies might not even function with these concepts in mind as they’re commonly defined. Many American indigenous communities did not enforce a rigid male-female binary (which is where the catch-all term “Two-Spirit” comes from). India has legalized a third gender while acknowledging intersex and gender variation repeatedly through certain belief systems and scriptures.

And why the intolerance for religious diversity? When the St. Thomas Christians settled in India, Hindu rulers were able to syncretically incorporate them into daily life without a long and violent history of coercive conversions in either direction. European Christian missionaries and explorers unquestionably were brutal and violent at times, but the St. Thomas Christian community suggests that there was never a fundamental incompatibility in beliefs that would’ve required violent conflict with indigenous belief systems anywhere in the world. The fact that European settlers and missionaries conducted violent campaigns to eradicate the indigenous institutions and cultures of America comes directly out of their failure to achieve rational dialogue. That which they could not understand or subjugate, they sought to destroy.

As much as America prides itself on separation of powers, checks and balances, democracy and republicanism, and constitutional written law, versions of these can be found throughout the hundreds or thousands of indigenous societies present before colonization. Many of these versions are far more complex and diverse than the system proposed by the Framers of the Constitution, and they may have existed continuously for as long as the United States has been around if not longer.

The Other Indians

Let’s flip over to the other side of the world and see how European colonial powers were handling contact with India. While Columbus thought he discovered India, Portgual had sent Vasco da Gama to the actual India where he landed in Calicut. Once again, they stumbled into a society where a patriarchal male ruler didn’t pass on power to his son and struggled to understand a society where women held institutional power. In fact it was a common tradition that sons were raised by the men of the mother’s family: “a mother is a fact, a father is an opinion” the saying went. This actually led to a more open sexual culture than Puritan societies, since there was no expectation for the male to produce his own heirs, and interreligious and internationality partnerships were common. It led to more opportunities to merge kinship networks and trade as well.

This meant that when Vasco da Gama was demanding monopolistic control in the market and an exclusion of Muslim merchants from the ports, he was effectively asking the leaders of Calicut to turn on kinship networks and completely failed to win favor with the women in the Zamorin’s family. In fact da Gama had been under the impression that there was a lost Christian kingdom in India started after Thomas the Apostle was sent, and he hoped to ally with these Christians against the Muslim Moors. When he and his crew first landed, they mistook a female deity’s temple as a statue of the Virgin Mary and partook in the rituals from the Hindu priests. Given that the Portuguese brought goods that the leaders of Calicut found (literally) laughably meager, such that it was arguably better to not have presented them lest it be perceived as a slight, they didn’t seem to come from a position to justify that kind of demand.

This is one of the reasons you actually should not use Wikipedia to build an understanding of historical events. It is useful for reference in a casual conversation but it will very easily paint a distorted narrative between incomplete and skewed evidence. In this case, there is a single source from 1894: Frederic Charles Danvers. Danvers was an India Official writing in the 1890s based primarily on records of the Portuguese state archives. Naturally, this set of evidence would portray Vasco da Gama’s violent escalations as a response to either provocation from Muslim merchants they considered mortal enemies or an irrational Zamorin who was sympathetic to those Muslims and insufficiently inclined to worship Jesus.

A much more detailed account of Portugal’s demand to gain monopolistic access in an otherwise remarkably diverse international marketplace is given in The Theft of India, as well as incredible amounts of violence and mutilation when they were denied special economic access. It’s an infuriating read at times, not least because of how Indian rulers demonstrated inhuman levels of patience and goodwill even after Portugal regularly took hostages and murdered whole ships of people.

We should also pause here to appreciate the scope of diversity in Calicut:

  • From Hindu communities, there were the Nairs as a military aristocracy, Nambudiri Brahmins as landholders and temple administrators, Ezhavas (locally known as Tiyyas), and a variety of local artisan castes that provided the shipbuilding that made Calicut’s economy possible.

  • From Muslim communities, there were Mappilas who descended from Arab and Persian merchants that integrated with local Malayali women over centuries. Pardesi Muslims came out of extensive merchant networks that spanned Mecca, Yemen, Hormuz, Persia, and Egypt to handle the spice trade along the Red Sea and Persian Gulf. Hadramis came from southern Yemen, Hijazis from Medina and Jeddah, Omanis from Oman to Gujarat, and Yemenis from Aden.

  • From Gujarati communities, aside from the Gujarati Muslims operating ships in the Muslim merchant networks, there were Banias and Jains specializing in accounting and textiles.

  • Other Hindu groups include Tamilian, Konkani, Kannada, Telugu, and Sri Lankan communities.

  • Southeast Asian groups included merchants from the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Malacca, Pasai, Aceh, etc.

  • A dedicated Chinese quarter since the era of Zheng He and Ming treasure voyages.

  • East African merchants from Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, and Sofala. Also, Ethiopians.

  • Jewish merchants, although the nearby Cochin had a larger permanent settlement to speak of than in Calicut.

  • Syrian Christians, Armenians, Parsis, etc.

Now let’s be clear: none of this implies a utopia of tolerance. In fact, managing this kind of diversity can require very rigid frameworks sometimes to ensure that a small altercation doesn’t escalate into an explosion of old grudges and new offenses. In a bustling marketplace, it’s all too easy for a bump between someone from an “untouchable” caste and a Brahmin priest to spiral into a conflict that leaves dozens dead. That did happen many times, but it does perhaps have less of a shocking impact in comparison to the Portguese sailors setting ships with children and their families on fire to send a message.

Even though they could not really grasp how Calicut worked and the international diversity of it, and they freely used violence to make a point, Portuguese explorers were able to understand that there was something comparable to their concept of “civilization” underneath it all. There were historic palaces, international trading markets, written language systems, religious hierarchies, etc. Plus, India is mentioned in the Bible as the place St. Thomas the Apostle went. Hindu deities were mistaken for Christian figures. When it came to indigenous nations, they could not recognize a “civilization” at all and appointed themselves to be the new Christian stewards of these lost souls who follow magic and witchcraft against the will of God as expressed in the Bible. It’s quite embarrassing for a superpower to only be able to see a civilization if there’s a church involved. It certainly seems like a primitive heuristic on which to recognize peoples, cultures, and belief systems.

The American Experiment

St. Augustine, Florida was founded in 1565 by Spanish explorers to secure their claim against French Huguenots, and it remains the oldest continuously inhabited European settlement in the borders of the modern continental United States. By this benchmark, we might say America is 461 years old.

Aboriginal nations have inhabited Australia for at least 50,000 years. The modern nation-state of Egypt is not comparable to ancient Egyptian structures that involve pharoahs and heiroglyphics, but there are still claims to demographic and cultural continuity dating back to 3100 BCE. China has institutions, written language, and bureaucratic traditions that show continuity past 2000 BCE. Iran as descended from Persian empires goes back to at least 1000 BCE. Hinduism goes back to 1500 BCE with older influences from Vedic and Proto-Indo-European tribes. Judaism goes back 3000 years. Zoroastrianism goes back between 600-1500 BCE. Japan seems to have the world’s oldest hereditary monarchy which historians seem to agree has existed continuously for 1500 years, though traditional dates go back to 660 BCE. English common law comes out of a continuous tradition from the 12th century.

461 years isn’t very long. The spread of European diseases was a massive advantage in depopulating the continent and was often framed as God’s will to justify Manifest Destiny. If we limit ourselves to the Declaration of Independence, America as the United States just celebrated its 250th birthday. The only Civil War this country has had was over the issue of abolishing slavery, 160 years ago (1865 for chattel slavery and 1866 for descendants of indigenous nations). The resolution to that was not the abolition of slavery but a new regulatory regime that requires a prosecution first. Even though voting rights existed on paper, it wasn’t until 1920 that women received the right to vote and 1965 that an enforceable Voting Rights Act allowed non-white citizens to vote in numbers beyond single digit percentages. America has never been invaded after 1812 and shares no borders with rivals, which puts it into a very unique bubble. It has never had, say, an invading Tibetan army raze Christian churches to construct Buddhist temples, and then been forced to argue for the legitimacy of Protestantism using Central Asian dialects. Our last two Presidents were both alive to see 48 stars on the flag and the start of the Cold War. The takeover of the people of Hawaii was completed in 1959, only 12 years after the British left India and 2 years before Portugal left. The US still holds several territories where citizens are denied the right to vote or participate meaningfully in other democratic processes, as well as being subject to different regulatory regimes; one of these seems to be its own capital, and the others involve offshore islands.

After WWII, with the old superpowers weakened and America insulated from the frontlines save for Pearl Harbor, they were in the best position to start building new capital networks in a wartorn world. The Marshall Plan allowed them to set the terms for a Europe indebted to America. They picked out scientists from Nazi Germany in Operation Paperclip, defectors from Soviet Russia seeking to escape a poor authoritarian state, data sharing agreements with Unit 731 from Japan, etc. They also brain drained the best of the best from the education systems in developing countries that they destabilized either willingly or unwillingly depending on their openness to giving American corporations property rights guarded by state power.

So if we were to evaluate America harshly, we might say it’s actually been a short-lived experiment in violent radical corporatism which tends towards monopolization of power and, once populist elements are introduced, Othering of competitors into destruction or subjugation. It has no philosophical or historical traditions to look back on if the current Constitution fails, except maybe the Anti-Federalists. Obviously we’re not going to tap into the actual indigenous traditions of nations who have been here; we genocided and enslaved them precisely to avoid that. There’s no tradition of oral storytelling as a form of knowledge preservation and community bonding that cuts across class stratifications. The New Age spiritual movements were a massive flop and American transcendentalism is rooted so heavily in exoticism and Orientalism. If say, some technology emerged where all written knowledge was suddenly made suspect because of a viral tool that generated a lot of unverified information in massive quantities, there may not be enough infrastructure or capital capable of salvaging and restoring our knowledge base. That would require trusted local experts who have lived in an area long enough to understand the changes it’s been through and what resources you can trust, whereas the average American seems to move every 5 years with half of those moves being out-of-county - and rising. Having a lot of technological advancements and industrial capacity is not the same as having the ability to pass it down outside of a corporation’s intellectual property rights. Hoarding information like this seems incredibly regressive when progress is made through collaboration and communication. There’s no stewardship model beyond the wealthiest families who have increased their number of properties in an area instead of moving to accommodate a growing family or a lost job like other working-class families. When the American Revolution led to periods of no clear governing authority, there were local organizations that managed day-to-day life because people can be fairly resourceful in groups that are reasonably autonomous. We may not have the means to do that anymore given how much we’ve become dependent on a single point of failure.
