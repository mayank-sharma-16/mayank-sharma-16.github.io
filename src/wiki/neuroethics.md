# Overview
Neuroethics is the field that studies the ethical implications of neuroscientific discoveries. 

There's really two things that we mean by neuroethics. There's the ethics of neuroscience, which contains a lot of extensions of bioethical dilemmas around human experimentation and treatment protocols, and then there's the neuroscience of ethics, which explores the neurological mechanisms through which human agency and identity are formed.

# Topics

## Free Will
Consider the trolley problem, where a trolley is headed down a track that has 5 people tied to it, but you can push 1 person in front of the trolley to stop it. The consequentialist and utilitarian answer that sacrifices one person to save five activates the dorsolateral prefrontal cortext (dlPFC) associated with calculations and working memory. The deontological answer that has a rule or principle against actively causing harm activates the ventromedial prefrontal cortext (vmPFC) and the amygdala, which are associated with emotional processing and value assignment. These types of observations raise questions about free will and the extent to which our sense of ethics is tied to the structures of our brain. 

There are three major schools of thought around the role of free will and moral responsibility:

- Hard Incompatibilism: If our actions are determined by our brains and no one has agency over the design of their brains, then holding individuals morally accountable for the outcomes of that brain is actually punishing them for something out of their control. This means justice should be oriented around the idea that individuals may have dysfunctional neural architectures that should be addressed through rehabilitation that involves scientific and medical guidance.

- Compatibilism: It's possible for determinism to coexist with moral responsibility so long as a person has the capacity for rational thinking, since the purpose of moral rules is to incentivize certain behaviors between people. If there's a preference in someone's brain to break a rule and they fail to exercise their rational thinking to stop themselves, holding them responsible with consequences is part of disincentivizing the behavior by reinforcing consequences to a rational brain. In people who suffer from disorders that involve delusions or psychosis, we can see instances where determinism in terms of brain structure is plainly interfering with the individual's capacity for rational thinking. 

- Illusionism/Revisionism: Objective moral responsibility may not exist just as the hard incompatibilists might say, but the framework of moral responsibility is an important societal construct. Illusionism follows the idea that people tend to behave less cooperatively and more aggressively when primed with ideas about free will being an illusion, so the illusion is important to maintain. Revisionism begins with the notion of revising the idea of moral responsibility such that we see the function of praising and blaming people as feedback loops for maintaining better standards of behavior.

### Substance Use Disorder
Substance use disorders have historically presented controversial challenges around free will and responsibility. 

The medical consensus surrounds the Brain Disease Model of Addiction (BDMA), which argues that chronic substance use alters the [[mesolimbic-system|mesolimbic dopamine pathway]] and hijacks the reward center such that the brain becomes less responsive to natural rewards while craving the high of a substance instead. The framing initially received support in part because of its potential to reduce stigma by removing moral weakness from the description of addiction. Unfortunately, this only changed the nature of the stigma; the disease model might have decreased blame towards addicts, but it increased social distance since it inadvertently promoted the idea that addicts were fundamentally and structurally broken as people. Many addicts themselves have internalized that idea of being biologically destined to fail in sobriety as a form of [[learned-helplessness|learned helplessness]].
