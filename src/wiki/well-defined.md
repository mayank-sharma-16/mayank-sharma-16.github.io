# Overview
In science and math, a "well-defined" concept is something that has a clear meaning so that it can be interpreted the same way by everyone.

# Historical Context
In the past, terms like [[habitual-drunkenness|"habitual drunkenness"]] existed, but there was no scientific or medical consensus about when that label should be applied. Legal cases based on someone's intoxication, whether divorce or criminal cases, often applied local heuristics about what constituted occasional drunkenness vs habitual drunkenness. In the DSM-I and DSM-II, alcohol problems were classified under "Sociopathic Personality Disturbance;" the psychoanalytic approach essentially forced addiction to be seen as a structural personality problem. 

As the field of psychiatry underwent a [[kraepelinian|Kraepelinian]] paradigm shift, disorders were diagnosed through patterns of specific criteria believed to come from distinct physiological causes. This allowed disorders to have well-defined labels and criteria. In the DSM-3, published in 1980, this led to the creation of "Substance Abuse" and "Substance Dependence" as distinct labels. The DSM-5 now has a single Substance Use Disorder label that checks for 11 criteria and categorizes someone's substance use pattern into Mild, Moderate, or Severe SUD.
