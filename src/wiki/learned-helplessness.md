# Overview
Learned helplessness is the concept that living entities become more passive if they believe their actions cannot influence outcomes, especially after being subjected to chronic stress or failure. One of the researchers who coined the term, Martin Seligman, popularized the concept as a primary founder of the Positive Psychology movement. As with all pop psych concepts, this has often led to it being distorted for feel-good messages. 

Consider how many people readily admit to being "bad at math" and recall feeling that way from a young age after some poor early experiences with math classes. One of the biggest obstacles for instructors and educators to overcome in math education is this type of [[growth-vs-fixed-mindset|fixed mindset]] in which students are convinced they can't deal with mathematical abstractions well while feeling at home in creative pursuits or the humanities. This strongly suggests that learned helplessness isn't necessarily a general psychological disposition that impacts someone's overall performance across subjects or tasks, but a response to feeling that the effort previously put into a pursuit rarely materializes in success. Because math is also taught in order of increasingly complex concepts that build on each other, falling behind necessitates accelerated learning later to catch up, which translates to asking for even more effort from an activity that the brain doesn't associate with success or rewards. Eventually, students who struggle with math might learn to avoid it and try to compensate by specializing in other disciplines. Here, there's a real utility to identifying the concept of learned helplessness and even developing metrics for tracking it. Studies show that low self-efficacy about math correlates with more hesitation on routine steps and avoidance of challenging problems.

Since the concept was first proposed in the 1960s, neuroscientists and researchers have found that passivity is generally the default psychological state rather than proactive problem-solving. Organisms are observed as having a freeze response to prolonged stress until realizing there are mechanisms to stop the stressor, after which the brain's medial prefrontal cortex shuts down the freeze response. 

Psychologically, it's still useful to identify learned helplessness when working with depression, PTSD, and other issues associated with trauma, but the latest research shows that our emphasis on changing internal beliefs about helplessness may not be effective without realistic external solutions for someone to take. I'd argue this lines up with studies that have shown that, within [[cognitive-behavioral-therapy|Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy]] and its associated modalities, the component of [[behavioral-activation|Behavioral Activation]] alone appears to have the same effectiveness as an approach that uses both BA and cognitive components. In other words, we are better at changing once we identify that change is possible through repeated experiences than by challenging internal beliefs for rationality or utility, especially if we're prone to rationalizing our decisions after we intuitively discern our incentives and desires. 

Within [[liberation-psychology|Liberation Psychology]], learned helplessness is not interpreted in terms of lacking coping skills or suffering from negative core beliefs; instead, the core issue is located in the environment and potentially oppressive structures that can be modified through collective action. 

Where learned helplessness becomes used problematically is when it's deployed as a criticism instead of an acknowledgement of someone's psychological state after their experiences. Take the case of someone who has survived years of abuse where attempts to leave their enviroment failed, either as a result of being punished for their attempts or simply repeated disappointments. What we might call "learned helplessness" in that situation was a survival adaptation to minimize effort where it seems wasteful or worsens the situation; it often ends up being an obstacle when someone has had a change in their environment that allows their efforts to be rewarded but retains the pattern of avoidance or freezing. This is substantially different than being unwilling to put in any effort to begin with or feeling entitled to something without effort, but, especially in popular psychology material, these can be conflated.
