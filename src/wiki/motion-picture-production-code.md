# Overview
The Motion Picture Production Code, also called the Hays Code, was a set of guidelines used by Hollywood studios between 1934 to 1968 to ward off censorship from every level of government and boycotts from civic organizations. It wasn't until *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson* (1952) that movies were actually considered a medium of communication that was protected by the First Amendment's free speech protections and put them beyond local censorship boards. The Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America (MPPDA) created the Production Code Administration (PCA) to enforce the Hays Code against its member studios. Studios would be fined $25,000 (about $615,000 in 2026) and member theaters pledged to never screen motion pictures without a PCA Certificate of Approval. 

For substance use, this meant any portrayals were forbidden if they:

- Encouraged or justified use

- Glamorized effects

- Oversimplified recovery

- Showed mechanics

- Emphasized profits

- Involved children

Government officials and politicians would often meet with studios to shape the way substance use was depicted. The name "Hays" comes from Will H. Hays, a former Postmaster General who was hired as a lobbyist for the MPPDA. Agencies like the Federal Bureau of Narcotics (FBN) led by Commissioner Harry J. Anslinger met with studio executives to push for storylines that depicted substance use as dangerous and criminal.

In 1968, the Hays Code was replaced with the modern rating system based on ages: G, M, R, and X.
