# Overview
Self-esteem is an individual's evaluation of their own value.

Despite the societal bias towards having an independent sense of self-esteem that remains immune to the judgment of others, researchers have proposed multiple evolutionary paradigms based on the functions of self-esteem:

- Sociometer Theory: self-esteem functions as an internal metric for how much someone is included or excluded socially, so fluctuations in self-esteem that drive one to seek validation from others promote social bonding and maintaining relationships. 

- Terror Management Theory (TMT): self-esteem is a psychological shield from the terror of contemplating our mortality because it compels us to integrate into cultural and institutional structures to feel lasting significance in.

- Identity Theory Integration: self-esteem is the result of processing external feedback for internalized standards about one's identity

# Historical Context
Leading up to the 1980s, self-esteem was framed as a single score describing how much someone generally liked themselves, In 1982, Viktor Gecas proposed two dimensions to self-esteem: efficacy-based, describing someone's perception of their own capacity and effectiveness, and worth-based, describing someone's perception that others value and respect them. In 2014, Peter Burke proposed a three-part mapping for self-esteem from identity that includes authenticity: social/group identity that generates worth-based esteem, role identity that generates efficacy-based esteem, and person identity which generates authenticity-based esteem. The authenticity dimension helps explain why someone can feel highly capable in their skills, highly valued by others, but still dislike themselves for not being who they feel they should be.
