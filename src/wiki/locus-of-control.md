# Overview
The locus of control describes where someone locates the causes of the events of their life. An internal locus of control means that someone locates the results of their lives to their own agency, whereas an external locus of control means that someone believes outside factors ultimately determine their fate. 

It can be easy to assume a strong internal locus of control is preferable because it implies someone is constantly looking to see what their role in a situation is and seeking opportunities to act, but it can also lead to self-blame and guilt where it may not be constructive. Someone can control how well they do in a job interview, but they can't change a bias from the interviewer about their background. A strong internal locus of control might lead one to continually improve their performance since they believe the effort can produce a change in their outcomes, but this can also hurt if it leads to negative attitudes about oneself for not being good enough to meet standards that may not even be possible. On the other hand, someone with a strong external locus of control may have an easier time dealing with the disappointment of an interview that wasn't good enough, but they may not be as driven to find room for improvement in their individual performance. In some cases, a external locus of control can extend into the idea of [[learned-helplessness|learned helplessness]].

These are all generalizations, of course. In practice, one's locus of control can vary by task or area of life. You might believe that your body weight is in your control but your height is not. A common critique of the original framework of the locus of control is that people separate whether a cause is internal or external from whether or not it is controllable. For instance, you may believe that your math ability or physical appearance is internal and that no outside entity is in control of that, but you may also believe that those aren't controllable. If you're religious and believe God personally intervenes in human affairs, then you might locate God as an external locus of control but still believe the internal action of prayer can help facilitate change. 

Frameworks of [[liberation-psychology|liberation psychology]] often start by describing an external locus of control to explore how systemic oppression interacts with individual psychology. An internal locus of control can be reclaimed through a community's collective action that pools individual power together, and the path to helping an individual do so is one of the goals of liberation psychology.
