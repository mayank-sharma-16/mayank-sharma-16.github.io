# Overview
Liberation psychology describes an approach to psychology that involves a critical perspective of the models and institutions of the field, namely through the way they're rooted in the biases of middle-class American norms that emphasize individualism within capitalist and imperialist structures. 

One of the more common patterns in liberation psychology is framing suffering and symptoms as normal adaptations to their conditions rather than maladaptive symptoms tied to personal traumas, which runs in strong contradiction to mainstream tenets of Western modalities. It's common to hear in both recovery circles and mental health treatment that one only has control over one's own actions and reactions, which is technically true, but can also have the unintended effect of absolving one of considering their role in their broader environment. For instance, it's true that no individual can single-handedly determine the outcome of a democratic election, but it's also true that each individual plays a role in the overall outcome, whether they abstain from voting or campaign for new converts. No single person can make or break an ecosystem, but we might still frown upon an individual who casually litters in public places. There are a variety of valid arguments to be made about the extent of this obligation one might have towards others, ranging from a basic principle to minimize harm onto others to an active mandate to protest sociopolitical injustice. Within liberation psychology frameworks, something like [[learned-helplessness|learned helplessness]] might be addressed through facilitating engagement with community structures or collective group dynamics rather than focusing solely on one's own coping skills or capacity for emotional regulation.
