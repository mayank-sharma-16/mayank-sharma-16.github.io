# Overview
Rational choice theory views individuals as calculating decision-makers, weighing costs and benefits to maximize their self-interest or satisfaction. 

# Historical Context
The premise that humans are rational actors driven by self-interest was a staple of conceptual frameworks for Enlightenment thinkers like Adam Smith and David Hume. Early on in the Cold War, there was a push to invest in new methods in social science that were more [[quantitative|quantitative]] and enabled manipulable models to guide policy, and so rational choice theory and methodological individualism were used as premises. Economics, criminology, political science, sociology, and other fields began adopting frameworks based on the theory, and generally it was received controversially. Critics would argue that humans are limited in their time and cognitive faculties to find the choice that maximizes self-interest and that humans often make decisions based on impulses, emotions, and cognitive shortcuts instead of a strict logical analysis. In the early 2000s, as a result of progress in cognitive science and [[neuroeconomics|neuroeconomics]], researchers like Daniel Kahneman proved that humans consistently use cognitive biases rather than rational optimization procedures.
