# Overview
Neuroeconomics is a field that explores the neurological and psychological mechanisms behind economic choices. 

Classical economics has generally assumed humans follow rational choice theory, where they act to maximize their self-interest according to the information they have. Neuroeconomics views humans are organisms whose behavior is tied to neural circuits in the brain. The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) calculates the subject value of options, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) compares the options to each other, and the striatum (basal ganglia) encodes salience and expected rewards. The value of options are dynamically calculated by checking the difference between expected reward and actual reward, which we call the reward prediction error (RPE). A positive error in which reality exceeds expectations causes a dopamine spike that increases the value of that option in the future. A negative error in which reality fails to meet expectations triggers a lower dopamine spike than expected, so the value of the option is [[discounting|discounted]] in the future. Choices are made as a result of the interactions of two networks. The first is the affective system, which reacts to immediate rewards and threats. The second is the executive system, which applies abstract rules and plans for the future.

With substance use patterns, the brain's baseline capacity to experience rewards is lowered as an adaptation to the constant dopamine spikes from using a substance. Cravings involve the brain seeking the extreme rewards of a substance that are guaranteed to produce more immediate dopamine spikes than the abstract reward of long-term goals like stable relationships or financial stability. If someone is a scarce environment that lacks natural rewards, then the relative value of using substances increases compared to the boredom or static suffering of other options. Many substances are also associated with a decrease in brain matter volume, so the executive system may further struggle to push back on the affective system seeking immediate rewards. This is one of the reasons [[contingency-management|Contingency Management (CM)]] is successful at helping sustain sobriety for those with stimulant use disorders where no reliably effective anti-craving medication is readily available.

Some scholars like Gene Heyman argue for the choice theory of addiction, which claims that addiction is not defined by involuntary compulsions but by voluntary choices shaped by reinforced preferences, and they respond to costs and benefits just like ordinary consumers do. The behaviors we call addiction are the result of melioration, or the act of choosing something that seems the best right now while isolating it from long-term impacts on future options. If the circuits in the brain responsible for calculating long-term consequences and future planning, like the dorsolateral prefrontal cortext (dlPFC), are compromised through chronic substance use, then the option of continuing substance use will seem like a highly rewarding option isolated from the abstract or long-term options. 

The cycle of addicts wanting to quit early in the day but wanting to use by the end of it can be explained by hyperbolic delay discounting rather than dishonesty. When someone's at work or school, their substance is generally not immediately available for open use, so the idea of using again is more distant and abstract. It becomes easier to compare the consequences of using again to the benefits of sobriety when both are in the future. When someone's at home or driving past a bar, the idea of using substances feels far more immediately accessible, which can genuinely switch the brain's preferences towards sustaining sobriety.

# Examples

## Walter Mischel's Stanford Marshmallow Experiment
You may have heard of Walter Mischel's Stanford Marshmallow Experiment where a child is given the choice of eating one marshmallow immediately or getting two marshmallows if they can wait for some period of time, like 15-20 minutes. The experiment tests the way the two networks interact to decide whether it's worth delaying the first marshmallow for the second one. The return on investment (RoI) is 100% for just 15 minutes, which seems high and obvious. Early follow-up studies have shown that a child's ability to wait strongly predicts higher SAT scores, lower BMI, and improved financial stability. Later studies found that the predictive power of the marshmallow test largely disappears when controlling for family wealth, parental education, and other environmental factors. 

In other words, a child who grows up in an unstable environment, where promises are broken and resources are scarce and irregularly available, is more likely to have adapted to a model of quickly securing immediate rewards when they're available. A child who grows up in a stable environment with a secure baseline for food has adapted to having the time to invest into long-term planning patterns. Neuroimaging studies have shown that children from the latter high-resource environments have greater volume and functional connectivity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortext (dlPFC) which tracks abstract rules that suppress immediate impulses, like keeping the high ROI in mind. Children from severely impoverished environments have less grey matter volume within their prefrontal cortex and lower volume in the hippocampus, which means there are fewer cognitive resources available to filter distractions or help delay the impulse for immediate [[gratification|gratification]]. The brain physically structures itself differently to think less about the future if the environment can't be trusted, which means prioritizing immediate rewards.
