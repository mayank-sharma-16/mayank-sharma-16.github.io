# Commentary
Black Phone was a really fun watch and a pretty fun rewatch. I wish the rules of the supernatural aspects were more fleshed out, or at least that there was something substantive to imagine as part of the world-building, but I can at least appreciate the choice to leave it ambiguous for a more mysterious, unpredictable atmosphere. After all, the main themes of the show include the unpredictable nature of abuse and the power of [[fight-flight-freeze-fawn|fight as a trauma response.]]

## The Grabber as The Abuser
The show's antagonist, the Grabber, is a parallel to Finney's father Terrence, an abusive alcoholic figure. They both can quickly cycle through moods, send mixed signals about their state of mind, apply rules inconsistently on a whim, lay out traps in their own rules, and gaslight to maintain their control on reality. The way Finney has to adapt to the Grabber's moods and rules is a reenactment of adapting to an abusive parent. For instance, the movie emphasizes sound cues that children who grow up with [[hypervigilance|hypervigilance]] learn to track when monitoring the mood of adults with power: floorboards, door handles, footsteps, subtle shifts in body language or tone of voice, etc. The "Naughty Boy" "game" that the Grabber sets up represents the way abusers have often already decided whether or not they want to deliver a punishment and look for a reason to justify it. Also, both Terrence and the Grabber are greatly upset at the idea that psychic and supernatural occurrences exist, with Terrence beating Gwen for having prophetic dreams and the Grabber screaming at Finney for hearing spirits through the black phone in the basement. They both need to interpret reality in a way that makes them feel in control of it so they lash out at those who bring up uncomfortable ideas that challenge their worldview. Notably, one of the most common themes in recovery circles is relinquishing the need to control things. In [[twelve-step|12-step]] groups, that's done by acknowledging the powerlessness of one's self-will and accepting the influence of a [[higher-power|Higher Power's]] will. In Buddhism-based groups like Refuge Recovery or Recovery Dharma, that looks like the principle of impermanence that advocates for practicing non-attachment from both the external world and the internal [[ego|ego]].

## Fighting for Freedom
Finney's freedom relies on being able to fight as a trauma response when all the other Fs have failed. Being a "good boy" for the Grabber represents the fawn response that avoids or delays punishment but doesn't bring one closer to freedom. In between some failed attempts to escape, he freezes and shuts down to conserve energy and process panic. At one point, he's able to make an attempt to leave the Grabber's house, though he's quickly recaptured before he can signal for help from anyone. Fight is the only option left to be free of the threat of the Grabber, just as it was the only way to make bullies leave Finney alone. We see that his sister Gwen is far more confrontational and aggressive than Finney is, who usually tries to run from threats or minimize himself and hope that his assailants or abuser move on quickly. Finney has to draw on the encouragement and presence of his psychic sister and the spirits of the boys who the Grabber previously killed in order to muster the courage to fight.

## Sibling Reveling
Finney and Gwen's relationship as siblings is extremely cooperative and caring, which is a common product of growing up in an abusive home. Children who grow up in abusive environments often learn to work together to secure their needs and feel safe if they can agree on the role of their abuser in failing to meet them. They might take turns interacting or managing their guardians to split up the work or suffering, or they might just be able to hear each other out with the certainty that they're talking to someone who understands what they're going through intimately. In some situations though, especially if the form of abuse and neglect from a caregiver is intended to provoke competition or infighting, siblings can end up replicating the unhealthy patterns learned from adults onto each other or selfishly look out for their own needs in a scarce environment.

Also, although the spirits of the other boys Finney talks to don't count as literal siblings, they still extend the theme of children supporting each other in the face of adults who abuse their power and neglect their responsibilities. Both before he was captured and after, others in his life either helped Finney in fights or had the fights for him. He rarely, if ever, fought back or initiated conflict on his own. Prior to fighting the Grabber, Finney tries several escape methods and avoids errors thanks to the information from previous attempts that the other boys made.

The police are able to find the bodies of the boys in the house across the street from where Finney was being kept, but they don't find him until Finney frees himself and walks outside. The Grabber's brother has a large conspiracy-like board in his living room about finding the Grabber, but he somehow misses the soundproof chamber where the Grabber kept boys in their basement (his cocaine habit can't have helped things either). Finney's dad helped by taking a leap of faith in Gwen's visions after Finney's disappearance. Adults at school, administrators and teachers, seem to have next to no presence in mediating conflicts between students, even when they result in repeated physical assaults. A point the movie seems to make is that even the most disruptive, "delinquent" kids can have something to offer in a sincere struggle against trauma 

A common saying in mental health recovery spaces to remind people of the importance of self-sufficiency is "no one is coming to save you." That might sound cynical or pessimistic, but this movie illustrates an important caveat to that saying; no one is coming to save you, but people might be willing to help you. If everyone puts in the effort to fight, we might be able to help each other save ourselves, but you have to participate in a collective struggle where people share their learning experiences from past failures. Much of the mental health recovery resources and community infrastructure of the last few centuries has been developed outside of mainstream institutional support. The stigma around mental health has led to most issues being addressed privately, whether in asylums, therapies, clinics, rehabilitation centers, digital platforms with remote access, or peer recovery circles that try to promote anonymity. Although most societies maintain stigma against those with mental or behavioral health issues in spite of any social safety net provided, communities and networks built on the idea of mutual-aid have arisen to fill the gap, like Alcoholics Anonymous (AA). These groups will repeatedly emphasize that one has no control over another's actions, and that offers a template for mutual-aid in which someone provides help without the expectation of return and without the expectation of honoring any conditions. The critical engine for the growth of these groups is their ability to maintain their members' faith in collective identities, peer support networks, and a fellowship with interpersonal or spiritual significance.
