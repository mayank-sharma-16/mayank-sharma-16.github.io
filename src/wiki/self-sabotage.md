# Overview
Self-sabotage is the act of undermining one's own goals, consciously or unconsciously. 

In recovery, self-sabotage is an extremely common topic that's addressed when examining patterns of relapsing. Sometimes it stems from a fear of failure, where someone might relapse in advance of an opportunity, because that way they don't have to confront the possibility of not being good enough. Other times, self-sabotage can stem from a fear of success because it's unfamiliar and the brain doesn't have strong [[schema|schemas]] to handle a change in [[self-image|self-image]]. Like many maladaptive coping mechanisms, self-sabotage was likely at one point a successful survival strategy for times when a child learned that standing out through success could attract criticism, jealousy, or unwanted attention. 

# Historical Context
Within psychology, the patterns of self-sabotage date back to Sigmund Freud and the death drive, also called Thanatos. Freud proposed that humans have an innate drive towards self-destruction in order to achieve the peace of inorganic existence, so they unconsciously recreate painful patterns that feel familiar and therefore safe. Carl Jung argued that repressed parts of our selves form the Shadow that operates outside of our conscious awareness, which means we might have unconscious parts of ourselves that we've repressed work against our conscious desires. 

In 1978, Edward Jones and Steven Berglas introduced the idea of "self-handicapping" to describe the pattern of people deliberately creating obstacles for themselves when they're anxious about an upcoming event. If someone is anxious about a test, they may stay up all night so that they have a reason to point to for potential failure that isn't their own skill or ability.
