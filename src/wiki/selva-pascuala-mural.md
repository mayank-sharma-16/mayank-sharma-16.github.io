# Overview
The Selva Pascuala mural is a rock painting in Villar del Humo, Spain that contains one of the earliest possible depictions of [[magic-mushrooms|magic mushrooms]] in Europe. There are two bulls painted, one above the other, with the alleged mushrooms in a line of 13 umbrella-like figures between them.

A 2011 study published in Economic Botany, Brian Akers and Gaston Guzman proposed that the mural depicted Psilocybe hispanica, a psychoactive species that often grows on bull dung and could explain the relevance of the bulls to the mushrooms. The leading theory about the paintings is that they represent [[shamanism|shamanistic]] rituals involving the consumption of mushrooms that were interpreted as providing access to different states of consciousness or visions. Two of the 13 figures have split bases resembling legs, which might suggest a psychological transformation that one experiences while using the mushrooms.

# Historical Context
The artwork was first discovered by archaeologists in 1918. By the 1960s, researchers commonly described the painting as a series of geometric symbols, though modern researchers reevaluated this interpretation given contemporary perspectives that acknowledge the integration of psychoactive substances into daily life. Still, there are arguments made by scholars that the painting represents something other than shrooms, including the bull and the symbols having been painted at different occasions and by different groups rather than as a single scene. These alternatives include abstract markers for territorial or social boundaries that may not have any particular significance except for their practical use.
