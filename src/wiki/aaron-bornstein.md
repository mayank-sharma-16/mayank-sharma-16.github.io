# Overview
Aaron Bornstein is a professor who leads the Computational Cognitive Neuroscience Laboratory (CCNL) at the University of California, Irvine (UCI), which studies the role of episodic memory retrieval in [[substance-use-disorder|Substance Use Disorder (SUD)]]. 

# Works

In 2020, Bornstein published a paper in the journal [[neuropsychopharmacology-journal|Neuropsychopharmacology]] alongside [[hanna-pickard|Hanna Pickard]] that proposed a framework for decision-making in relapse or active addiction. They argue that, when it encounters a [[trigger|trigger]] associated with substance use, the brain selectively samples early positive memories over negative consequences:

*Rather than try to break learned associations between drug cues and drug rewards, treatment should aim to strengthen existing and/or create new associations between drug cues and drug-inconsistent rewards.* [^0]

This helps explain why the brain can emit [[craving|cravings]] to use substances in spite of full awareness about the potential negative consequences, but without framing substance use as a habit that has to be retrained:

*Although drug choices in SUD have features that resemble habitual behavior, there is nonetheless limited evidence in support of a habit theory of addiction: in the majority of cases, consumption appears to remain goal-directed.* [^0]

"Goal-directed" means that the brain isn't just acting out of a conditioned, habitual routine to a trigger for cravings, but attempting to calculate the potential payoff of making different decisions based on internal goals. That calculation might be distorted by early positive memories of substance use that are being retrieved by the brain or a [[discounting|discounting]] of future rewards that come with sobriety compare to immediately accessible awards, but it's not as automatic as the idea of a habit implies. New rewarding experiences can give the brain new memories to sample when weighing the payoff of substance use.

It also explains why behavioral interventions like [[contingency-management|Contingency Management (CM)]] that offer regular tangible rewards for sustained sobriety are so effective and favorably comparable to other therapies:

*In forced-choice studies offering the immediate opportunity to use crack cocaine or receive money reward, individuals with SUD frequently choose money over drug reward. In addition, contingency management treatment is highly effective and offers positive reward contingent on drug-free urine samples in the form of money, prizes, and most recently and successfully, employment* [^0]
