# Overview
The self is one of the most fundamental concepts in psychology and philosophy, and the theory of the self one uses can shape everything about the ideas, lifestyle, and treatment that work best for an individual. 

"Western" ideas of the self tend to be individualistic, in that each person is viewed as an autonomous unit defined by intrinsic desires and personal attributes who bears ultimate responsibility over their own outcomes. In other cultures, the self is seen as a component of their community and is necessarily defined by the social roles they occupy. For instance, Buddhist worldviews argue that all things are impermanent and interconnected, which means that one's self is constantly in a state of change influenced by the environment. 

The relevance of this distinction becomes apparent in a lot of exercises and activities found in Western therapy. Many exercises geared towards finding an "authentic self" are necessarily using a particular idea of the self isolated from the environment that conditions "inauthentic" behavior. One might believe that there is no such thing as a "true self," that the "self" is a construct under constant change that has to be accepted in its current state rather than dissected to separate authentic and inauthentic patterns.
