# Overview
A Counterblaste to Tobacco is an Early Modern English treatise denouncing [[tobacco|tobacco]], written in 1604 by King James VI of Scotland and I of England. It's considered one of the earliest anti-smoking publications and examples of public health policy rhetoric.

# Quotes

## To the reader

*As every human body (dear countrymen) how wholesome soever, is notwithstanding subject, or at least naturally inclined to some sorts of diseases, or infirmities: so is there no commonwealth, or body-politic, how well governed, or peaceable soever it be, that lacks the own popular errors, and naturally inclined corruptions... For remedy whereof, it is the king's part (as the proper physician of his politic-body) to purge it of all those diseases, by medicines meet for the same: as by a certain mild, and yet just form of government, to maintain the public quietness, and prevent all occasions of commotion...*

- King James follows in the tradition of many political writers of his time who reasoned about monarchies through the metaphor of a human body and the "head" of state. Specifically, he positions the role of a king as the "physician" for this "body-politic" that can become sick or injured as every human body can be. Given that this is a treatise against tobacco, the body and health metaphor has strong thematic alignment that reinforces the connections between community, spiritual, and physical health such that the king is the most appropriate authority to discuss the issues. However, at the same time, there were limits to how much he could actually implement any kind of ban since the demand for it was already so widespread. Instead of aiming for a [[prohibitionism|prohibition]]-based policy, he sought to discourage tobacco use through taxes and cultural shifts, so he adds on that for "these base sorts of corruption" that anyone from the people "may serve to be a physician... by persuading reformation thereof." This pattern, where a state that lacks enforcement mechanisms against drugs calls on the common population to become moral police, can be found all throughout the history of drug policy. Consider [[reefer-madness|Reefer Madness]], one of the earliest anti-[[cannabis|cannabis]] films in the United States, in which government officials and doctors alike call on local parents to step up their monitoring of youth drug use through a similar [[call-to-action|call to action]] after the disastrous experiences with Prohibition left the public wary of widespread state action.
