# Overview
Alexander Hamilton was the first Secretary of the Treasury of the United States. 

Hamilton wrote extensively about the threat "ardent [[spirits|spirits]]" (or hard [[liquor|liquor]] as concentrated forms of [[alcohol|alcohol]]) posed to public health and the national moral character of the US, especially for soldiers and militias. In Federalist No. 12, he discusses the virtues of taxing liquor with the added benefit of bringing in significant revenue for the state:

*The single article of ardent spirits, under federal regulation, might be made to furnish a considerable revenue... That article would well bear this rate of duty; and if it should tend to diminish the consumption of it, such an effect would be equally favorable to the agriculture, to the economy, to the morals, and to the health of the society. There is, perhaps, nothing so much a subject of national extravagance as these spirits.*

He believed that beer and cider were healthy alternatives to the hard liquor that was associated with [[habitual-drunkenness|"habitual drunkenness"]], so he believed in [[moderation|moderation]] rather than [[abstinence|abstinence]] as many early [[temperance|temperance]] advocates did. In 1790, he submitted a report to Congress advocating for the [[whiskey-excise-act|Whiskey Excise Act]] and attributed excessive drinking habits to the affordability of spirits. 

There is no evidence to suggest Hamilton participated in substance use or drinking alcohol to a problematic extent, although some scholars and authors have argued that Hamilton did suffer from hypomanic episodes or bipolar disorder exacerbated by extreme stress and trauma. In the day following his fatal duel with Aaron Burr, he was given [[laudanum|laudanum]] to manage the pain of being shot as a standard medicine administered by physicians in the early 1800s.
