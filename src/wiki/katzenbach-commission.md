# Overview
The Katzenbach Commission, formally called the President's Commission on Law Enforcement and Administration of Justice, was a group of 19 people appointed by U.S. President Lyndon B. Johnson in 1965 to study the criminal justice system in response to soaring crime rates through the 1960s. They delivered the National Crime Survey, later called the National Criminal Victimization Survey. 

In 1967, they released a report "The Challenge of Crime in a Free Society," that showed some empirical data suggesting that victimization far outpaced statistics provided by the police. That suggested people were reluctant to report crimes, much of which was driven by the conditions of poverty and youth disillusionment about institutions managing the future, and that the justice system was too fragmented and obsolete to earn people's trust. They made 200 recommendations, many of which were adopted and shaped the fundamentals of policing:

- 9-1-1: the creation of a single national emergency number to streamline police dispatch

- Centralizing crime data: standardizing the FBI's Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) system

- Community policing: law enforcement needed to walk in communities and build relations, as well as share the diversity of the neighborhoods they police

- Professionalization: mandatory minimum training hours, psychological screenings for officers, and hiring more candidates with college degrees

- Federal funding: providing the case for the creation of the Law Enforcement Assistance Administration (LEAA) which gave billions of dollars in [[training-and-technical-assistance|training and technical assistance (TTA)]] until 1982 when its structure was absorbed into the Office of Justice Programs (OJP)

Chapter 8 of their report is dedicated to Narcotics and Drug Abuse. It notably includes a separation of [[cannabis|marijuana]] from heroin in terms of the scientific knowledge available then about the dangers of weed and concerns about harsh criminal sentences for it. That aside, it advocates for viewing [[addiction|addiction]] as a medical and public health issue rather than a moral failing or the result of a criminal personality, as well as the concept of civil commitment which allows individuals with addiction to be placed in regulated [[rehab|rehabilitation facilities]] rather than standard criminal justice facilities like state prisons. There's also an argument about [[mandatory-minimums|mandatory minimum]] sentencing being counterproductive and recommended that judges have flexibility in sentencing those with a disease from those who participate in trafficking. As for addressing the root cause of drug issues, the commission pointed at organized crime as the primary engine behind the drug trade and recommended that law enforcement renew their focus on high-level networks rather than low-level users. A problem underlying all of these other issues is that the federal government has lacked data on substance use patterns and the effectiveness of rehab programs, which makes data-driven policymaking difficult.
