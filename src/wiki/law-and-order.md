# Overview
"Law and order" is not just a show that's spanned 25 seasons across 36 years as of 2026. It's also one of the most common political euphemisms used to discuss the brutal enforcement mechanisms behind the War on Drugs. After all, the opposite of "law and order" is "crime and chaos," and what human being lucky enough to be born with the red, white, and blue running through their blood in the shining city on the hill would be opposed to the enforcement of the United States Constitution? 

As it turns out, there's a circular problem here, and we need to delineate three concepts to understand it: power is the ability to act to shape reality, authority is the position of dictating how power should be used, and legitimacy is the acceptance of the use of power. Someone who breaks into your house is exercising power even though the authority to decide what happens in it rests with you, and since you presumably don't approve of the break-in, it's not a legitimate entry into your home. 

A similar principle can be applied to indigenous reservations in the United States. European colonialists exerted their power to perpetrate mass genocide and warfare against indigenous nations and created their own systems of authority on top of new societies, but indigenous nations never fully recognized those as legitimate, especially the United States federal government which has regularly broken its treaties. Still, the federal government has assigned itself the authority to ultimately dictate what happens on tribal lands; local tribal authorities are allowed to exist and make decisions with some power, but the federal government uses its own power to ultimately approve or disapprove of their structure and decision-making.

One of the inevitable problems with authority is that its power inherently shapes the information that people use to determine the legitimacy of authority. An authority of a system might declare that some material is unsuitable for publication and distribution using their power over information channels, but that material might contain criticisms of that authority figure which would call into question their legitimacy. Instead, the authoritative entity might shape the distribution of information to provide justifications their own legitimacy. Consider education. If a school board or other regulatory body decides to use textbooks that frame colonial America as a necessary progression towards spreading rational Enlightenment thinking that was being attacked by native tribes who wanted to cling onto inferior or outdated traditions, children might grow up thinking the United States is imperfect but generally aims itself towards progress that betters the world's living conditions. This might fly in the face of other works by researchers and academics that depicts America as an imperialist nation founded on the idea of corporate freedom to seek profits which co-opts all other systems towards that end, which is closer to the narrative someone might receive if they grew up in a country that has suffered from a history of Western imperialism and occupation. At the same time, authorities in those countries seek to legitimize themselves by propagating narratives about their own nobility and progress being undermined by Western powers, which are then blamed for the flaws observed within that nation.
