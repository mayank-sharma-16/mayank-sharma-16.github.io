# Overview
A longitudinal study tracks variables over a period of time, in contrast to a cross-sectional analysis that collects data about a single point in time. When we want to study cause-and-effect relationships between variables that might influence each other, we need a longitudinal study that observes their changes over time.

Consider the relationship between age and intelligence. A cross-sectional study might look at the population by age bracket, see higher intelligence scores in ages 20-30 than 50-60, and suggest a conclusion that this means intelligence decreases as one's age increases. However, a longitudinal study that tracks a specific group of people from ages 20 to 50 might find that intelligence scores remain stable or modestly increase on average, which contradicts the finding of the first study. Instead, taken together, we might interpret the studies to suggest that each generation of people tends to have higher base intelligence than the generation of people that came before. This is a real phenomenon observed in IQ scores called the Flynn Effect where the growth of education and nutrition in developing nations throughout the 20th century was improving the foundations for learning and abstract thinking in children. 

A similar partial picture is painted in studies about the prevalence of substance use disorders. Cross-sectional studies tend to show that the majority of people who meet the criteria for SUD in their youth recover without treatment, also called spontaneous remission. Addiction rates drop off sharply after the age of 30. However, longitudinal studies show that many of those who struggle with addiction go through periods of abstinence that might include relapses and many die young if they follow an uninterrupted trajectory of increasingly risky substance use patterns. For some, meeting the criteria for SUD can be the result of a phase of life like heavy college drinking or the culture of a job that normalizes heavy drinking after work, which they then age out of as they get older and adjust their lifestyles around family, career, or health circumstances. The population that struggles with substance use to the point of requiring clinical treatment generally experience substance use disorder as a chronic progressive condition that presents with alternating periods of remission into abstinence or moderation between relapses into active addiction.
