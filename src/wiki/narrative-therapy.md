# Overview
Narrative therapy is a form of therapy that views identity as a series of narratives rather than a structure that may be defective for biological or psychological reasons, and it frames problems as external forces acting on the client which can be addressed by constructing alternative narratives that feature resilience.

Suppose a client says "I can't go outside because I get overwhelmed with anxiety." Asking a question from the traditional model of internalized problems might sound like "What are you anxious about when you go outside and see crowds?" Alternatively, a model of externalized problems might sound like "What does Anxiety tell you when you go outside that makes you feel helpless?" or "Where did Anxiety learn to repeat these thoughts at you?" to explore the sociocultural experiences or rules that influence the individual. Finding solutions for this Anxiety might start with a question like "How did you beat Anxiety in the past?" 

Narrative therapy, much like [[liberation-psychology]], emerged in the 1970s and 1980s as a rejection of the Western convention of pathologizing individuals with an [[locus-of-control|internal locus of control]] rather than organizing against social structures that determine someone's environment and cues. It was founded by Michael White and David Epston and influenced heavily by Michel Foucault's arguments that internal feelings of failure are the result of dominant societal expectations subjugating individual identity. A major goal of narrative therapy is deconstructing the societal rules that produce an internal sense of failure.
