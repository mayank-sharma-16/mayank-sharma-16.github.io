# Overview
A Common Struggle: A Personal Journey Through the Past and Future of Mental Illness and Addiction (2015) is both a memoir and policy analysis written by [[patrick-j-kennedy|Patrick J. Kennedy]] and [[stephen-fried|Stephen Fried]].

# Quotes

## Prologue
*I grew up among people who were geniuses at not talking about things. When I was a teenager going for therapy during my parents' divorce, I wouldn't tell my psychiatrist the truth because I wasn't sure I could trust him to keep things private. Then one day I walked into a bookstore and browsed the 'Kennedy section' and saw that many of the books included the 'family secrets' I had refused to discuss. But I still wouldn't talk about them.*

- For those unfamiliar with American history, the Kennedy family has been the closest thing Americans have enjoyed to a royal family whose drama becomes the subject of both national tabloids and historical scholarship. President John F. Kennedy's sex life, assassination, nepotism, and underworld connections have all been common topics found in American media, but the rest of the Kennedy family has involved scandals too. Rosemary Kennedy was lobotomized for behavioral difficulties, Ted Kennedy failed to report that he drove off a bridge with a woman in the car for 10 hours (called "Chappaquiddick" for the place it happened, the event was directly referenced in [[succession|Succession]]), Joseph Kennedy Sr. was accused of being a [[bootlegger|bootlegger]] during [[prohibitionism|Prohibition]], and claims have been made about political deals between the Kennedy family and Italian organized crime groups ("the Mafia"). These are the general type of "family secrets" that Patrick refers to here.

*I did this with my Republican House colleague Jim Ramstad from Minnesota.  Before my crash I had known him, although not well, as one of the only members of Congress who was openly in recovery. But after my arrest and hospitalization he was the first one to come visit me at the Mayo Clinic. I asked if he would be my sponsor in recovery—I had never had a real sponsor before—and he invited me into his network  of friends in recovery on Capitol Hill.*

- Though it's from 2015, it's still remarkable to see two politicians from across the aisle develop an intimate bond to address extremely personal struggles, like that between someone in [[recovery|recovery]] and their [[sponsor|sponsor]] to whom they would admit all their [[character-defects|character defects]] and wrongdoings as per [[twelve-step|12-step]] group practices.

*My hope is that by writing about and exposing the worlds I get to visit - as a politician, advocate, patient, and family member - I might be able to make your journey less isolated. These struggles are much more common than most people realize, but too many of us still face them alone, if we face them at all. That isn't necessary, it isn't healthy, and it isn't how any of us want to live our lives. I believe more than every that we have the power to help change the world for people who have mental illnesses and addictions, and for all of those whose lives are touched by these brain diseases - which is to say, all of us. In fact, I have bet my life on it.*

- Here, Patrick reveals the impetus for the book's title, where he hopes to use his personal journey from his own position of privilege and influence to lead a conversation about mental health as a common struggle experienced by families. It's notable that Patrick spends quite some time in his Prologue establishing the futility and self-defeating nature of this type of stigma around mental health that leads to suppression instead of collective advocacy, using both his own father in the U.S. Senate and colleagues in the U.S. House of Representatives as examples of leaders who often suffer from the same stigma and lack of care as their constituents do because of policy consequences.

## Chapter 1

*If you are lucky, you have no idea what it's like to have a disease that society questions because people can't "see" it and aren't sure they "believe in" it. I have a couple of those illnesses, which are easy to deny or downplay. While people always tried to be sympathetic... I also felt dismissed as weak, as if I could control these illnesses but simply chose not to... This is something I wish for everyone who has a mental illness or addiction disorder... to have your illness taken as seriously and sympathetically as cancer.*

- Patrick is describing the concept of an [[invisible-disability|invisible disability]] here, which refer to both physical and mental health disabilities that don't have obvious external signals to observe. For some context, surveys show at least 70% of all disabilities are invisible, which means 1 in 10 adults in the United States have one. The extreme majority of people, 96%, living with chronic illnesses have an invisible disability. Although many people are outwardly sympathetic about having mental health issues and invisible disabilities, Patrick describes how even friends and family would struggle to take his bipolar disorder and substance use disorder seriously compared to a physical problem like his cancer diagnosis, which gave him a strange kind of pleasure to have received.
