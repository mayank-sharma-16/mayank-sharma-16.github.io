# Overview
Defining shame is complicated because the term is widely used and we think (and hope) that we're pointing at the same feeling, but actual definitions can be contradictory or mutually exclusive.

The conventional ideas about guilt vs. shame in recovery circles is that guilt refers to the feeling that one has done something wrong while shame refers to the feeling that one's being is fundamentally wrong. The implication then is that guilt might be useful, but shame is not.

Jean-Paul Sartre argued that shame is fundamentally a social experience that is felt not when someone does something wrong, but when someone realizes they are vulnerable to judgment in someone else's worldview. If you were completely alone and could trust that you were, shame would be impossible to experience. Bernard Williams argued that even when someone experiences shame in private, it's because they're viewing themselves through the eyes of an imaginary audience.

Paul Gilbert explicitly separated the concept into external shame, or the vulnerability to being seen as bad, and internal shame, the feeling that one is bad. Plenty of people might not believe they're bad, but might be highly concerned about the consequences of being treated as bad for justifiable reasons. The difference is significant, as it determines whether the problem comes from an internal issue like [[cognitive-distortion|cognitive distortions]] or a genuinely hostile external environment.
