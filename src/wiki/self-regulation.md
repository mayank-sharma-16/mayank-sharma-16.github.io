# Overview
Self-regulation refers to one's ability to observe oneself, judge the behavior, and adjust their responses. 

# Historical Context
The earliest foundations of the term self-regulation come from the idea of [[homeostasis|homeostasis]] with the body physically adjusting itself to maintain baseline functions, which Walter Bradford described as "regulation."

America in the mid-20th century was dominated by [[behaviorism|behaviorist]] perspectives that argued human behavior was shaped entirely by external rewards and punishment. Albert Bandura introduced Social Learning Theory, which instead argued that humans create internal representations of feedback loops rather than purely reacting to environmental cues. Bandura described "self-regulation" as a three-step process consisting of self-observation, judgment, and self-response. Charles Carver and Michael Scheier introduced the TOTE loop as a framework of the human mind that uses [[positive-reinforcement|positive reinforcement]] and [[negative-reinforcement|negative reinforcement]] feedback loops: Test, Operate, Test, Exit. [[Self-control]] is considered a more narrow subset of self-regulation that emphasizes the ability to control impulses and initiate efforts.

Different specializations within psychology built on the concept of self-regulation in different ways. Social psychologists like Roy Baumeister explored the Strength Model that centers self-control in the efforts of self-regulation. Barry Zimmerman developed Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) as an educational framework that focused on how students step through forethought, performance, and self-reflection. Researchers in psychophysiology like Stuart Shanker tied self-regulation to the ability for the central nervous system to manage stress and excitement.
