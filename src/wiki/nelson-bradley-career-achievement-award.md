# Overview
The Nelson Bradley Career Achievement Award was established in 1983 by the National Association of Addiction Treatment Providers (NAATP) as a lifetime achievement award. 

Nelson J. Bradley was a psychiatrist who served as the superintendent of Willmar State Hospital in Minnesota, pioneering an approach that incorporated [[twelve-step|12-Step]] principles and peer counselors into treatment facilities, now called the Minnesota Model or Twelve-Step Facilitation (TSF). A 2020 Cochrane Systematic Review analyzed 27 studies of 10,000 participants and found that manualized TSF programs inspired by the Minnesota Model are more effective for sustained long-term abstinence than clinical interventions like [[cognitive-behavioral-therapy|Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT)]]. 

Bradley was instrumental in the scientific community's shift to seeing addiction as a chronic brain disease than a moral or willpower problem, though other researchers have argued that the brain disease model has pathologized and medicalized an issue rooted in socioeconomic systems. 

Some argue that the main value of the Minnesota Model is peer support, which increases recovery capital by introducing people to mutual aid societies that share emotional support, but tangible support as well. Navigating healthcare, employment, housing, culture, and other aspects of daily life are made significantly easier through supportive community networks, which clinical or therapeutic interventions are not necessarily equipped to provide or substitute for. 

Bradley's influence on the field was substantial, and the Minnesota Model remains the most common base for treatment formats used in the United States. Bradley's legacy did a lot of work to destigmatize addiction away from the untreatable death sentence that medical professionals assumed it was. We can discuss the flaws of the Minnesota Model as a particular protocol or treatment with the advantage of new evidence and hindsight, but the man himself made major changes in the way addiction was perceived by the scientific and medical communities.
