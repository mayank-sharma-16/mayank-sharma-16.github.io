# Overview
10 Cloverfield Lane is a standalone companion story to the original Cloverfield movie that shares the premise of an incomprehensible otherworldly threat forcing humanity into a struggle for survival, although with a claustrophobic bunker centering human conflict this time rather than a ground zero incident that reveals human frailty.

# Substance Use Themes
This movie isn't about addiction or an elaborate allegory that can be compared to addiction, but substances do facilitate several key parts of the plot. Setting the story in a bunker sets up themes of self-empowerment through overcoming the internal [[avoidance|avoidance]] of one's external fears.

## Howard's Need for Control
For Howard, the human antagonist, his bunker is a tool to control Michelle and Emmett, which he rationalizes as protection for them against a dangerous and unpredictable world. His methods to distill [[vodka|vodka]] despite being in a bunker reflects his paranoia about losing control by being caught unprepared, and so his attempt to share his alcohol as a friendly gesture falls flat given the coercive need for control underlying all his interactions. The same happens when he provides sedatives and painkillers to Michelle for the injuries from her accident; it only reinforces a power disparity in how prepared one is for handling the other. Then the movie reveals that Howard has lied repeatedly about his past and his motives, including the details of the "accident" that led to him dragging an unconscious Michelle into the bunker. His real agenda is to play out the fantasy of being alone with a daughter figure as a way of avoiding the reality of being estranged from his real daughter, and so for Howard the bunker is a cage of his own design and control that gives him the comfort of avoiding loneliness with everything he could ever want in one locked spot.

## Michelle's Escape
Michelle, our key protagonist, holds onto an unopened bottle of whiskey at the start of the movie while leaving her fiance after some kind of a conflict. We don't really get details about this either, but the end of the movie features Michelle using the same bottle as a [[molotov-cocktail|Molotov cocktail]] before driving to Houston to join up with a resistance force fighting against the invaders. The bunker represents the crossroads that Michelle is at in life, where she can either choose to continue her passive avoidance patterns when she's clearly unhappy or take a risk to be free. She overcomes Howard's character to free herself from the bunker, and she then uses her bottle of whiskey to make a Molotov cocktail to use against an alien ship. Howard was right that a real invasion did happen and that it was dangerous to go outside, yet she overcomes both the controller in the name of protection and the hostile external threats alike.

## Emmett's Self-Sabotage
Emmett, a secondary protagonist, explains how he [[self-sabotage|sabotaged]] his athletic scholarship in his youth by making sure to get so blackout drunk that he would fail to wake up in time to catch the bus. This sabotage stemming from the fear of success, is an extremely common pattern to hear about and discuss in recovery circles. Failure can be humiliating if you have to face the idea that your estimation of yourself was wrong. Some people have become so accustomed to expecting failure that success doesn't make sense to them, as if they'd be pretending to be someone they're not (as in [[imposter-syndrome|imposter syndrome]]). If someone uses a substance to cope with their failures, and they use to sabotage their own successes because only failure seems appropriate or earned to them, they may be stuck in a self-destructive cycle that can lead to chronic substance use patterns. In Emmett's case, he doesn't appear to be someone with ongoing [[dependence|dependence]] or habits, but his past choices with drinking still kept him in the same 40 square miles he lived his whole life. When he dies, it's after he alone takes the blame for a conspiracy he and Michelle were planning to overpower Howard, and Howard executes him. The act is still self-destruction, but it also becomes self-sacrifice since it confronts a threat rather than self-sabotage out of avoidance.

So, the film doesn't center substance use or drinking as a theme itself, but alcohol is associated with avoidance within the film in each of the three characters as they confront the constraints of the bunker.
