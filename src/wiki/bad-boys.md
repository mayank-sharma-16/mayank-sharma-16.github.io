# Overview
Bad Boys is a 1995 buddy-cop action-comedy film that features Will Smith as a cop who routinely breaks the law in the process of enforcing it while pursuing violent criminals and drug dealers, following the theft of $100 million worth of [[heroin|heroin]] from the police department's evidence lockers. 

# Commentary

## Copaganda
The heroin is a [[macguffin|MacGuffin]] to give our protagonists an excuse to play the role of aggressive, sarcastic cops who flout the rules and norms in the name of the greater good. Crime entertainment from the 1990s framed drug enforcement as a heroic, masculine role whose aggression and violence are an inevitable result of fighting the dangers of organized crime in the drug trade. Those who lie to conceal their connection to the drug use or the drug trade are built up as villainous thugs while the cops who lie are just getting into wacky shenanigans as a result of their human flaws. To quote one of the punchlines as a witness in protection is handcuffed to a steering wheel inside a car: "Is this even legal? I don't think this is legal." Meanwhile, the drug dealers that the cops investigate are literally set up at a place called Club Hell. "Bad Boys" is meant to have a kind of charming rogueishness to it, like "Mad Men."

## Glamorizing 
The movie has been accused of [[Glamorizing|glamorizing]] the nightlife associated with drugs through aesthetics that involve flashy displays of wealth, gloss and glamour, and a reference to sexual thrills or taboos. Michael Bay is known for including these aesthetics in the films he directs. However, there are plenty of grimy environments that the movie shows as well, especially around manufacturing and low-level distribution. If anything, the main lifestyle glamorized is that of Will Smith's character Mike Lowrey, who lives the life of a wealthy bachelor thanks to his inheritance. His partner Marcus Burnett, a family man with three kids who feels dissatisfied with his sex life in his marriage, is regularly jealous of Lowrey. The drug dealers are depicted in a range between stereotypical bosses in suits and low-level informants who still work physical jobs, absent of any "rags-to-riches" storyline or "thieves with honor" characters.

## Black Masculinity

One of the reasons that the buddy-cop formula is popular is because it naturally forces confrontations between two people who represent a dynamic of opposing pairs yet have to protect each other in dangerous situations. Sometimes that means a good cop/bad cop pairing to present a carrot/stick approach and how the state needs to use both in the process of law enforcement. 

In this case, Lowrey and Burnett are both the stick who abuse their authority and disregard norms, but since the aggression is constantly leading to banter and slapstick comedy that furthers the plot, it becomes entertaining rather than threatening to an audience imagining this world. The contrast between Lowrey as a privileged bachelor and Burnett as a working family man give two different but valid representations of masculinity in black men, who are even accepted for their rule-breaking violence by a system of law enforcement so long as it furthers the agenda of apprehending criminals. It's also noteworthy that the main leader of the cartel is a wealthy European, Fouchet, which means that the strength of these black men is directed towards foreigners rather than a domestic system of crime or racialized politics around US drug policing.

In sum, the film signals that black men can retain their masculinity while navigating different lifestyles and roles in society, and even indulge in the excesses of toxic masculinity that lead to violence and abuse of power, so long as their actions ultimately align with the greater good of taking down the villains of the state. Specifically these include international organized crime syndicates whose operations reach the lowest level of working Joe looking for a quick and dirty side hustle. Various scholars who study the evolution of black archetypes in comedy, which includes the infamous Jim Crow, have examined the buddy-cop formula in the 1980s and 1990s as a way for black celebrities to play vigilante-like roles that superficially break ordinary norms while serving the main interests of a criminal justice system built on politics from the [[war-on-drugs|War on Drugs]]. Ed Guerrero has described this pattern of adopting the aesthetics of violent black resistance to vigilante or anti-hero policing as "contain and commodify."
