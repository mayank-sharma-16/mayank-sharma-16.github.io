# Overview
Self-image refers to someone's beliefs or internal model of themselves. It's related to but distinct from [[self-esteem|self-esteem]]; self-image refers to how someone sees themselves while self-esteem refers to how someone feels about themselves, which includes how their self-image makes them feel.

# Models

## Self-Schema Theory
In Hazel Markus's Self-Schema Theory, the self-image is a network of cognitive [[schema|schemas]] stored in long-term memory. Each person holds schemas for different parts of their life that form part of the overall self-image, like a "shy" schema when at the grocery store, a "silent anger" schema when in conflict with a family member, or a "trustworthy confidante" schema when hearing a friend vent about their relationship. When we encounter information from the world, we apply [[selective-attention|selective attention]] towards information and cues related to self-schemas that validate them while filtering out contradictory evidence. 

## Self-Discrepancy Theory
E. Tory Higgins proposed a system of the self into three states:

- The Actual Self: this is the self-image, which is the representation of the qualities you believe you actually have

- The Ideal Self: this is the representation of the qualities you or someone else wants you to have 

- The Ought Self: this is the representation of the qualities you or someone believes you have an obligation to have

When people experience a discrepancy between their Actual Self and Ideal Self, they're more likely to experience depression and disappointment. When people experience a discrepancy between their Actual Self and Ought Self, they're more likely to experience emotions like anxiety and guilt.

## Schema Therapy
Jeffrey Young derived Schema Therapy for [[early-maladaptive-schemas|Early Maladaptive Schemas (EMS)]], which are frameworks internalized during childhood that might be dysfunctional or warped in ways that produce distress, including somatic symptoms. If a child experiences unmet needs like a lack of compassion from caregivers, they might adopt maladaptive schemas for their self-image, like a "defective" schema that says one is fundamentally broken and unlovable. 

## Internal Working Models
John Bowlby and Kim Bartholomew proposed Internal Working Models as an approach to the development of mental representations rooted in attachment theory. Early relationships lead individuals to create the Model of Self (self-image) and the Model of Other, and four paths result out of that:

- Positive Model of Self + Positive Model of Other = Secure 

- Negative Model of Self + Positive Model of Other = Preoccupied, Anxious-Ambivalent

- Positive Model of Self + Negative Model of Other = Dismissing-Avoidant

- Negative Model of Self + Negative Model of Other = Fearful-Avoidant, Disorganized

## Looking-Glass Self
Charles Cooley's model is a sociological framework rather than one driven by clinical psychology, but it has remained influential in both fields. There are three steps:

1. Imagination: you imagine the way your appearance and actions appear to others

2. Interpretation: you imagine how others judge your imagined appearance

3. Internalization: you develop a self-image based on the perceived judgment from others
