# Overview
Nostalgia casually means the sentiment of longing for the past. In the past, it used to be a formal diagnosis itself that was documented often in 19th century wars when soldiers appeared to have chronic despair after distance from home. By the time the DSM-5 was published, a clinically significant level of nostalgia might be seen as a sign of an adjustment disorder, prolonged grief disorder, or Major Depressive Disorder (MDD).

Although indulging nostalgia is often interpreted as being resistant to change and stuck in old ways, researchers have found evidence that nostalgia can have some positive psychological benefits. It can improve social connectedness, strengthen one's sense of a continuous identity, be a source of motivation, and stimulate the release of comforting neurotransmitters like oxytocin. At the same time, nostalgia can become a maladaptive coping mechanism that really does enable someone to avoid dealing with a hopeless or unpleasant present. Trying to recreate an idealized past can also be an unhealthy pattern that interferes with growth and accepting change.
