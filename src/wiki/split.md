# Overview
This is one of the many movies and shows that I find repugnant for a sensationalized use of mental health disorders as a plot point, so before discussing any details of the movie, I want to just put a few facts out there.

First, individuals who have dissociative disorders are far more likely to be victims of violence or cause harm to themselves than commit violence against anyone else, which makes sense since dissociative disorders are generally the result of a prolonged history of trauma. 90% of those with a diagnosis of Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID) have documented histories of chronic child abuse or neglect. Keep in mind that the prevalence of DID is around 1%, meaning that if you've met 100 people, it's plausible that you've met someone who has it, and you likely had no clue.

Second, post-traumatic growth (PTG) is the concept that an individual who undergoes trauma doesn't just return to their previous baselines prior to the trauma, but instead develops more psychological resilience, empathy, and purposeful direction than before. It is not the background of a supervillain origin story where someone develops new abilities from some hidden ocean of human potential. It is the result of someone overcoming significant challenges, both internal and external, with the resources available within humans in general. Hollywood tends to think it's doing something clever by inverting the public perception of trauma as something that breaks someone into a dysfunctional mess and instead deriving the antithesis to be some kind of sudden transformation with upsides and downsides, dramaticized in a movie's narrative as entertainment with a moral lesson about perseverance and potential. In truth, healing oneself after trauma is a long process that comes with no singular triumphant moment or immunity to the things that were once troubling; it's an arduous process of developing the resilience and coping skills to handle a grueling emotional spectrum, and it often involves healing through new environments and processes than just defeating a villain that stands as a metaphor for past issues.

Third, dissociative mechanisms are survival adaptations, meaning that individuals who have DID are just ordinary people who often go to great lengths to try and hide symptoms, and only a very small minority exhibit any kind of obvious switches in their identity. This is standard for anyone who might be neurodivergent and learns to "mask" their internal functions to avoid standing out, which can even extend to denying the existence of symptoms to themselves. This means that those who do switch between alters are more likely to make up excuses for why they seem different than just openly acknowledge other personalities to someone. To an outside observer, a switch can just look like someone spacing out before snapping back to the present moment, which one might easily attribute to being tired or preoccupied.

Fourth, because the brain is not literally a compartmentalized space or set of functions, switches don't commonly manifest as distinct personalities checking in and out of control of a body. Instead, someone might experience strong passive influence from an alter or multiple alters simultaneously asserting influence at once, which can lead to dissociation, sometimes described specifically as depersonalization or derealization. Actual switches between alters aren't conscious planned events any more than flashbacks resulting from PTSD are. Certain stimuli can serve as triggering events, especially if they're associated with threats or major stressor, but someone might even experience a switch just in encountering a safe friend. Internally, switches can be heavily disorienting, meaning that headaches, nausea, dizziness, blurred vision, shivers, muscle twitches, and other somatic symptoms can emerge. Amnesia in DID, similar to flashbacks, can result in someone losing time and having no idea how they got somewhere, which means the idea of personalities somehow coordinating an elaborate conspiracy with each other an extremely far-fetched fantastical notion. 

Fifth, while different alters in DID can be associated with bodily changes like different baseline heart rates and even different allergy profiles, the idea of one alter being able to climb on walls in a way no human can, having the skin strength to resist knives and shotguns, or curing their diabetes is science-fiction. 

James McAvoy plays his parts well, and I'd by lying if I said there wasn't well-executed entertainment behind the premise or that I disapprove of the general idea of being inspired by mental health struggles to produce artistic entertainment, but this movie very explicitly embraces DID as a plot device in ways that go beyond trauma demons. It courts integration with modern psychology and therapy over an actual diagnosis people receive and battle social stigma about. M. Night Shyamalan is low-hanging fruit as far as film criticism goes, so I'm going to be fair: [[psycho|Psycho]] suffers from these same problems, and I'm not necessarily willing to give it a pass just because it's a product of the 1960s.
