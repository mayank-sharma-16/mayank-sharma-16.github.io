# Overview
Moral psychology describes the psychological frameworks used in moral judgment and reasoning, which involves an intersection of philosophy, psychology, and cognitive science. 

# Categories
There are several systems for categorizing moral psychologies relative to each other. 

## Shweder's Big Three Ethics
Richard Shweder proposed that moral reasoning systems across the globe fell into one of the Big Three frameworks:

- The Ethic of Autonomy: individuals are the primary moral agents, so actions are morally wrong if they deprive someone of autonomy. Under this view, someone may have a right to use and sell substances because they have ought to have bodily autonomy and the right to trade property, and they can only have their behavior regulated if it impedes on someone else's autonomy. However, one could also argue that addictive substances inherently impair someone's autonomy, so selling them is the morally wrong autonomous act in the situation.

- The Ethic of Community: individuals are members of larger groups, so actions are morally wrong if they threaten group cohesion for the sake of individual preferences. Substances that lead individuals to fall out of compliance with their social expectations or society's norms lead to condemnation from this view since it weakens group cohesion. However, other substances are seen as gifts that improve group morale, like bringing coffee for everyone in the office or bringing wine to a dinner party.

- The Ethic of Divinity: individuals are creatures that carry the sacred in them, so actions are considered wrong if they degrade or pollute the sacredness of a person or the divine order. In this perspective, the consumption of certain substances or participation in certain processes is thought to harm the sacred character within an individual, and purification rituals are often required to rid one of the contamination or harm. 

## Rule-based vs. Role-based Morality
Another axis of comparison involves:

- Rule-based morality: moral codes operate based on abstract rules like legal codes or math codes, like "do not lie" or "treat others equally", such that lying about a murder to protect a family member is considered wrong because one's moral rules say that killing people is wrong and lying is wrong.

- Role-based morality: moral imperatives are dependent on the context of one's role, such that lying to protect a family member in spite of the rules or fairness is considered an act of loyalty in their role as family. Similarly, a lawyer who helps to obscure or diminish evidence of wrongdoing on behalf of a client is considered professional and disciplined in their role as amoral legal defense.

## Haidt's Moral Foundation Theory
Jonathan Haidt's Moral Foundation Theory identifies 6 moral foundations that overlap with some of the points discussed above:

1. Harm/Care 

2. Fairness/Cheating

3. Loyalty/Betrayal

4. Authority/Subversion

5. Sanctity/Degradation

6. Liberty/Oppression

Haidt's research indicates that those who identify as politically liberal in the United States tend to highly value the Harm/Care and Fairness/Cheating with less emphasis on the others. Meanwhile, the politically conservative tend to have a more even balance across the others, meaning they emphasize Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, and Sanctity/Degradation more. 

# Examples

## Protestant Moral Psychology
A phrase I use very often on this site and in general that's applicable to American culture is "Protestant moral psychology." By this, I mean a constant internalized moral surveillance that looks for the signs someone is one of the few destined for success in their demonstrations of purity, discipline, and work ethic. The abstract moral laws of God apply to everyone, which then leads to lot of emphasis on laws and contracts.

Max Weber argued that the Protestant Reformation led a moral transformation in the significance of work such that it became a moral calling instead of just a material necessity. Weber attributed the foothold of capitalism and industrialism in America and Western societies to this psychological association between self-worth, self-reliance, and productivity, though this perspective has been heavily criticized by scholars and commentators. Marxist scholars argued that Weber got the cause-and-effect backwards so that wealthy property owners adopted the Protestant theology that justified their capital accumulation, and modern historians have access to more data analysis that backs that up. Historians have also pointed out that many of the capitalist structures like banking networks and international joint-stock ventures were already being used as far back as the Renaissance under a Catholic Italy. Plus, Protestant regions like Scotland didn't reflect anywhere near the same kind of growth that America had. Where Protestant beliefs did encourage more growth was through an emphasis on increasing literacy rates, since Protestant communities wanted every child to be able to read the Bible themselves and set up school systems to train children to do so.

[[aa-is-religious|Alcoholics Anonymous and other 12-step groups are heavily rooted in Protestant moral psychology.]] William Wilson (Bill W.) attributes the spiritual theory of AA to the Oxford Group, which was explicitly a Protestant revivalist movement led by Lutheran minister Frank Buchman. The idea of an individual needing to surrender their ego and accept being powerless through a spiritual awakening in order to let the will of a [[higher-power|Higher Power]] restore and maintain their sanity is profoundly Protestant. Internal self-examination to identify moral wrongs and purify oneself through rituals involving the confession of the conscience so that a Higher Power may act on and through you is a core theme in 12-step groups. The use of the term "alcoholic" or "addict" as an equalizer of identity encourages a perspective that views everyone as uniform individuals following abstract rules rather than necessarily repairing one's relationship to society outside of the program.
