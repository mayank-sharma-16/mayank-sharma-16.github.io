# Overview
A carcinogen refers to anything that can increase the risk of developing cancer, whehter it's a substance, an organism, or radiation exposure. 

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) groups carcinogens into four categories based on hazard, defined as whether the carcinogen can cause cancer under any circumstances, rather than risk, defined as the likelihood of the carcinogen causes cancer in typical exposure.

- Group 1: Carcinogenic to humans. Examples include tobacco, alcohol, processed meats, radiation exposure, asbestos.

- Group 2A: Probably carcinogenic to humans. Examples include red meat, graveyard shifts, extremely hot beverages, byproducts of frying foods.

- Group 2B: Possibly carcinogenic to humans. Examples include aloe vera extracts, engine exhausts, pickled vegetables.

- Group 3: Not classifiable as carcinogenic. Coffee, tea, fluorescent lighting, chlorinated drinking water.

As for meats, there's an increasingly popular argument that the disparities in colon cancer and gastrointestinal issues between Western nations and Eastern nations can be tracked to red meat consumption patterns. South Asian countries like India and Bangladesh maintain lower baseline rates of colon cancer, which is often attributed to cultural principles and socioeconomic systems that emphasize plant-centric diets with fiber rather than red or processed meats. In Asian communities that have begun to rapidly industrialize and shifted toward processed foods, GI cancer rates seem to be increasing as well. The exact mechanisms are still being studied, but some theories suggest that red meat diets encourage the growth of inflammatory carcinogenic gut bacteria while plant-based diets promote the growth of fiber-loving bacteria that fortify intestinal linings.
