# Introduction

*Well, there's my excuse. I'm late because my tits caught on fire.*

I have a soft spot for movies that were made in the same year as me. Is *28 Days* an enjoyable movie? I would say yes. Is it a good depiction of addiction? I would say meh. It's the type of movie you might watch in rehab so that you can poke fun at how it differs from reality while being similar enough to merit the comparison in the first place. Overall, I think it comes closer to treating rehab as a summer camp through which a young woman matures to discover the type of love she really wants than the first step in dealing with the struggles of trying to survive with a mental health disorder that has fatal implications and roots in unhealed trauma. Those concepts are certainly touched on in the movie, but their narrative purpose is strongly structured around the standard rom-com formula of a past that must be overcome, with success symbolized by finding one's "true" love. Compare this to something like *Trainspotting*, which deals much more directly with the raw ugliness of addiction, withdrawals, and the consequences to everyone around.

Also, the soundtrack really does not let you forget that this was made in the early aughts. 

# Live Commentary

*You make it impossible to love you.*

I would say the beginning of this movie sets up the basic premise of an archetypal addict's life prior to entering rehab fairly well. We've established that Gwen has a pattern of nonstop drinking or using, that she has a codependent relationship with her boyfriend (Dominick West plays a great alcoholic, see McNulty in [[the-wire|The Wire]]), and that the other loved ones in her life have been routinely hurt by attempting to manage their love for her when it comes with the consequences of addiction. Granted, there's still a slapstick element to the portrayal that an audience might laugh at, and Gwen's character is written to be unusually witty as the movie's protagonist, but the core ideas hold up. Both Gwen and her boyfriend say, on the way to Gwen's sister's wedding, that the whole point of their lifestyle is "to care less." The inherently self-destructive nature of addiction is articulated early on between these two sentiments: the point of Gwen's drinking and using is to care less, and as a result, she can't care about showing up for the people in her life and becomes impossible to love. Only her equally detached and destructive boyfriend is capable of following her rollercoaster. 

When Gwen ends up stealing a limo and crashing it into someone's house, we get a cutaway to her going to rehab. We infer (and shortly get confirmation) that she's court-ordered to attend rehab because the alternative was prison. 

*Well, you know, you never hear of anyone being raped with a plunger in rehab.*

*Well, no, you don't hear about it because they cover it up.*

Anecdotally, I have heard of instances of sexual harassment or assault happen in rehabs and treatment centers that went completely unaddressed by staff because it boiled down to "he said, she said," or some variation of that as per the genders involved. I've heard it happen between clients and between staff members and clients. Many people who come to treatment have already been victims of that exact kind of assault and abuse, and many failed to see justice come of it when reporting it through official channels. The instances where they were dealt with appropriately were usually cases where cameras in and out of houses were available to substantiate allegations, though you'll also have many people complain about the cameras themselves being invasions of privacy. I would cautiously guess that no set of rules or regulations can meaningfully prevent harassment or assault if someone is truly motivated to get away with it, so ultimately we need to tackle the issue through mechanisms found in socialization and cultural attitudes. The movie doesn't address this except for the throwaway joke I quoted above.

*We carry our own bags here. This isn't the Sheraton.*

I actually think a decent number of places help you out with carrying your luggage and belongings. A, because you're probably too high, drunk, and/or in agonizing withdrawals, and B, because they need to inspect it anyways to make sure you're not sneaking in contrabrand, including instruments that could be used to harm oneself or others (the "sharps"). For instance, Gwen's eyelash curler, mirror, and prescribed Vicodin for "back pain" are all thrown out.

Gwen's intake in general is smoother than most, which can involve an hour of questions and potentially taking blood samples or doing a TB test. Most centers will take a picture of you upon entry for both identification reasons in their computer systems and as a "Before" picture for you to compare to your "After" self. One of the most unrealistic parts of the movie so far is how great Gwen looks in this picture. 

*No phone calls, except during posted phone time and then only for 10 minutes.*

Phone policies vary a ton from facility to facility. Some places don't allow phone access at all unless it's a facility phone (in residential settings, a home phone) under supervision and for a specific duration like 10 minutes. Other facilities have a "blackout" period, generally around a week or more, in which someone has no phone access at all. This has a few functions: digital detoxing to break compulsive technology habits, preventing coordination with someone on the outside who might deliver drugs when someone is at their most vulnerable, and guiding clients to focus on their recovery instead of dissociating through [[doomscrolling|doomscrolling]].

*No fraternization. That's romance and/or sex between patients.*

Yeah, this one is just plain impossible to stop. If people want to fuck, they will find a way to fuck. I've known people to have sex at the center where classes and therapy sessions are held. I mean, you take a bunch of people severely deprived of dopamine who more likely than not have some type of trauma that hampered their ability to make healthy decisions and desperately seeking some validation and comfort from a life that was likely being burned to the ground alongside codependent relationships, and you're going to have people seeking intimacy through romance or sex right away. It's like a college dorm, except everyone is filled with severe dysfunction instead of parental expectations and career pressures.

*Oh, and we chant here. Don't be put off by it. It's just some people prefer it to the Serenity prayer.*

Boy oh boy do I have [[aa-is-religious|thoughts on this.]]

The Serenity prayer reads:

*God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, the courage to change the things I can, and the wisdom to know the difference*

Reinhold Niebuhr wrote it in the 1930s in the context of the Great Depression and fascist uprisings in World War II, particularly Nazi Germany. He intended it to bring solace to religious activists who participated in active, collective resistance to oppression. Niebuhr was a staunch, vocal critic of the Oxford Group and Frank Buchman, whose evangelism directly inspired the philosophy of Alcoholics Anonymous according to William Wilson (Bill W.) himself. For context, Buchman once stated "I thank heaven for a man like Adolf Hitler, who built a front line of defense against the anti-Christ of Communism." Niebuhr was, understandably, appalled at the idea that a utopia could be brought on Earth through the conversion of Hitler and the Nazi leadership to Christianity. He considered the notion of individual top-down transformation to be a backwards approach compared to collective action that allowed everyday people to pool their leverage together to overcome authoritarian structures, whether found in corporate bureaucracy or fascist totalitarianism. The Serenity prayer was intended for social justice activists, not for individuals with an ego problem to detach themselves further from accepting responsibility for the society around them. AA has certainly taken steps to distance itself from Buchmanism, such as appealing to social outcasts and destitute unemployed classes rather than totalitarian leadership and focusing on anonymity as rather than explicit cults of personality, but AA is fundamentally incompatible with collective political action. On top of a mandate to not have stances on "outside issues," AA philosophy more often than not encourages one to step back from attempting action if it can be rationalized as "out of one's control." It's a hyperindividualistic philosophy, though I do commend the anarchist structure of its localized groups.

Long story short, many people who attend AA or other 12-step meetings are wary of reciting the Serenity prayer, or any prayer for that matter. Many groups close out their meetings with the "Lord's Prayer", which is even more explicitly religious. Sometimes groups accommodate this by changing what their closing out "chant" is, but other times you just have to walk out if you're not comfortable.

*Your room. You're responsible for keeping it clean.*

This is definitely the general expectation you'll get from rehabs, although enforcement varies. Sometimes you'll have a roommate who eats in their room despite rules against it and leaves trash and dishes, lets piles of dirty laundry build up, and go a week without showering no matter how sweaty they get while trying to cover up their BO with AXE spray. I have come to appreciate strict and uptight staff members more than lax ones as a result. 

*Oh, um, what's your drug of choice? Alcohol, cocaine, Percodan, heroin, glue?*

*I have to pick just one?*

*Your gateway drug. What you go for first.*

*I guess I drink.*

You will have this conversation many, many, many times in recovery. And for good reason. As much as you'll hear that "we're all addicts, we're all trying to get better," there actually are differences between patterns of substance use. In my anecdotal experience, people whose drug of choice (DOC) is alcohol are the least likely to be marginalized, simply by the virtue of being the least likely to be in environments where other illicit substances are present. Even those who start drinking as teens usually stole from their parents or older friends, whereas those who start using harder drugs are more likely to have a social network involving in ongoing drug trafficking. I have never heard anyone say their DOC is nicotine, since people smoke like chimneys in recovery.

*I don't think she was staring, Daniel.*

*Oh? And how would you know, faggot? You're about as perceptive as Helen Keller.*

Recovery spaces are not the most progressive places, generally speaking. If you're anything like me and grew up in Call of Duty lobbies or the pre-filtered Internet that made it really easy for kids to find videos of ISIS beheadings, this might just go in one ear and out the other. Otherwise, you might end up with a little culture shock when you see that hardly anyone raises objections to that kind of language. Deeply opinionated bigotry is still jarring to me though, like when someone has clearly thought through their justifications to issue opinions about race or gender and proudly announces them.

*Don't worry about Daniel. He's just self-conscious about his neck because he did it to himself. He had this thing where he'd pump his stomach so he wouldn't get a hangover. l guess he knew how, because, you know, he's a doctor. Or was. He lost his license after he killed that patient, but.... Anyway, l guess one night while he was scraping that tube to his stomach...somehow he screwed up and he couldn't breathe and he had to...poke a hole in his throat with a pen or something.*

My entire [[goofy-shit|goofy shit]] page exists because you hear or experience stories like this all the time. Shoutout to the guy who told me about working in a lab as a tech with access to experimental horse tranquilizers whose co-worker/co-user gouged out his eyes on a bad trip.

This scene also gives us the first instance in which Gwen experiences something of a dizzy spell that accompanies withdrawals, marked by editing that slows down time and adds a white filter over the screen. It's not the best visual depiction of the disorientation, but it's alright for 2000s editing.

*Chocolate. Heroin addict.*

Gwen's roommate, a heroin addict, wakes up late at night to eat chocolate as a way of managing cravings. There's actually some science behind this. Heroin is an [[exogenous|exogenous]] opioid that binds to the brain's mu-opioid receptors intended for [[endogenous|endogenous]] opioids. Eating chocolate, particularly the high fats and sugar concentrates, prompts the brain to release enkephalins and endorphins that bind to the same mu-opioid receptors as heroin, which can provide relief to a brain suddenly starved of exogenous opioids to stimulate those neural pathways. Other substances like alcohol primarily operate on different neural pathways like GABA and glutamate, so while alcohol withdrawal can induce cravings for sweets as well, this stems from low blood sugar from lacking the simple carbohydrates of alcohol.

Also, I too am guilty of eating snacks loudly in the middle of the night. I'm sorry, but they shouldn't make the wrappers so fucking loud. I do my best.

*You girls are a lot of fun. And that's the most important thing. Because if you're not having fun, peanuts, what's the friggin' point?*

The way Gwen ends up sprawled on the bathroom floor, caught between vomiting in the toilet and tossing cold water at her face, is painfully familiar. The flashbacks to her childhood with her mother and sister, as well as her at a party with her boyfriend, are also typical in the first few days of withdrawal. 

With alcohol withdrawal, which is presumably the dominant type depicted on screen for us, the brain is suddenly deprived of the alcohol that mimics GABA (associated with calming neural activity) after becoming more sensitve to glutamate (associated with exciting neural activity). That means the central nervous system loses the ability to calm itself down while becoming hyper-excited, causing the amygdala to nonstop alert the brain that survival mode operations are necessary. One might repetitively manufacture worst-case scenarios or intense anxiety about the future. Simultaneously, the hippocampus has been conditioned by the suppression from alcohol to become more sensitive to glutamate as well, so the hyper-excitement results in disordered thoughts about the past constantly resurfacing, including trauma and feelings of intense shame.

Gwen's memories involve her mother as a clear influence on her own drinking habits. However, compare what she remembers now to what she said in the cab on the way to the wedding: her mother says there's no point to anything if it's not fun, but earlier Gwen said the point is to care less. Those aren't really the same thing, and in fact, we can see the thematic journey of Gwen's addiction already spelled out for us. At first, substance use can be fun and recreational, but as it becomes a chronic crutch that leads to dependence, it becomes a way to drown out an otherwise intolerable life in which caring about anything is too painful.

*You can keep your drugs and your whiskey sours. We got us a higher power! Yay, God!*

I don't know how this chant is preferable to the Serenity prayer. It's lame while still invoking the same religious context. I do appreciate that the movie doesn't pretend like a "Higher Power" isn't just obviously meant to be God though. Even AA official literature says that references to God should be seen as a shorthand for Higher Power, which just cements how interchangeable they're designed to be.

*There's no caffeine, it's mood-altering.*

This was unusual to see, in my experience. Every recovery meeting and facility I've ever taken note of provided caffeinated coffee, and some even allow energy drinks despite certain clients being definitively dependent on them to start their day. The line between acceptable and non-acceptable drugs that I'm most familiar with is "mind-altering" rather than "mood-altering," the distinction being that a mind-altering substance changes your actual cognitive processes while mood-altering substances just change a specific feeling. Marijuana is considered mind-altering, but nicotine and caffeine are considered mood-altering. Prescription drugs obviously span both, but are given an exception when taken through a medical recommendation. There are definitely arguments to be made that these distinctions are ultimately socially-constructed rather than empirically-justified.

*Lady, the people out there are addicts, okay? They need cigarettes like they need air.*

Case in point, I can count on one hand out of the hundreds of people in recovery I've met who actually stated they don't vape or smoke, not counting those quitting or attempting to quit.

*It works if you work it. It's worth it!*

The first group therapy session is just plainly funny to me for how relatable it is. I am often bored out of my fucking mind in most groups. There's always someone who wants to say something and takes an inordinate amount of time to articulate something simple, a facilitator whose uncanny positive regard for their patients leads to praise for the slightest effort to participate, and, for me, staring around the room studying literally anything and everything from the walls to people's shoes in the hopes of seeing something mildly stimulating. I know, that's mean, but at least I'm honest sometimes. They end the meeting on the normal Serenity prayer, which you might be inclined to write up as an inconsistency in the script since Gwen was told earlier that not everyone feels comfortable with it in the group chants, but I'm almost inclined to say that it's just true to how inconsistent the policies about prayers are in any particular recovery setting. You will almost certainly be asked to participate in a prayer in any environment that's fundamentally wrapped around AA philosophy, regardless of how secular it advertises itself.

*Hey, Gwen C! Your bed was not made up, your clothes were all over the place. I'm giving you an F for daily inspection and fining you a dollar.*

I've never seen monetary fines for failing to make up one's bed or clean one's room in a recovery context, probably because a lot of people in treatment are incredibly broke. Unfortunately there aren't too many disciplinary measures a facility can take if someone doesn't want to comply with guidelines around respect and civility. In general, I would warn the average person looking to enter a rehab that you shouldn't count on house managers or staff members to enforce ground rules because they really don't have too many hard power options to use. They function more as mediators than disciplinarians. If someone is facing jail time or prison time as an alternative to treatment thanks to a [[diversion|diversion program]], then being kicked out of the program can be a meaningful consequence, as is Gwen's case. Although, because this is a story with a protagonist who has to undergo an internal change that we can root for, this isn't the consequence that works for her.

*I'm not like those people out there. I can control myself. I can, if I... If that's... If I wanted to, I could, if that's what I wanted. I could. I can. I can!*

This is the stereotypical [[denial|denial]] phase that addicts are supposed to go through, and it's no surprise that it shows up in the movie as part of Gwen's character development. "I could stop any time I wanted" is a cliche as far as addiction goes. One of the reasons AA asks people to introduce themselves with the "I'm Mank, and I'm an alcoholic" format is to both work past denial and to build a sense of shared identity. 

In reality, a lot of addicts are perfectly aware that their substance use patterns are destructive and that they have a problem they can't stop on their own. What they might be ambivalent about is the merits of seeking treatment or admitting their problems to others out of fears related to stigma and judgment. Some people are simply too depressed to value themselves enough to think treatment is worth pursuing. Others don't think it would help anyways, especially if they come from families where someone suffered from their substance use without ever seeking help. Something something quiet desperation. And there are those who simply don't want to stop. Whatever your opinion of that may be, some people have simply crunched the numbers and decided they prefer not being sober despite the tradeoffs. But, that's not as narratively gratifying for a general audience.

*If that'll make you happy, I will stop drinking. And then, I would tell myself, "tonight, I will not get wasted." And then something would happen. Or nothing would happen. And, I'd get that feeling, and you all know what that feeling is, when your skin is screaming and your hands are shaking, and your stomach feels like it wants to jump through your throat. And you know that if anyone had a clue how wrong it felt to be sober, they wouldn't dream of asking you to stay that way. They would say "Oh, jeez, I didn't know. Here. You know, it's... It's okay for you. You do that mound of cocaine. Have a drink. Have 20 drinks. Whatever... Whatever you need to do to feel like a normal human being, you do it."*

God bless Steve Buscemi. This monologue is highly referenced by people who like this movie as striking a particular chord with them. It really is impossible to explain the craving and compulsions of addiction to someone who hasn't had it. You can tell them to imagine being dehydrated in a desert for 5 days and being offered a glass of water if they run over a stranger, or you can tell them to imagine the worst case of poison ivy they ever felt ravaging their body with the need to itch, or any of the other analogies, but none of them really describe how much more intense and holistic the actual thing is. I can't call it insanity, because the impulse is so strong that, as Buscemi's character says, if you knew it, you completely understand why someone would give into it, and how miserable of an experience it can feel to not give into it. You know that people who haven't experienced it lead their everyday lives falling into temptations that aren't even a hundredth as strong as the one to drink or use: ruining their diet for Oreos, speeding, gossipping, sleeping in and calling sick to work, snapping at their family, bingeing a show on a streaming platform, doomscrolling social media, unsafe sex with a toxic ex, dropping a racial slur, all those other little bad habits that should be trivial to stop if your life was on the line, though it virtually never is. You know that people can scrape by with manageable consequences for those kinds of things, and it won't ruin their life and people will sympathize with those flaws, but you will be judged for the inability to hold back from something stronger than they've ever experienced. It will never be fair, but that is what it is. You have to be able to find a reason to live in spite of that, and unfortunately, that won't happen for a lot of people. We don't live in a world that makes that possible for everyone. For all the elaboration on this blog about recovery and behavioral interventions rooted in meaningful incentives, I think George Carlin said it best: you need people around you who care, and you need something to look forward to.
