# Overview
The Nelson Bradley Career Achievement Award was established in 1983 by the National Association of Addiction Treatment Providers (NAATP) as a lifetime achievement award. 

Nelson J. Bradley was a psychiatrist who served as the superintendent of Willmar State Hospital in Minnesota, pioneering an approach that incorporated 12-Step principles and peer counselors into treatment facilities, now called the Minnesota Model or Twelve-Step Facilitation (TSF). A 2020 Cochrane Systematic Review analyzed 27 studies of 10,000 participants and found that manualized TSF programs inspired by the Minnesota Model are more effective for sustained long-term abstinence than clinical interventions like Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT). 

Bradley was instrumental in the scientific community's shift to seeing addiction as a chronic brain disease than a moral or willpower problem, though other researchers have argued that the brain disease model has pathologized and medicalized an issue rooted in socioeconomic systems. 

Others argue that the main value of the Minnesota Model is peer support, which increases recovery capital by introducing people to mutual aid societies that share emotional support, but tangible support as well. Navigating healthcare, employment, housing, culture, and other aspects of daily life are made significantly easier through supportive community networks, which clinical or therapeutic interventions are not necessarily equipped to provide or substitute for. Alternative systems like the Community Reinforcement Approach (CRA) consistently outperform TSF models in reducing drinking, securing employment, and stabilizing households over time, suggesting that the medical intervention aspects of the Minnesota Model (28-days in a treatment facility) are not the real driver of improvement. 

There is also substantial criticism that complex cases with co-occurring psychiatric illnesses, such as complex PTSD which the DSM-5 does not list despite up to 40% of individuals with substance use disorders testing positive for trauma-related disorders, are either excluded from the studies or are addressed with far worse results than someone solely struggling with substance use.

Despite these caveats, Bradley's influence on the field was substantial, and the Minnesota Model remains the most common base for treatment formats used in the United States. Bradley's legacy did a lot of work to destigmatize addiction away from the untreatable death sentence that medical professionals assumed it was. We can discuss the flaws of the Minnesota Model as a particular protocol or treatment with the advantage of new evidence and hindsight, but the man himself made major changes in the way addiction was perceived by the scientific and medical communities.
