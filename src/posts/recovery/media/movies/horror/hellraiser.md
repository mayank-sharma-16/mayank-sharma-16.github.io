*"The doors to the pleasure of Heaven or Hell. I didn't care which... I thought I'd gone to the limits. I hadn't. The Cenobites gave me an experience beyond limits... pain and pleasure, indivisible."*

The original Hellraiser movie is comfortably a contender for one of my favorite movies of all time. The sequels have been not as good, to say the least, including one movie clearly made to extend the IP rights. 

In the 2022 sequel, the protagonist is overtly struggling with addiction as part of the plot, but I still feel the original was far better at tapping into the raw themes underlying addiction.

One of the most confusing aspects of addiction to people who have never abused substances or struggled with compulsions is how someone can keep using despite the pain outweighing the pleasure. It's actually confusing for most addicts who attempt recovery too, which often leads to the conclusion that one must simply be incapable of valuing anything over the addictive stimulus. The quotes above from Frank Cotton, the real antagonist driving the events of the film, reveal some of this thinking in a visual narrative that's helpful to dissect.

# Frank
Frank is shown to be a hedonistic thrill-seeker who has no regard for norms or rules. He travels the world in search of taboo pleasures beyond what everyday life could provide, obviously including drugs and sex. He has an affair with his brother Larry's wife, Julia, which introduces the elements of Frank's debauchery into the family. Kirsty, Larry's daughter, is our primary protagonist as a stereotypical coming-of-age woman seeking to carve out an independent life for herself.

In the book version, Frank's hedonism burns through the capacity for normal life, and he becomes jealous of Larry's ability to be fulfilled in his routine ordinary life with a family. In the movie version, we see Frank put on Larry's skin after a murder as a disguise, but the context from the book adds another dimension to Frank becoming Larry and having an affair with Larry's wife. He wants to be his brother, and he horrifically attempts to literally enact the substitution.

From this perspective, the addiction analogy seems natural. When Frank says he doesn't care whether he found heaven or hell, he's reached the point that many addicts do where nothing on Earth seems to satisfy the desire for instant intense gratification. 

The Lament Configuration that he finds on his travels to Morocco has the same promise of exotic transcendentalism that give substances their allure. Talk To Me plays with a similar concept by treating its hand as something ancient and traveled. The Lament Configuration is a puzzle box which someone must activate intentionally before knowing what the outcome can be, mimicking the ritual preparation that goes into using substances alongside the risk of something horrible happening after. The way Frank brings the box back to the old home Larry, Julia, and their daughter Kirsty move into isn't very different than the way drugs can cross borders to end up in the hands of everyday people. 

Kirsty is the protagonist of the film, a teenager on the threshold of exploring adulthood. She's beginning to explore sexuality with her boyfriend at the same time that she encounters Frank and the Lament Configuration, which we can read as experimenting with drugs she stumbled upon. No peer pressure, no crazy partying, just the all-too-common story of kids  finding an adult's stash and applying their basic human curiosity. Even though she was absentmindedly playing with the Configuration, she still activates it to summon the Cenobites. Rather than dragging her away though, she has the presence of mind to cut a deal that would lead the Cenobites to Frank, their escapee. 

The Cenobites are a great concept that stands out in an era were villains like Freddy Krueger and Jason Vorhees became breakout icons of the horror genre. Fun fact, Clive Barker was inspired by his visits to underground BDSM clubs for the movie's costuming choices, so if your instinct upon seeing them was to scream "BDSM DEMONS", you're definitely not alone. Well, as the iconic Cenobite Pinhead says: "Demons to some, angels to others."
