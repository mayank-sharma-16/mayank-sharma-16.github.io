Whenever people ask me if I have a study or work habit I can share with them, I confess I have a horribly erratic work pattern that waits for inspiration to strike. There's no secret to it. I'm just very introverted, very obsessive, and very dissociated. If you put a gun to my head and forced me to come up with something, I'd end up vaguely gesturing to the idea of meta-learning. There's no gun to my head, but it's only a matter of time so I'll do it anyways.

Meta-learning is what it sounds like: learning about learning. 

First, education isn't studying isn't learning isn't rationality isn't intelligence.

Education is a form of infrastructure that distributes intellectual capital. An apprenticeship is a form of education in which you learn by modeling an expert and iterating with your personal intuition. A school is an educational institution that gives you a claim to some experience in studying what you're told with discipline over a prolonged period of time. A bookshelf can be a means to education. It's an external thing that you can use, and after a certain amount, some societies will label you "educated" or some equivalent construct.

Studying is the process of taking information from the outside world and running it through your brain. You can study a painting for hours without learning anything. You can study a textbook ten times and not commit anything to memory or understanding. You can study religious scripture without becoming any better of a person if you don't learn to apply it properly. There are lots of tricks if your goal is to study for a grade or a test so you can promptly forget everything after. Some people are really good at studying situations to figure out what advice to give others, but they haven't learned to actually navigate that situation themselves. There are lots of cases where a brilliant therapist is described as a horrible parent or spouse. 

A relevant concept for this is *processing fluency.* I have despicable processing fluency. It takes me a really long time to process information even when I'm interested in it. If you tell me you're going to ask a question after we read a section or watch a video, I will grab a sentence from the first 30 seconds and spend the rest of my time figuring out what to say about that part. If you tell me that you intend to quiz me on a set of materials once a week for a year, I will immediately set all my curiosity in the subject aside in order to see the studying I have to do to pass assessments. They're not assessing how well you study or learn, they're assessing how much you failed to study for of a predetermined set of topics, which is why discipline (and the resources to prepare) almost always beats raw intelligence and genuine learning in standardized systems.

Learning is the process of actually committing something to your memory and understanding for access later. In order to learn, I have to figure out where my knowledge gaps are or what assumptions I haven't tested in a while. That's not a process you can standardize, not even with project-based learning that you assess with standardized criteria. You don't know what you don't know. Sometimes it can take a while to even describe a problem you can feel, but it helps to begin looking at the patterns within the problems themselves. For example, I currently think the problem of recovery relies on the problem of modeling addiction, and the problem of modeling addiction relies on having a theory of suffering derived from a theory of the human condition. 

AA indicates suffering comes from an unwillingness to be honest about one's character defects and worship a Higher Power for guidance, strength, and forgiveness. Recovery Dharma argues suffering comes from attachment, and the cravings of addiction represent powerful attachments which take sustained commitment and community to lift oneself out of. Shaivist texts propose that the root of suffering is losing oneself in a finite ego instead of recognition of the infinite reality that all finite concepts are manifested from. Ancient Mesoamerican cultures like the Aztecs had the concept of "rootedness" to have a firm foundation against falling into extremes in any direction and suffering as a result. Reinhold Niebuhr, author of the Serenity Prayer, was at one point a pro-union Socialist Party leader who coordinated with Martin Luther King Jr. as part of a mandate to apply Christian virtues to social justice against manmade suffering on Earth. A secular materialist might just say that's all abstract philosophy and theology that makes for a fun intellectual exercise, but suffering is really just caused by our nervous systems sending out neurotransmitters that we register as pain which is sometimes useful as information for our overall survival.

I can't say I know much about any of those frameworks in particular depth, but seeing how they each tackle a theory of suffering sets up good starting points for further exploration, especially in terms of what recovery might look like with each model's approach.

Rationality is a quality that enables and improves learning, but a very rational person could also fail to learn enough to arrive at a solution to their problems. People can learn to be more rational, or at least identify patterns of irrationality that they can then address, but we're also going to run into lots of problems defining rationality. Is believing in God a belief that should be evaluated on the basis of rationality? What about egalitarianism or the sanctity of life? There are lots of beliefs that are unfalsifiable, non-rational (distinct from irrational) but held so closely by people that they will walk right out of a room if you attempt to negotiate them. I will walk out of any room that comes to the conclusion *"attempting to change the conditions and circumstances of your life through self-empowerment and collective action is irrational."* In my opinion, that's rhetorical cover for authoritarianism and abusers. 

Niebuhr, a devout Christian in contrast to my radical skeptic, has been more forceful than I could ever be on the subject: 

*The increasingly obvious fascist philosophy which informs the group movement is in other words not only socially vicious but religiously vapid. The slightest acquaintance with the history of Christian thought on the problem of the relation of the absolute demands of the gospel to the relativities of politics and economics would prove its childishness. A careful study of the gospel itself, particularly its abhorrence of the self-righteousness of the righteous, would reveal the danger of any doctrine which promises powerful men the possibility of fully doing the will of God. They had better be admonished that after they have done what they think right they will still remain unprofitable servants.*
