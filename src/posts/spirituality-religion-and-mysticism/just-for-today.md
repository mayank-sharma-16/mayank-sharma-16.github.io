# Just For Today
Just for Today describes itself as a daily meditation book that's derived from the literature and practices of Narcotics Anonymous, which is derived from Alcoholics Anonymous, which is derived from the Oxford Group, which is derived from Higher Life Theology in English Protestant tent revivalist movements. I am very adamant about the opinion of AA being religious, not just spiritual, and you can find a discussion about that here. And that's okay. 

I've said numerous times before that religion is absolutely a valid part of an individual and community's recovery capital, and there are undoubtedly people who would not have noticed any changes to their sobriety or mental health or quality of life without it. I have no intentions of talking someone out of a religion or into one. I just think we need to be honest when things are religious so we don't blame people when they don't fit into a one-size-fits-all solution. After all, the AA/NA 12 Step programs recite literature every meeting that says those who don't stay sober through the program are "constitutionally incapable" of having the prerequisite virtues to do so. If I am ever forceful or critical in my tone, it is solely because I think a claim that strong has to be met with a strong repudiation. It's one of the reasons I'm extremely critical of *The Four Agreements* as well, so there's nothing about AA or Christianity in particular which gives me an axe to grind. 

Actually, I'm a big fan of Jesus, especially on the evils of hoarding wealth and marginalizing the outcasts who are already suffering. I have no doubt that Jesus wanted us to embrace homeless addicts alongside lepers and prostitutes. Benjamin Rush, one of the earliest doctors to propose a disease model, wrote that a good Christian would not take those afflicted with chronic drunkenness differently than other sinners who were lost. I'm also a big fan of Ronald Niebuhr, who wrote a collectivized version of the Serenity Prayer from which the individualist Serenity Prayer comes and had some very interesting thoughts on international relations. Hindu belief systems tend to err on the side of syncretic coexistence with other systems so that it's very easy to take what works and leave what doesn't while respecting people for their choices.

So in no way do I think Just For Today is bad or unhelpful. I just think it's religious, and that's okay. One of the easiest ways to illustrate this point is by focusing in on how often the daily meditation actually ends up being a prayer to a personal Higher Power who must be loving, caring, and powerful enough to be the cause of all things within an independent higher reality. If we take the suggestion that the Higher Power can really be anything of your choice, then let's see what happens when we directly substitute a few of those into the text. I'm incrementally working through this book, but in the process, I'll try substituting a doorknob, the abstract concept of love, an impersonal God who doesn't intervene in daily life after setting the universe in motion, and Satan. None of those describe my own beliefs.

## January 1
*We have a daily reprieve from our addiction as long as we remain vigilant. Each day, we carry the principles of recovery into all we do, and each night, we thank our Higher Power for another day clean.*

If we look at the origins of the term "daily reprieve" in the Big Book, we see:

*What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition. Every day is a day when we must carry the vision of God’s will into all of our activities.*

By definition, a "reprieve" is the act of delaying a punishment, such as a government offering a reprieve in a death penalty case so that it may be considered further. A "daily reprieve" thus means having a daily delay in your punishment. This is a morally loaded proposition obviously; not everyone's belief system begins with the idea that a disease is punishment. It's possible to be born with an addiction or develop one as a child because of an adult supplying substances. Nearly half the people diagnosed with a substance use disorder also have another mental disorder which might prompt us to ask how much of the compulsive use is about self-medication. 

It might be normal, if not necessarily healthy, for an individual to suspect that they specifically suffered from PTSD because of a situation they provoked from the universe like karma or punishment for sin. It would be quite strange though if our baseline for handling PTSD involved telling people who were in a car crash or witnessed a tragic death that they need to pray to a Higher Power everyday to have their punishment of a flashback delayed. We might want to consider that your health is in part a function of your environment, that you don't have infinite capacity to change your environment, that you don't even have infinite capacity to change your mind/body/soul or however you model human existence, and so adding a moral component encourages blaming the individual rather than evaluating systems-based and evidence-backed solutions. 

But say we accept the moralization of health issues. How do you thank a doorknob for giving you another day of sobriety? How do you pray to the abstract concept of love, is that like praying to Cupid to be struck by love whenever your depressive episodes worsen and drinking seems like an escape? How do you ask for a daily reprieve from an impersonal God that doesn't intervene in daily life? What is the vision of the will of an entity that has no will for intervention? We could charitably say that the form matters rather than the substance here. The act of imagining a Higher Power might be enough to reap the benefits of feeling connected to a bigger picture or having a supportive relationship. Evidence certainly suggests a correlation between believing in a Higher Power and reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression. As we'll see though, the Higher Power or God you're asked to pick has very restrictive constraints that will rule out most ideas about spirituality and religion.

## January 2
*"Take a deep breath and talk to God*

*"Sometimes when we pray, a remarkable thing happens: We find the means, ways, and energies to perform tasks far beyond our capacities."*

*Each time life presents us with another little setback to our daily plans, we can simply take a deep breath and talk to the God of our understanding. Knowing we can draw patience, tolerance, or whatever we need from that Power, we find ourselves coping better and smiling more often.*

*Just for today: I will take a deep breath and talk to my God whenever I feel frustrated.*

This one is even more difficult to imagine doing with a doorknob, an abstract concept, or an impersonal God. You can't talk to any of those with an expectation that anything in your life will change without entering the world of magical thinking or spiritual bypassing. The doorknob is not going to give you the Power to surpass your finite bandwidth to make decisions in your life. It only makes sense to ask for "means, ways, and energies to perform tasks far beyond our capacities" if you're implying that God will reply by granting you Power you don't currently have. 

You could talk to Satan as a loving and caring Father, but I'm going to guess 99% of you reading this are feeling a little uncomfortable at that idea, especially if you've come to describe alcohol (cunning, baffling, and powerful) as a temptation of the Devil. Carl Jung certainly used that framing, and it makes sense to me. I too would think someone who mentioned Satan as their Higher Power in a church might be ruffling feathers substantially. Anecdotally, those in recovery I've met with Satanic tattoos are quickly ostracized by devout Christians even if they're willing to work the rest of the program. I would think it's a good practical decision for there to be a different 12 Step formulation for someone who considers themselves a Satanist, although many Satanists might argue that the entire premise of their beliefs is a rejection of the hierarchy formed by a distant Higher Power.

## January 3
*"We eventually redefine our beliefs and understanding to the point where we see that our greatest need is for knowledge of God's will for us and the strength to carry that out."*

*In our God's will we find freedom from self-will.*

*I will seek the fulfillment of my greatest need: a vital, guiding connection with the God of my understanding.*

This seems like a very particular philosophical and theological position on free will in relation to God. But the doorknob has no will for you. An impersonal God might have a will for you if we accept that God set a universe in motion knowing what would happen after as time goes on, but that still doesn't mean you're going to establish anything like a "guiding connection" that would create instructions you need to carry out. 

It also might just not be your greatest need. It is possible to be spiritually fulfilled but lack one of the many other factors that go into recovery capital. A devout Christian who spends hours meditating on God's glory and grace might still be homeless and struggle to get medication. Giving that person safe and stable housing would substantially improve their lives and even their ability to pray in the way they want. Giving that person a copy of Just For Today or The Big Book, probably less so. They might read the whole book thinking "I've done all this, but when do I get off the streets and into a doctor's office as a result of living according to these spiritual principles?" 

## January 4
*In the company of other addicts, we knew we would not be rejected.*

This one isn't directly about religion, but I'd like to talk about it anyways. This is not true enough of the time. There is absolutely nothing about a fellowship of addiction that avoids the pitfalls every human has in terms of their assumptions, biases, prejudices, first impressions, trauma responses, impulsive icks, beliefs about incompatible beliefs, and other reasons to discriminate between people. 

You might meet people who are only friendly because they're hitting on you to get to the infamous 13th Step. You might have sponsors or sponsees who ghost you without relapsing. You might hear people tell a whole room they do not believe you will get sober without accepting God, or that anyone who doesn't accept God is either scared, egoistic, or lost in sin. You might have members of the group who are very vocal about their political opinions concerning diversity, to put it gently. 

Someone in AA with over a decade of sobriety once gave me this warning. I insisted everyone I knew had been nice to me even if we had different backgrounds, and that has still been true 90% of the time. I'm still glad to have gotten the heads-up about the other 10% of the time, so I feel compelled to share the same warning I got. You are not necessarily the problem if a cohort, speaker, or prominent member doesn't like you or your approach to recovery. You can find lots of support groups for substance use and other mental health disorders that will work with the approach that fits you best, so don't give up and don't internalize any negativity coming your way.

## January 5
I actually like this one as is. It's just about how internal change is not necessarily obvious to those who have seen you in active addiction for prolonged periods of time. It may take time for them to see you've been able to address the disease head-on and that you have plans in case of a relapse so that the spirals of the past are far less likely to happen. I would just add that some relationships will never come back with the same trust and intimacy, and it's good to be prepared for that scenario so that you don't put all your life's eggs in a single basket. The concept of living amends is a powerful one that can keep you feeling connected to someone you can't make actual amends with anymore. What would they imagine you would be doing if you really changed?

It's also possible you realize burning some bridges was among the best things to ever happen to you. We are incredibly susceptible to the influence of those around us, so much so that some studies suggest individual characteristics are a poor predictor of sustained abstinence compared to the overall recovery capital in the community that individual is in, or even the presence of a couple highly supportive relationships. We tend not to take the bold risk of leaving a social network or relationship to be single free-floating atoms because humans are social creatures. Isolation and exile are strongly associated with death or at least a high likelihood of harm with no one to help. At the same time, we may not realize how toxic and limiting certain communities or networks can be to our overall health and potential. 

This doesn't mean you won't mourn or grieve for what should've, could've, would've been. It just means the grass might actually be greener on the other side, especially if you go at it with intention, awareness, skills, and capital. You can redefine yourself in a way that makes you comfortable in your skin and find others who like that version of you. It sucks, but it's also a harsh truth that people sometimes act like crabs in a bucket if someone succeeds in improving their life. One of the greatest credits I can give to the Higher Power methods is that they do provide an avenue out of those buckets.

## January 6
*They no longer ask "why"; they ask "how." The value of endless speculation pales in comparison to the experience of addicts who've found a way to stay clean and live clean.*

This one is more subtle.

St. Augustine wrote *crede ut intelligas* which translates roughly to "believe to understand," and Anselm of Canterbury wrote *fides quaerens intellectum* which maps to "faith seeking understanding." They argued you first had to take a leap of faith in God in order to engage in reason, because otherwise the faculties of reason would be disordered by sin. There are some very lengthy and engaging arguments to this conclusion that I think are absolutely worth reading and discussing, but my main point is that the process of trusting something works on the basis of another's testimony to a spiritual awakening without knowing how it works has the residue of a particular faith-based epistemology. It's not even irrational; it makes a lot of sense that you have to first achieve sanity before you can apply it to your life, and if a group of people are telling you that they understood the process after taking that step, it makes a lot of sense to follow them on that path. Someone suffering from a disorder that produces delusions might need medication before they can understand they need medication. But remember that AA asks you to carry its principles in your daily affairs, so you might hear "trust the process" outside of the context of an AA meeting very often in the recovery space. 

I personally believe in "trust but verify." Trust that something is worth trying if it's helped others, and verify that the outcomes match the personal criteria for success. I can trust that a new medication prescribed by a professional might help reduce symptoms of a health condition. I can verify it does by taking it, and if it doesn't, I can return to the professional and we can iterate further. I have trusted that trying the Steps would be a worthwhile endeavor. I verified that the concepts of Powerlessness, surrender, higher independent reality, an etiology from morality, and spiritual awakening are at best tangential and at worst incompatible for my specific religious and spiritual practices.

In a "faith seeking understanding" or "trust the process" model, the most readily apparent solution might be to have more faith, more trust, and more willpower to try a similar set of approaches again. You can always rationalize that you were still operating on too much self-will. Until you've achieved a long period of sobriety, you might be told to follow in the footsteps of others who succeeded ("stick to the winners"), but of course there's a survivorship bias there. People who recovered without AA are not going to be in AA meetings and probably won't be advertising their substance abuse outside of AA either. People who died while working the steps are also not going to be in AA meetings to discuss their experiences. 

That's partly why some groups like Recovery Dharma embrace the spirit of inquiry:

From the Recovery Dharma text: *The Buddha did not ask us to believe his teachings on faith alone. He encouraged us to investigate them through our own experience* and *We encourage everyone to take what is useful and leave the rest.*

From the Kalama Sutta: *Do not go by reports, by legends, by traditions, by scripture, by logical conjecture, by inference, by analogies, by agreement through pondering views, by probability, or by the thought, 'This contemplative is our teacher.*

This is decidedly not the tone or approach taken in AA/NA and the accompanying literature. It's radical in the opposite direction: trust nothing, consider everything. Lean on your own understanding. It's even more radical than SMART Recovery, which puts considerable trust in medical and scientific institutions to generate the evidence for the evidence-backed approach and thereby has narrower methods within which to contest a finding (and there are plenty of criticisms of "evidence-backed" as a marketing term from researchers inside the field criticizing other researchers). At least SMART Recovery challenges their own approaches regularly while looking at the latest research behind harm-reduction, somatic experiencing, social stigma, etc. None of these mean that you should refrain from acting until you've understood the programs completely, but it does mean that you shouldn't wait to ask questions. Inquire while you practice.

## January 7

*Many of us suffered severely from physical, sexual, and emotional abuse. Getting high and staying high seemed like the only possible way to cope with such abuse. Others suffered in less noticeable but equally painful ways before addiction took hold. We lacked direction and purpose. We were spiritually empty. We felt isolated, unable to empathize with others. We had none of the things that give life its sense and value.*

*And we've been given the gift of conscious contact with a Power greater than ourselves, providing us with the inner strength and direction we so sorely lacked in the past.*

I find it a little alarming that we would attach so many moral and spiritual qualities onto an abstract archetype of a victim of abuse. We emphasize using "I-statements" in meetings so much that saying a paragraph like this in one would warrant a chairperson or facilitator cutting you off. If you've been through any trauma, you might feel isolated, detached, and stuck afterwards. If you've been through an extended period of trauma or even a lifetime of it, you might really feel those things and more. But "unable to empathize with others... had none of the things that give life its sense and value?" In my experience, those who have PTSD or C-PTSD as a result of abuse are far more hypervigilant about how others feel than those without that diagnosis, and those who have been in long-term abusive dynamics are far more likely to end up in people-pleasing or co-dependent dynamics where their empathy and desire for conflict resolution are taken advantage of. They are often very grateful for small comforts. Someone in active addiction can certainly lose access to their empathy and prioritize substance use over other things (that's definitionally what "substance use disorder" means, the brain thinks a substance is as critical for survival as water in a desert), but I would not go as far as to generalize that to all who have been abused even outside of active addiction. 

## January 8
*Our best measure of growth is our spiritual condition, the basis of our recovery. If we're still depending on people, places, and things to provide our inner satisfaction, like a child depending on its parents for everything, we do indeed have some growing to do. But if we stand secure on the foundation of our spiritual condition, considering its maintenance our most important responsibility, we can claim maturity.*

If you're promoting spirituality, that's one thing. If you're claiming spirituality is a priority to be measured alongside your adherence to principles that stem from a Higher Power's care and direction and will, you're promoting religion. Note that lots of religions are actually very prescriptive about how you relate to people, places, and things. You might have sacred spaces with special significance, and you might have obligations that relate to the community and society you're living in. Consider death rites. In many religions, your spiritual health and passage to the afterlife or next life is contingent on having a community that does certain rituals for you after your death. If you interpreted "depending on people, places, and things" to mean "I will drink unless I get to visit Disneyland this year," then yes, you're being childish. 

Remember again though, the Buchmanism behind the Oxford Group and AA principles was intended to convert powerful individuals who already had access to all the material things in existence. Henry Ford might be able to agree that more people, places, and things would just yield diminishing marginal returns for his quality of life. Modern addiction researchers are pretty clear that community recovery capital has more predictive power for an individual's sustained abstinence than individual recovery capital. In other words, you absolutely need other people, places, and things to achieve any kind of security and fellowship. You're also responsible for seeking out those things to the best of your ability, but obviously there is never a guarantee that you're going to have access to what you need. Your doorknob may not be the consistent provider of your necessities that you would want out of a Higher Power.

## January 9
*My sponsor has cared for me when I couldn't care for myself. Today, I will do something nice for my sponsor.*

While this *can* certainly be true and it *can* be an excellent aspect of your recovery, as the recovery capital framework suggests that one or two highly supportive relationships can matter more than any particular individual trait, there are also many ways it can go wrong. This one doesn't have to do anything with religion directly, but there's still advice that should be shared with newcomers here.

Sponsors are, by design, not professionals or trained in any capacity by an institution we can hold accountable or publishes standards to uphold. The advice they give for dealing with trauma, relationships, distress, cravings, spirituality and religion, and anything else that's part of recovery is not professional advice. That means that the advice you get might be wildly contradictory to what a trained medical professional gives you, and there's no real accountability for a sponsor who gives harmful suggestions or acts inappropriately. The only thing you know for sure before choosing a sponsor is that you have one health condition in common. There's no guarantee they'll empathize with anxiety, depression, PTSD, bipolar disorder, ADHD, DID, or any of the other health conditions that people often discover for the first time when seeking treatment. And if they do, there's no guarantee they'll understand how to help you through it. 

One particular pattern to be mindful of is spiritual bypassing, in which a sponsor, or anyone for that matter, might offer a purely spiritual explanation for a problem in a way that can't ever be falsified. Examples of spiritual bypassing include telling someone working through a loss that "the universe works in mysterious ways" or "it's all part of God's plan" or "you manifest from the energy you choose to have" without engaging with the messy complexity of grief and trauma. This isn't actually specific to AA, sponsors, or recovery. Much of this comes from a hyperindividualistic culture that dismisses environmental factors in an individual's outcomes.

Be careful in choosing a sponsor if that's the path you take (or a mentor if you're from Recovery Dharma or Refuge Recovery, as the same exact warnings apply), and it's okay if you decide you'd like another one. Remember that the Steps don't actually require a sponsor, so you're not constitutionally incapable of honesty just because you feel you can do this with the fellowship as a whole, the supportive network you already have, and therapists or doctors who can assess your health holistically. 

## January 10
*Belief in a Higher Power can make all the difference when the going gets tough! When things don't go our way in recovery, our sponsor may direct us to make a "gratitude list." When we do, we should include our faith in a Power greater than ourselves on the list. One of the greatest gifts we receive from the Twelve Steps is our belief in a God of our own understanding.*

*The Twelve Steps gently lead us toward a spiritual awakening.*

*The steps are our path to a relationship with a God of our understanding. This Higher Power gives us strength when our road gets rough.*

*Are we grateful for our deepening relationship with a Higher Power? Do we remember to thank God for each day clean, no matter what has happened that day? Do we remember that, no matter how deep our despair or how great our joy, the God of our understanding is with us?  Our recovery is a gift, a gift that we sometimes take for granted. Each day we stay clean, we can rejoice in our Higher Power's care.  Just for today: I am grateful for my relationship with a Higher Power that cares for me.*

Notice that the belief in God is actually a gift from doing the 12 Steps, not an ingredient in doing them. This is not a BYO invitation for Higher Powers. We repeatedly see that the book suggests the 12 Steps is a way to meet a Higher Power and that we must convert our Higher Power into a loving and caring one if they don't fit the 12 Step structure. 

Like you can't just add "of our understanding" after the word "God" like a spiritual loophole when you repeatedly shoot down other people's completely valid understandings. That's really no different than saying "I'm going to buy a bunch of drugs... in Minecraft" or Buchman claiming he had the right to alter the understanding of God's will that others in the Oxford Group achieved when they claimed they made conscious contact. There's a very particular Higher Power and God that fits the mold here.

## January 11
*"As we develop faith in our daily lives, we find that our Higher Power supplies us with the strength and guidance that we need."  Basic Text, p. 91*

*The seed is planted when we ask our Higher Power for help and then acknowledge the source of our help when it comes. We nurture the tiny seed of faith with the sunlight of our prayers each day. Our faith grows, a reward for living life on its own terms. One day we realize our faith has become like a huge spreading tree; it doesn't stop the storms of life, but we know that we are safe in its shelter. Just for today: I know that faith in my Higher Power will not calm the storms of life, but it will calm my heart. I will let my faith shelter me in times of trouble*

We're very explicitly leaning into the "faith seeking understanding" angle here. This is extremely heavy-handed about faith and "acknowledging the source of our help when it comes" as it upgrades the concept of the Higher Power to also be all-powerful, such that it governs the universe and is the source of all helpful things in your life. I shouldn't have to mention that your doorknob and abstract concepts like love don't meet this requirement. No, not even a "love holds the universe together" Hallmark card way.  

## January 12
*"Having had a spiritual awakening as a result of these steps..."  Step Twelve*

*"How will I know when I have had a spiritual awakening?" For many of us, a spiritual awakening comes gradually. Perhaps our first spiritual awareness is as simple as a new appreciation for life. Maybe one day we'll suddenly discover the sound of birds singing early in the morning. The simple beauty of a flower may remind us that there is a Power greater than ourselves at work around us.*

*Often, our spiritual awakening is something that grows stronger over time. We can strive for more spiritual awareness simply by living our lives. We can persist in efforts to improve our conscious contact through prayer and meditation on a daily basis. We can listen within for the guidance we need. We can question other addicts about their experiences with spirituality. We can take time to appreciate the world around us.*

*Just for today: I will reflect on the spiritual awakenings I have experienced. I will strive to be God-conscious. I will take time out in the day to appreciate my Higher Power's handiwork.*

What's cool about this part is that it actually didn't answer the question at all. The concepts of mindfulness and gratitude are in there, but you can experience those without a spiritual awakening or being "God-conscious." This isn't a new problem either. Buchman's idea of conscious contact with God would involve things like claiming God told him he could only pay a certain amount for a particular service. He would adjust the instructions others had written during their attempts to make conscious contact with God. 

Also, "handiwork" gives away that this Higher Power is synonymous with Creator or Maker. Is your doorknob creating any handiwork to admire lately?
