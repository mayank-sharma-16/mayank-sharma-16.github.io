# Being Religious is Okay
AA is religious, and that's okay.

Before I go into my own thoughts, I'm going to bring up the findings and analysis of actual court cases that made it through the American legal system. They already flesh out a pretty thorough rebuttal of the idea that AA is neutral or just spiritual rather than religious, and you can rest assured that this perspective has been validated by more credible sources than me.

In *Warner v. Orange County (1996)*, the 2nd Circuit Court ruled that even if it was possible for every person to substitute God with a definition consistent with their own beliefs, holding hands in a prayer circle to recite a passage from the New Testament attributed to Jesus Christ unquestionably made it a Christian program. Standing silently in the circle is still considered a form of coercion into a fellowship that does not align with someone's sincerely held beliefs, even if they're an atheist who considers the prayer inconsequential. Interestingly, there was a dissent in a remand in 1999 that claimed attendance of a few meetings waives one's right to object to it; the majority disagreed that AA was forthcoming enough about its religious nature for someone to consent to it even after attendance.

In *Griffin v. Coughlin (1999)*, the specific argument about replacing God with a Higher Power, or that AA is spiritual but not religious, is addressed:

*Pointing to A.A. literature, respondents averred that the references to God actually mean some "higher power as the individual may understand such higher power," not as the concept would be known by "organized religions." Thus, respondents claimed that the A.A. component of the ASAT Program "does not make specific references to God as an institutional religion would wherein the individual is required to worship, praise, give thanks or petition to a Creator" (Affidavit of Lorraine Cohen, Senior Correctional Counselor for ASAT, N.Y. St. Dept. of Correctional Servs).*

The argument being made by those who say AA is not religious is that references to God should be seen as a personal Higher Power or personal Creator, not a reference to a concept from an organized religion. I would argue that almost no one uses "religion" just to mean an organized method of worship associated with particular institutions.

*As previously noted, the Appellate Division relied upon the A.A. Big Book and the A.A. Twelve Steps/Twelve Traditions texts to find that, despite the repeated references to "God" in the Twelve Steps and Twelve Traditions, A.A. does not " 'demand' " adherence to any particular faith but to " 'spirituality' " and " 'open mindedness' " (id., at 190, 626 N.Y.S.2d 1011 [quoting the A.A. Big Book and A.A. Twelve Steps/Twelve Traditions] ). The Court also found quite significant that A.A. allows participants to select their own conception of God, as shown by the reference in Step 3 to "God as we understood Him."*

This is something you will still hear in recovery circles, but it's really not malicious. There is a genuine belief that the concept of a Higher Power in the program is neutral or universal, and that submitting to one is a critical humbling of the self before recovery can fully happen in order to leave some egoistic past version of oneself behind. You will be told that you can use anything as a Higher Power, but I really doubt you can do the Steps in a meaningful way if you're asking your pet rock to cure you of your "character defects." If you understand God to be an impersonal substrate for existence, you might be able to treat your pet rock as an emergent part of that substrate, but you would still be unable to meaningfully engage with it in the way the Steps ask. If you have to communicate with the Higher Power, you're implicitly being asked to pick a real entity outside of yourself that you can have dialogue with, otherwise you're kind of playing pretend.

*In our view, the Appellate Division erred in rejecting the petition in this case by applying too narrow a concept of religion or religious activity for Establishment Clause analysis and disregarding the compulsion used to induce petitioner to attend and participate in A.A. meetings heavily laced with at least general religious content.*

As pointed out above, to many, "religion" refers to organized methodologies surrounding an institutionalized canon of works and "spirituality" refers to the parts concerning physical, emotional, and/or mental regulation for an individual or a group. This isn't wrong per se; this distinction can be very functional for everyday life to signal that you're not trying to invoke an established religion with its own authorities and canons to explain a ritual or practice. A spiritual practice can seem like a supplement to a religious practice rather than a replacement, which means programs like AA can claim they're a spiritual wrapper for any religion. However, separating religion from spirituality and philosophy is not as easily done as you might think. 

In America, the First Amendment means defining Religion is necessary to enforce laws, Philosophy is a department at universities, and Spirituality can be a catch-all for anything mystical, exotic, immaterial, and/or esoteric without a hierarchical social organization. These categories can feel natural if you've grown up with them, but it's not a distinction that universally makes much sense. Many belief systems begin with a metaphysical framework. Certain Shaivist sects argue that all of reality and existence is of one infinite consciousness that we are born out of. Buddhist argue that all things are impermanent, so an infinite consciousness isn't possible. That might influence whether your conduct includes developing an awareness of nothingness, purifying yourself of pollutants, exploring yourself without inhibitions to see what comes out, performing rituals to work with forces outside yourself, inquiry and contemplation of the relation between things, praying, studying texts, making a pilgrimage, or sitting in a group where you feel good vibes whether it's a place of worship or not. How do you decide which of these is religious and which of these is spiritual?

The court here is aware of this subjectivity around the interpretation of "religion" but concludes that the version being used was far too narrow. The phrase "at least general religious content" helps to clarify that things can be considered religious without referring to a specific system or denomination with institutions.

*Moreover, even if we were to agree with the Appellate Division's holding that the governing principles and practices of A.A., as incorporated in the ASAT Program, do not necessarily require an atheist participant to accept the existence of God in the religious sense, or to engage in religious activity, we would, nonetheless, find that the mandatory and exclusive incorporation of A.A. doctrine and practices in the ASAT program violates Establishment Clause principles requiring governmental neutrality with respect to religion.*

The Court mentions that even if they bought into the argument that no one is required to accept the existence of God to participate in AA, it still doesn't have neutrality on religion. The practices themselves, completely independent of an individual's intention, rise to religious content.

*Concededly, there are passages in A.A. literature, relied upon heavily by respondents, the Appellate Division and the dissent here, which, in stressing the openness and inclusiveness of the A.A. movement, eschew any intent to impose a particular sectarian set of beliefs or a particular concept of God upon participants.*

*However, a fair reading of the fundamental A.A. doctrinal writings discloses that their dominant theme is unequivocally religious, certainly in the broad definitional sense as "manifesting faithful devotion to an acknowledged ultimate reality or deity" (Webster's 9th New Collegiate Dictionary 995 [9th ed 1990]). Indeed, the A.A. basic literature most reasonably would be characterized as reflecting the traditional elements common to most theistic religions.* 

The Court doesn't seem to think that the AA passages about neutrality check out with the "unequivocally religious" nature of the rest of the literature. There is nothing that really stops someone from saying "I believe in a personal God but I am not religious, even though I attend a Christian Church where we read the Bible, recite prayers in circles, proclaim our love for Jesus Christ, and review our past with a particular moral framework to decide what was wrong and requires confession." The Oxford Group's literature actually does do that, as we'll see later.

To do a CTRL+F and replace "God" with "Higher Power" while throwing in some disclaimers doesn't really make it neutral. A well-meaning person once suggested to me that the meetings taking place in Christian churches was just because the churches were friendly to the AA community and had long-lasting relationships. I wasn't quite sure how to say this, but it seemed to me that is exactly the point. If a particular type of religious institution is consistently finding that a certain program with a Higher Power is worth hosting within their halls, that may be because they find their religion to be very compatible with that belief system. If other religious institutions aren't as open, that may suggest less compatibility.

*Thus, God is named or referred to in five of the 12 steps. "Working" the 12 steps includes confessing to God the "nature of our wrongs" (Step 5), appealing to God "to remove our shortcomings" (Step 7) and seeking "through prayer and meditation" to make "contact" with God and achieve "knowledge of His Will" (Step 11 [emphasis supplied]). The 12 Traditions include a profession of belief that "there is one ultimate authority — a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience."*

In my opinion, you could go further and say The Big Book is an analogy to the Bible (which is itself a term for books from an Ancient Greek root), and the 12 steps and traditions arguably seem inspired by the 10 commandments and 12 apostles. I've heard many people in recovery say their daily readings are from either the Bible or the Big Book. If you can use the two interchangeably for personal enlightenment, I would suggest there might be some strong connections in their belief systems.  

*the writings demonstrably express an aspiration that each member of the 682 682 movement will ultimately commit to a belief in the existence of a Supreme Being of independent higher reality than humankind. Thus, in the A.A. Big Book — the basic text of A.A. — chapter l, "Bill's Story," describes the spiritual transformation of one of the cofounders of A.A., in which he finally achieved salvation from his alcoholism: by "enter[ing] upon a new relationship with my Creator * * * [i]t meant destruction of self-centeredness. I must turn in all things to the Father of Light who presides over us all" (A.A. Big Book, at 13-14).*

Creator, Father of Light, salvation, "spiritual transformation", "presiding over", and Supreme Being are all loaded with implications related to religious discourse. But the phrase that actually cements this as a very particular type of religious and spiritual framework is the phrase "independent higher reality than humankind." This is explicitly asserting dualism which numerous religious belief systems explicitly reject at a very fundamental level. Many religions argue that there is only one infinite reality that we observe with our finite measurements, and there is no external entity who resides in another reality that we ascend to. If you're Christian, you can easily see that "independent higher reality" as Heaven, but I'm going to imagine that it's a Bloodborne type of deal if my religion has no equivalent.

*In chapter 4, entitled "We Agnostics" the theme is unambiguously proselytizing: "As soon as we admitted the possible existence of a Creative Intelligence, a Spirit of the Universe underlying the totality of things, we began to be possessed of a new sense of power and direction, provided we took other simple steps. We found that God does not make too hard terms with those who seek Him" (id., at 46). "Instead of regarding ourselves as intelligent agents, spearheads of God's ever advancing Creation, we agnostics and atheists chose to believe that our human intelligence was the last word, the alpha and the omega, the beginning and end of all. Rather vain of us, wasn't it? "We, who have traveled this dubious path, beg you to lay aside prejudice, even against organized religion. We have learned that whatever the human frailties of various faiths may be, those faiths have given purpose and direction to millions. People of faith have a logical idea of what life is all about" (id., at 49).*

A passage about how it is vain to not seek God in a chapter about agnostic perspectives really strains the "spiritual, not religious" label. You cannot say you are neutral in spirituality while characterizing those who aren't part of the dominant interpretation as having moralized "character defects."

*The foregoing demonstrates beyond peradventure that doctrinally and as actually practiced in the 12 step methodology, adherence to the A.A. fellowship entails engagement in religious activity and religious proselytization. Followers are urged to accept the existence of God as a Supreme Being, Creator, Father of Light and Spirit of the Universe. In "working" the 12 steps, participants become actively involved in seeking such a God through prayer, confessing wrongs and asking for removal of shortcomings. These expressions and practices constitute, as a matter of law, religious exercise for Establishment Clause purposes, no less than the nondenominational prayer in Engel v Vitale (370 US 421), that is, "a solemn avowal of divine faith and supplication for the blessings of the Almighty. The nature of such a prayer has always been religious" (id., at 424-425 [emphasis supplied]; see also, Lee v Weisman, 505 US 577, 603-604 [Blackmun, J., concurring]).*

The "God as we understand Him" part becomes a lot narrower when the Higher Power is likened to a Supreme Being, Creator, Father of Light, and Spirit of the Universe. You are not just being asked to agree to God, you're being asked to participate in dialogue with a personal God.

*The dissent's thesis that there was an historical evolution of A.A. doctrine and therapeutic/rehabilitative methodology from the initial, more religious A.A. Big Book to the later, essentially secular, Twelve Traditions (see, dissenting opn, at 699-700, 702-703), is refuted by the writings themselves. Thus, the 1952 A.A. Twelve Steps/Twelve Traditions volume itself states unequivocally in its Foreword that "[t]he book `Alcoholics Anonymous' [the 1939 A.A. Big Book] became the basic text of the Fellowship, and it still is" (A.A. Twelve Steps/Twelve Traditions, at 17 [emphasis supplied]). The Foreword to the 685 685 1952 volume also explains the differing roles of the Twelve Steps and the Twelve Traditions. "A.A.'s Twelve Steps are a group of principles, spiritual in nature, which, if practiced as a way of life, can expel the obsession to drink and enable the sufferer to become happily and usefully whole" (A.A. Twelve Steps/Twelve Traditions, at 15 [emphasis supplied]). "[T]he spiritual ideas of the Society were codified for the first time in the Twelve Steps" (id., at 17 [emphasis supplied]).*

And this should answer those who say AA used to be religious but secularized as it spread. 

You can read the whole case here: https://aaagnostica.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Griffin-v.-Coughlin-1996.pdf. It's very thorough in examining the evidence from AA literature.

Now let's take a look at excerpts from the dissent:

*Many people may believe that A.A. is an entity of spiritual essence or experience. Referenced incorporation of its literature into ASAT to forge a religious alchemy that implicates the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment by some foreboding compulsion feature, however, is not justified or proven. Greater quantum and quality should be required to cross that constitutionally blurred barrier. Indeed, the repeated evocation of a generalized deity figure and symbol or some nondenominational, secular alternative "Higher Power" fails to support this profound absorption of A.A. and ASAT into the territory of a compulsory, constitutionally forbidden religious encounter. We reiterate, in summary, the cogent resolution of this case by the unanimous Appellate Division:*

So the problem is fundamentally one of ignorance, willful or not. "Generalized deity figure" and "alternative 'Higher Power'" are obviously religious concepts. I cannot imagine anyone could say this if they were aware of non-Abrahamic religions, particularly from non-Westernized cultures.

*"[I]t is our conclusion that petitioner has failed to 715 715 make an adequate record to state a claim for an Establishment Clause violation. The petition cites nothing of a religious nature about this particular ASAT program or its practices other than the fact that it is modeled after the principles of AA which make references to 'God' and a 'Higher Power'.* 

We can hop over to *Kerr v. Farrey (1996)* from the 7th Circuit Court for some explicit discussion about theological premises:

*When Kerr first attended the NA meeting, he objected to dragging God's name into “this messy business of addictions,” and he expressed his disagreement with the view of God that was propounded at the meeting.   Kerr regarded NA's deterministic view of God to be in conflict with his own belief about free will;  more generally, he found it offensive to his personal religious beliefs.*

Note that here, we don't have an atheist or an agnostic or someone from a non-monotheistic belief system, but someone who believes in God but from a different monotheistic perspective. 

*Warden Farrey confirmed in her affidavit that inmates were required to “observe” the NA meetings, although she stated that they were not required to “participate.”   She also conceded that “Narcotics Anonymous does use the concept of a ‘higher being’ in their treatment approach.”   This concept, she went on to say, was viewed as a very personal matter and could range from a religious concept of God to the non-religious concept of individual willpower.*

One of the most commonly noted frustrations with attempting to generalize the concepts from AA is the way specific rituals are required but the justification to do them is left for you to fill in. You'll be told that the Higher Power can be an inanimate object, but when it turns out your pet rock isn't granting you the wisdom to know the difference between things you can and can't change, you're just going to have to find a new Higher Power. But you know, God is a very convenient one that will always be there for you if you tire of praying to your pet rock:

*The district court thought that the NA program escaped the “religious” label because the twelve steps used phrases like “God, as we understood Him,” and because the warden indicated that the concept of God could include the non-religious idea of willpower within the individual.   We are unable to agree with this interpretation.   A straightforward reading of the twelve steps shows clearly that the steps are based on the monotheistic idea of a single God or Supreme Being.*

I like to imagine there's a bit of exasperation behind "We are unable to agree with this" followed by "A straightforward reading."    

*True, that God might be known as Allah to some, or YHWH to others, or the Holy Trinity to still others, but the twelve steps consistently refer to “God, as we understood Him.” Even if we expanded the steps to include polytheistic ideals, or animistic philosophies, they are still fundamentally based on a religious concept of a Higher Power.   Kerr alleged, furthermore, that the meetings were permeated with explicit religious content.   This was therefore not a case (again, on the present record) where the only religious note was struck by the insertion of the words “under God” in the Pledge of Allegiance, or other incidental references that the courts have upheld.   See, e.g., Sherman v. Wheeling School District, 980 F.2d 437 (7th Cir.1992).*

The very concept of a Higher Power is fundamentally religious. You just can't do a program based on the premise of having a theistic idea of a Higher Power without that theistic premise being part of the bottom line. The 12 steps run through a cycle of sin, confession, repentance, divine grace, spiritual awakening, and proselytization. That's a feature of the program and why it can work for those who follow certain faiths, and also why it can be deeply alienating to others. Kerr didn't really develop the free will argument much further, but we can at least see that there are reasons even Christians or others following a monotheistic faith might feel alienated by the premises of AA.

# The Oxford Group
I was being a little generous by leading with court case excerpts that examined the literature and practices at face value. If we look at the history, it's very hard to figure out when exactly in the development of this program it became spiritual rather than religious.

Jessie Penn-Lewis was born in 1861 South Wales to a Calvinist Methodist family. Her grandfather was a minister, and her mother worked in the temperance movement. At 19, she married William Penn-Lewis, who claimed a lineage to the William Penn who founded Pennsylvania as a Quaker. She had a leadership position in a temperance movement chapter and the Young Women's Christian Association (YWCA) in Richmond through the Holy Trinity Church. 

That's where she was exposed to vicar Evan H. Hopkins of the Keswick tent revival movement, also referred to as the Higher Life movement after a book called *Higher Christian Life.* The movement's core premise is that there are actually two categories of Christians: those who accept Christ on Earth and count as carnal Christians, and then those who have surrendered to a second conversion that elevates them into spiritual Christians. The phrase "Let Go and Let God" was about this notion of surrendering so that Christ can work through you rather than pursuing spirituality with your own will.

In 1908, following a surge of depression after a financial dispute over a hospice for young men that led to his resignation, the Lutheran minister Frank Buchman traveled to the Keswick convention in the hopes of discussing his problems with a Quaker-influenced Baptist pastor named Frederick Brotherton Meyer. Meyer wasn't there, so Buchman listened to Penn-Lewis preaching to a small group of less than two dozen people instead. Without getting into the specifics of Penn-Lewis' theological propositions just yet, we can see that her warning about centering one's own righteousness rather than the Cross or Holy Spirit would be a thread in the inspired groups going forward. Buchman began to feel that his hatred and rage towards the board over the hospice was actually egoistic pride that his way was righteous because his cause was helping children:

*I sat there and realized how my sin, my pride, my selfishness, and my ill-will had eclipsed me from God... I was the center of my own life. Not Him. That big 'I' had to be crossed out. I saw my resentments against those men standing out like tombstones in my heart.*

He took a moral inventory, confessed his sins, and tried to make amends. So began Buchman's explicit journey to replace his ego with Spirit. When he finally met Meyer later, Meyer asked him: "Do you let the Holy Spirit guide you in all that you are doing... Do you give God enough uninterrupted time really to tell you what to do?" From here comes the notion of meditation to improve conscious contact with God. As Buchman grew a following through the YMCA at Pennsylvania State University, and eventually a global reputation, he settled into a practice based around personal evangelism in intimate circles. 

A harsh analysis of Buchman might conclude that he grew very skeptical of institutions despite his external emphasis on forgiveness, humility, and avoiding self-righteousness. He first had a nervous breakdown after his quarrel over funding with the board members of his hospice. At Hartford, he began holding private personal evangelism groups in parallel to the academic theology of the university, and he refused to comply with university demands to actually adhere to a schedule and curriculum instead of traveling whenever God's will told him to leave. He eventually wrote "The seminary today is an expensive luxury for propagating theology which is oftentimes wholly divorced from life." In 1920, John D. Rockefeller was impressed by Buchman and offered to finance a global evangelist movement based on his works; he rejected the offer with skepticism towards developing an administrative apparatus run by a corporation that would prioritize its own survival as an institution. The Ministerium of Pennsylvania that served as Buchman's Lutheran synod was routinely upset that he ignored financial reporting requirements, geographical boundaries, and theological guidelines. Large anonymous donors tend to raise concerns. A few years later at Princeton, followers of Buchman penetrated the Philadelphian Society to practice "soul surgery," which really meant forming small groups to confess their sexual activities and anxieties. The Princeton president John Grier ordered an investigation after numerous complaints which led to Buchman and his followers being banned from campus. At one point, he had traveled to China as a missionary, but was asked to leave the country after complaints about his attitude towards other missionaries created conflict. Among these was the claim that too much homosexuality was making the missionaries less effective.

Trying his luck in England with house parties around Oxford University, Buchman finally kicked off the Oxford Group as a lasting movement. It helped that he rented out entire hotels and hosted house parties on wealthy estates to lure in students from prestigious universities nearby. I think it's worth flagging this pattern of recruiting students and academics into fledgling movements through lavish parties in proximity to campuses; it certainly will come up again, and it certainly hasn't stopped being a method to recruit young people today. 

The literature from those who participated is very revealing:

*The Oxford Group is not a religion; it has no hierarchy, no temples, no endowments; its workers have no salaries, no plans but God’s Plan; every country is their country, every man their brother. They are Holy Crusaders in modern dress, wearing spiritual armour. Their aim is A New World Order for Christ, the King.*

*The Oxford Group is often confused in the minds of strangers with the Oxford Movement. The former which, by accident rather than by design, uses Oxford as the nominal centre for its activities in England, is a campaign for the renaissance of the practice among men of the truths of simple Christianity; the latter is a Catholic renaissance within the Church of England.*

*The Oxford Group works within churches of all denominations, planning to bring those outside back into their folds and to re-awaken those within to their responsibilities as Christians. **It advocates nothing that is not the fundamental basis of all Christian Faith**, and takes no side in sectarian disputes. This means living as near as we can, by God’s help, to the life He has mapped out for us. When we diverge from His plan for us we can, by surrendering our lives to Him, get back to that plan again, and by our Christian endeavours to live as parallel as we can to Christ’s life here on earth and, with God’s Guidance to show us how we can best do that, we can continue on that plan and bring it to fruition.*

*The aims of the Oxford Group are to bring into the world the realization of the power of the Holy Spirit as a force for spiritual and material stability and betterment of the world*

We can see that "religion" already had a narrow interpretation for the convenience of the Oxford Group. Here, "religion" is a shorthand for organized belief systems that might come with hierarchies, sacred places, scriptural canons, and dedicated capital for expansion. In other words, the Oxford Group would not agree that a practice involving faith in Christ or the Christian God on its own forms a religion or religious belief. But in no way does that temper the ambition for world domination through new Holy Crusades.

*There is no reason why this giving of our lives to God should mean our being pious or religious bores. Neither should it mean that we have to give up our ordinary obligations or duties. It means we surrender to God everything that stands between Him and us*

They give a bit of their reasoning for avoiding a claim to religion here: there's an effort to separate their group's marketing from the rigorous study required of "religious bores." If the program is only a *spiritual* supplement to your existing religion, then it promises a social network of support without requiring you to make specific sacrifices or adhere to specific guidelines. You can have multiple *spiritual* practices, but you generally run into issues if you try to maintain multiple religions. You're less likely to upset your religious family or home church for visiting a spiritual network that claims to encourage the virtues those institutions like rather than replace them. 

We also see that the concept of "surrender" comes directly out of the idea of surrendering in order to allow space for the Holy Spirit to fill you with God's will, which is the ultimate rational plan for the universe, but this "surrender" does not necessarily involve renouncing materialism and worldly attachments. It asks you to "Let Go and Let God" guide you through your life of worldly attachments. This is another reason that "God as we understand him" and "Higher Power" don't remove the religious character of AA: the concept of "surrender" retains its original meaning about supplanting your ego with that of something outside of yourself, and you're going to struggle a lot to make sense of it in a way that doesn't involve a conception of God that is compatible with the Christian understanding. Your pet rock will not be giving you guidance after you surrender to it. And arguably, given that even those of a monotheistic faith have had objections, it's not even generalizable to all Christians.

But hey, I'm not a Christian, maybe you think I just have an axe to grind as someone outside the faith. Allow me to share the arguments made by a Christian contemporary of Buchman who is highly quoted by both the AA community and secular academic institutions.

# Niebuhr and Buchman
*God, grant me the serenity*
*to accept the things*
*I cannot change,*
*Courage to change the*
*things I can, and the*
*wisdom to know the difference.*
*Living one day at a time;*
*Enjoying one moment at a time;*
*Accepting hardship as the*
*pathway to peace.*
*Taking, as He did, this*
*sinful world as it is,*
*not as I would have it.*
*Trusting that He will make*
*all things right if I*
*surrender to His Will;*

*That I may be reasonably happy*
*in this life, and supremely*
*happy with Him forever in*
*the next.*
*Amen*

You might recognize the beginning of this poem by Ronald Niebuhr as the Serenity Prayer, which is recited very often in recovery circles as ways to close out meetings. But that's not the version Niebuhr wrote at first.

*God, give us grace to accept with serenity*
*the things that cannot be changed,*
*Courage to change the things*
*which should be changed,*
*and the Wisdom to distinguish*
*the one from the other.*

Notice the *us,* which is a recurring pattern in Niebuhr's works:

*O God, the sovereign of nations, teach us how we may build a common life in which the nations of the world may find peace and justice. Show us what we ought to do. Show us also what are the limits of our power and what we cannot do. Recall us to our dignity as co-workers with You now and evermore.*

*O God, who has bound us together in this bundle of life, give us the grace to understand how our lives depend upon one another and our responsibilities to You.*

Niebuhr was a leading figure in the Socialist Party of America. Much of his work was about using his faith as a premise for advocacy and community. He was a pacifist prior to WWII, but he eventually adopted the position that justice must be a priority over love since pacifism could not prevent sin from forces like the Axis Powers: 

*"No nation can ever be good enough to save another nation purely by the power of love...justice is probably the highest ideal to which human groups can aspire. And justice...inevitably involves the assertion of right against right and interest against interest until some kind of harmony is achieved... As long as the world of man remains a place where nature and God, the real and the ideal, meet, human progress will depend upon the judicious use of the forces of nature in the service of the ideal... We must try to dissuade Japan from her military venture, but must use coercion to frustrate her designs if necessary."*

So much for not changing people, places, or things, huh?

As the Cold War began, he separated himself from organizations with communist ties in opposition to Soviet Russia, as he had never been a fan of Marxist groups that created a quasi-religious belief system in his opinion. He spoke out against the rise of the KKK in response to a diversifying America, though he maintained a conservative position on segregation. Martin Luther King Jr. and Niebuhr had quoted and supported each other at times. King credits Niebuhr more than Gandhi for the inspiration for nonviolent protest. They both opposed the Vietnam War with a sophisticated analysis of applying Christian virtues to international relations and distributive social justice.

This matters because it contextualizes the Serenity Prayer not as a personal philosophy, but a collective call to action. It doesn't matter if you as an individual cannot completely change society; you have an obligation to try changing some of it as part of a collective that experiences the justices and injustices of society together. In fact, you are obligated to resist the oppression of institutions through force if necessary; Niebuhr argued that dominant classes in power never cede power voluntarily because they're subject to temptations and sins like any individual. But if you read the Serenity Prayer as is popularly quoted out of context, you might think you're just facing a fated challenge that you have to develop the coping skills to endure until it passes. Niebuhr's understanding of Serenity seems to be that it's a psychological tool you could use to strengthen yourself when engaging in justice campaigns without burning out. 

So when Niebuhr criticizes Buchman, it's not just a theological disagreement. While Buchman was hosting high society house parties around Ivy League campuses and Oxford, Niebuhr was in Detroit and observed the brutal labor conditions of workers without unions. The two had remarkably different motivations from the start. Buchman was motivated by changing the world by converting its most powerful authoritarians under a unified agenda, while Niebuhr was concerned with empowering and uplifting communities through collective action. Niebuhr expressed his disgust at Buchman's worldview very openly: 

*We can see how unbelievably naïve this movement is in its efforts to save the world. If it would content itself with preaching repentance to drunkards and adulterers one might be willing to respect it as a religious revival method which knows how to confront the sinner with God... But when it runs to…  Hitler, or to any seat of power, always with the idea that it is on the verge of saving the world by bringing the people who control the world under God-control, it is difficult to restrain the contempt which one feels for this dangerous childishness.*

Oh, I forgot to mention: Buchman tried to convert Hitler to Christianity:

*I thank heaven for a man like Adolf Hitler, who built a front line of defence against the anti-Christ of Communism*

*"Think what it would mean to the world if Hitler surrendered to God... Through such a man God could control a nation overnight and solve every last bewildering problem."*

*Anti-semitism? Bad, naturally. I suppose Hitler sees a Karl Marx in every Jew.*

*Socialists want to change society, and they fail because they don’t change the individual. I want to change the individual, but I want to change the individuals who matter. If I can change Mussolini, I can change Italy. If I can change Hitler, I can change Germany.*

Buchman never got a face-to-face meeting with Hitler, but he did attend the Nuremburg Rallies and Berlin Olympics as a guest of associates of Himmler. That was the extent of his penetration of Nazi leadership. To be perfectly fair here, Buchman was not a Nazi and opposed the violence the Nazis systematically rolled out with industrial scale. He truly believed a Christian dictatorship could emerge to solve society's problems. In general, he was sympathetic to the authoritarian model and sought to convert heads of corporations those like Henry Ford. One of the most common explanations for his behavior was that he was just naive and utopic to a fault with an incredibly oversimplified model of reality. He was never really seen as having a good grasp on reality anyways. By 1939, the Oxford Group was banned from Germany and considered a subversive tactic from the Allied powers. Niebuhr's response:

*The social philosophy of the Oxford group, long implicit in its strategy, is made explicit, and revealed in all its childishness and viciousness*

*The Oxford Group Movement... imagines it possible to clear up the social injustices of our era by a series of personal conversions... It is a religion which satisfies the desires of the privileged classes for a faith which will not disturb their social security.*

Note that he explicitly calls the Oxford Group Movement a religion, and he explicitly links the spread of it to institutions of power and families of privilege. If we were to give the religion a name, Niebuhr would suggest *Buchmanism.* 

# Bill Wilson and the Oxford Group
So after reading all this, we arrive at a kind of awkward position because Bill Wilson said:

*AA's first step was derived largely from my own physician, Dr. Silkworth, and my sponsor Ebby and his Oxford Group friend, Rowland Hazard. I refer to the medical hopelessness of alcoholism - our 'powerlessness' over alcohol. The rest of the Twelve Steps stem directly from those Oxford Group teachings that applied specifically to us... I should acknowledge our great debt to the Oxford Group people. It was fortunate that they laid particular emphasis on spiritual principles that we needed.*

So it's really hard to avoid connecting AA to Buchmanism when Wilson is so vocal about their teachings coming directly from the Oxford Group.

Wilson was born in Vermont in 1895 to a tragic early childhood. He was raised by his grandparents after his parents divorce was followed by abandonment, and his first love died in surgery in 1912. He attended Norwich University and married Louis Burnham in 1918. After a promising military career, he moved to Brookyln and worked on Wall Street through the 1929 crash. At this point, he reports a deep collapse into depression which led to lots of binge drinking and stints in hospitals for detoxing. 

During his fourth detox, he was visited by a previous drinking buddy named Ebby Thacher who credited the Oxford Group for helping him achieve sobriety. He was undergoing the Towns-Lambert regimen which involved a fusion of Atropa belladonna and Hyoscyamus niger, both being hallucinogens known to cause extreme delirium separate from the delirium tremens of withdrawal. In this time, he claims to have experienced a White Light that felt like a spiritual awakening and lost his obsession for drinking, although his fears of a relapse led him to seek another alcoholic to talk to from a church directory: Dr. Bob Smith in Akron, Ohio. They seem to have hit it off, and Smith took his final drink on June 10, 1935 which marks the official founding date of AA. By 1937, after claiming to have helped 40 men through an obsession with drinking, Wilson and Smith approached Rockefeller (well, his spiritual advisor who recognized them as affiliated with the Oxford Group) to ask for funding for hospitals and full-time salaries to be missionaries. 

Supposedly, Rockefeller gave $5000 as a personal donation but refused the $50,000 seed funding for the hospitals and missionary crew, stating "money would ruin this thing." A quick comparison between Buchman's project and Wilson's project might suggest that one was more aligned with Rockefeller's personal interests. Buchman's top-down approach targeted industry and world leaders whose participation in a general spiritual program could provide an extremely valuable network for both opportunities and information. Wilson had an unappealing track record of business and financial dealings since his Wall Street days, and he was trying to help those on the margins of society with little left to lose. The former seemed like an investment in a spiritual movement, the latter seemed like philanthropy for a desperately sick population. 

Before Rockefeller made his decision, he sent out Frank Amos to report on what early AA seemed like, which gives us some idea of what AA was like before The Big Book was consolidated for mass publication. Based on secondhand sources (I'm lazy and will attach a link to the report here once I find it), Amos seems to have observed the following about AA from shadowing the leaders, attending meetings, and interviewing alcoholics and their loved ones.

*1. An alcoholic must realize that he is an alcoholic, incurable from a medical viewpoint, and that he must never again drink anything with alcohol in it.*

*2. He must surrender himself absolutely to God, realizing that in himself there is no hope.*

*3. Not only must he want to stop drinking permanently, he must remove from his life other sins such as hatred, adultery, and others which frequently accompany alcoholism. Unless he will do this absolutely, Smith and his associates refuse to work with him.*

*4. He must have devotions every morning -- a "quiet time" of prayer and some reading from the Bible and other religious literature. Unless this is faithfully followed, there is grave danger of backsliding.*

*5. He must be willing to help other alcoholics get straightened out. This throws up a protective barrier and strengthens his own willpower and convictions.*

*6. It is important, but not vital, that he meet frequently with other reformed alcoholics and form both a social and a religious comradeship.*

*7. Important, but not vital, that he attend some religious service at least once weekly.*

Unquestionably, you can see AA was originally run as a religious and moralistic Bible-based program, even if you don't want to call it Christian or reference any particular denomination. In fact personal prayer based on the Bible was considered non-negotiable compared to meetings with other alcoholics, as well as identifying "sins" in your life and working to purge them. Note that I'm not attacking the efficacy of it. There's plenty of statements about how hopeless some alcoholics and their families felt before attempting to build a recovery program with the AA fellowship. The term "miracle" or "miraculous" seems to come up often. 

As for the mention of a Dr. Silkworth: Benjamin Rush was among the first to characterize alcoholism to an "odious disease" back in 1784, but it took the failures of temperance movements, policymakers, and eugenicists alike for institutions to begin shifting towards a disease model in the 20th century. Rush is remarkably compassionate and insightful about alcoholism; he truly intends to treat it as a disease with a spiritual component where moral failings are a symptom, and he invokes his Christian faith to argue for treating them with compassion even if they have done immoral things. He's fascinating to me, and I'm writing more on him for another time. Throughout the 1800s, the disease model was steadily maintained and developed through clinical movements while temperance movements embraced social, moral, legal, political, and spiritual considerations. 

In 1937, Dr. William D. Silkworth reworked the disease model, which had never enjoyed mainstream consensus up to that point, to have two parts: a physical allergy and a mental obsession. The allergy comparison hasn't literally held up, but tracing alcoholism to a physiological problem on top of a mental one helped it to seem like a legitimate medical issue rather than a pure willpower and moral failing. "Powerlessness" was a reference to this allergy-like reaction where after one drink, an alcoholic can feel uncontrollable urges to have more. During periods of sobriety, there come strong thoughts that one can return to drinking safely, which is termed as obsession. The term "powerlessness" wasn't about literally surrendering power or agency in your life or recovery. That seems like a novel interpretation in order to match the "surrender" to a Higher Power which will fill you with its own will or power instead.

Silkworth wrote *The Doctor's Opinion*, which, as a complete section, is one of the more outdated parts of the AA book. He says one thing that is completely outside of any medical advice or expertise: "You may rely absolutely on anything they say about themselves." This has never been true in the history of the world for any human being. 

So to summarize the history: the root of AA's spiritual principles lies in Buchman's non-denominational interpretation of Higher Life theology which arose from Protestant tent rival movements in Keswick, England and spread to elite colleges as an individualistic spiritual network for the elite and powerful. At minimum, AA started as a deeply religious program that took Oxford Group methods and teachings for an addiction-specific fellowship. Like Niebuhr, Wilson noted that the utopian ambition of saving the world was a bit much, especially for alcoholics who aren't sure if they can even save themselves. He also noted that the Oxford Group was taking a top-down approach towards influence that would consistently exclude the addicts who hit rock-bottom and live on the margins of society, and they were stepping out of the *spiritual* emphasis and into specific *religious* instructions so much that the Catholic Church eventually condemned them. 

I would argue Wilson did not ultimately succeed in producing a secular spiritual program. He did succeed in taking the Oxford Group's ideological project into a different political direction that aimed at grassroots organization instead of controversial hierarchies.

# Personal Experiences
I hate bringing up unverifiable personal experiences, but when they form a pattern and they've been validated by someone else, I feel compelled to share them tentatively as a warning. 

A lot of people in AA frown upon having an understanding of religion and spirituality that doesn't work with the 12 steps. Sometimes it's as explicit as "if God scares you out of these rooms, your addiction will bring you back" or "I don't know how anyone can do this without praying for strength from God" or "I don't care if it offends you, God is the reason I'm here today." The organizations around AA will tell you that there's no central authoritative governing body that can vet safety or quality of meetings. By design, they are not intended to be institutional or professional, which is fine. When you roll the dice, you might have some truly great inclusive groups, and you might have some that make you feel a little on edge for not sharing in their zeal.

Don't get me wrong though, there are plenty of excellent mentors and peers you can find in AA. It remains an accessible way to meet others struggling with substance abuse, and there are plenty who just use it to have a place to share in a group that feels safe rather than work any of the Steps. If you have trouble at one, any town or city that has one group will almost certainly have another, or another that will pop up soon. 

Really good AA groups will also provide access to all kinds of recovery capital, including employment opportunities dedicated for those in recovery, rental assistance to secure safe and stable housing, transportation for meetings and sober outings, directories of local nonprofits, clothes, meals, help navigating the medical system, and just some swell friends you can talk about deep traumas with. Even if you don't work a single step, those resources alone can dramatically increase your quality of life and sustained abstinence.

I just think people should know that it's a religious program. I wouldn't say you should stop going to gurudwaras for free meals, but I'd definitely let you know it's a sacred place of worship for Sikhs. I wouldn't tell you to skip Diwali celebrations, but I would let you know it's a festival that's part of a religious calendar rooted in Hindu cosmology and epics. I wouldn't recommend to anyone that they should never try AA and 12 step programs, but I would say it's religious and not everyone's Higher Power, if they have one, will be compatible with it.

# Some Nuances
These are less direct points, but they're worth mentioning.

## Creator Worship is Not Universal
Not every religion follows the idea that the Creator figure, if they even have one, is an ultimate authority. In fact, not every Christian sect even believes that praying to God would lead to any kind of intervention, that the universe was set in motion a long time ago and the laws of physics and logic are God's laws. There are really cool arguments made by religious scientists positing exactly that.

Many Hindu belief systems see the Creator as the least significant part of their pantheons or trinities. I mean, you've already been created through no volition of your own, for better or for worse. Venerating the Creator is an act of looking to the past rather than the future before you in which you have a purpose to fulfill. Krishna, Shiva, and Vishnu are all actively worshipped while worshipping or building temples for Brahma is discouraged. 

Buddhists often dispense with the idea of gods altogether. Although there are branches that maintain theistic or supernatural beliefs, deities are not assumed to be a primordial Creator or avatars from a single universal consciousness. They're understood to be sentient beings bound by the laws of reincarnation, karma, and dharma like humans. The concept of dependent origination for all things makes an uncaused first cause through a Creator God virtually impossible.

## God is not necessarily a Him or a Father
To many, God sits above human constructs like gender roles or sex as determined by physical genitalia. To others, God can manifest in any gender or sex if so desired. In some Hindu traditions, the personification of the trinity of male deities can be a supreme female deity like Durga. Framing a Higher Power as a necessarily masculine Father can be an intersection of Creator worship and particular parent-gender dynamics, which is very far from neutral or universally applicable.

# It Really is Okay to Be Religious
Seriously, I'm not complaining about the efficacy of AA in this page. It clearly does work to keep lots of people sober. What motivates me to spend this much time establishing the religiosity of AA is that it doesn't work for everyone, and AA principles can set up a framework to blame you for that. It's a part of the opening script to meetings called *How It Works*: 

*Rarely have we seen a person fail who has thoroughly followed our path. Those who do not recover are people who cannot or will not completely give themselves to this simple program, usually men and women who are constitutionally incapable of being honest with themselves. There are such unfortunates. They are not at fault; they seem to have been born that way. They are naturally incapable of grasping and developing a manner of living which demands rigorous honesty. Their chances are less than average. There are those, too, who suffer from grave emotional and mental disorders, but many of them do recover if they have the capacity to be honest.*

*If you have decided you want what we have and are willing to go to any length to get it — then you are ready to take certain steps. At some of these we balked. We thought we could find an easier, softer way. But we could not. With all the earnestness at our command, we beg of you to be fearless and thorough from the very start. Some of us have tried to hold on to our old ideas and the result was nil until we let go absolutely.*

*Remember that we deal with alcohol — cunning, baffling, powerful! Without help it is too much for us. But there is One who has all power — that One is God. May you find Him now! Half measures availed us nothing. We stood at the turning point. We asked His protection and care with complete abandon.*

Taken in sum, you are to blame if you have not chosen to embrace a benevolent and omnipotent God as the sole Power to solve the Powerlessness you have to admit to in Step 1. You have been born in a way that has left you "constitutionally incapable of being honest" enough to address your emotional and mental disorders. If you don't pray for God and His protection (capital G, capital H), you're seeking a softer and easier way than the difficulty of a spiritual awakening through a conversion from the 12 Steps. You are one of the "unfortunates."

I'm not going to say this is right or wrong as theology or metaphysics or ethics. I'll reiterate this constantly just as I did at the top: if this program works for you, great. If this method works for you and you want to share it with people, that's fine. It's free and anonymous, you never have to come back if you hate it. You don't need to sign a waiver or join an email list or anything. No blood pacts with demons or timeshare salesmen. Others have called it a cult, and if so, it's the easiest cult to walk away from if you decide it's not for you.

I just think if you were born with a different background than someone who would have fit in at the Oxford Group, the problem isn't you. The problem is not that you have a different faith or religion than the branch of religion called Buchmanism. You are not an "unfortunate." You are not "constitutionally incapable of being honest" or having the self-awareness to make meaningful progress in your life. You do not have to surrender yourself into Powerlessness and seek a Higher Power out of moral necessity. You can want a different approach than a one-size-fits-all method developed almost a hundred years ago by a very monolithic society that did not understand how most cultures, religions, governments, or nations of the world worked. The method might be great for the ones it works for, just like medications can be great but you would never blame someone if the medication didn't work. If they stopped taking it because it wasn't working and produced unwelcome side effects, we would think it rational to see a professional to chart a new course of action for your unique self.

Everyone's body, mind, and soul are different. That has to be acknowledged if we want to really be inclusive about recoveryy. Just consider the widely accepted alternatives we now have: Recovery Dharma, SMART Recovery, Refuge Recovery, LifeRing Secular Recovery, etc. Chances are you'll probably still end up going to an AA meeting since that's the default option people are aware of, but know that you have other recovery options too that don't reflect poorly on you if you choose them.
