## Max Weber
If you take AP Government & Politics, you might briefly touch on Weber and his theories about bureaucracy. He actually had ideas that in hindsight are very wild.

Max Weber had a theory of personality that framed an American Puritan set of ideals as the most desirable to cultivate and institutionalize. He was seeking to articulate a theory of personality in society that would be characterized by their anti-authoritarianism and autonomy, not overtly seeking a model of subjugation. He tried to survey systems like Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, and Judaism to find the unique aspects of the Puritan personality based on his limited understanding of these societies and their histories. 

From India and China, he interpreted the "Asiatic personality" as traditional and irrational, and therefore incapable of developing into fully autonomous people. He wished that the German education system would try to cultivate the Puritan personality, especially as a solution to developing new political leadership in the wake of a revolution. When we create binaries like this, they tend to permeate and replicate themselves in other constructions of the world. If the ideal political leader was a Puritan Occidental, then the civil servant who should follow this leader with a personality should be a non-personality like his profile of Asiatic (non-)personalities. Sara R. Farris' work on Max Weber should be sought for further reading on this specific pedagogical-political model.

My point in bringing up Max Weber is to reflect how our current education system still has a narrative about itself that precludes how much re-education happens, lest it reveal how fluid institutions are and how brittle grand narratives can be. We can certainly credit him for advancing ideas about bureaucratic organization from a sociological perspective, but it's strange that we skip over the part that his leader-servant binary was so closely tied to his ideals of Puritan Occidental personality and Asiatic non-personality, and that he explicitly wanted the German educated elite to adopt the former. 

Because we can't simply turn these concepts neutral by taking an approach that blinds us to why we even include them in syllabi. The presumption of a distinct Western rationality that grew out of some Puritan Occidental pedagogical-political structure of society is one that Western tellings of history still promote. We like to tell the story of a Puritan Reformation that empowered the individual into literacy, then an Enlightenment movement that articulated rational discourse towards a justification for organizing society, then the First Amendment happened which allowed elites to create a republic that debates ideas openly, then we abolished slavery so that marginalized groups could receive the wonderful Western education that allows them to advocate for themselves under civil rights frameworks, then in 2008 we made racism irrelevant thanks to the unique social progressivism of elite colleges, their alumni network, and consumers voting with their wallet. 

Within this context, Weber seems like part of an imperfect-but-improving rational discourse whose influence is waning in comparison to modern social trends. On the other hand, one might argue that much of our modern rivalry with China comes out of a deep existential identity crisis that if an Eastern nation ends up more technologically, militarily, and economically successful in the long-run, the myth of inevitable progress through Western rationality derived from Enlightenment ideals might just collapse. We are still engaged in negotiating our identity across some Western vs Eastern ideals.
