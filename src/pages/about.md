# About MankNotes
I read a lot, and I write a lot. Some of it might be useful to people. I aim to keep this as accessible as possible. I will never have a paywall to access any information I post here. I monetize this site through directory subscriptions, personal education through office hours, and donations.

There is no more awe to be experienced anywhere more than the world of addiction and recovery. I haven't found anything that's more interdisciplinary as an intellectual project while constantly challenging the premises we take for granted about life. At the same time, even our most educated decision-makers apply pre-phrenology pseudoscience to all things mental health. Every social institution has completely fumbled their approach to addressing addiction in ways that are very revealing about the systemic failures of their management paradigms. There's a fascinating and baffling chasm between what we can learn and what we have an interest in knowing, and that chasm is worthy of study itself.

Partly, it's not a subject for the faint of heart or the "just doing my job" types. You will see a lot of barbaric, medieval-era policies implemented because of rhetoric and vibes instead of reason and values. You will encounter incredibly complex theories about the nature of suffering, identity, and existence from cultures around the world. You will get to know people through their childhood traumas before their favorite bands. You will see young promising people die painful, preventable deaths. You will see people who were homeless, penniless, and on the verge of death becomes lawyers and researchers. You feel how brittle the veneer of objectivity is that surrounds our scientific and medical institutions. You catch your own assumptions about people and their backgrounds, and you feel ashamed of having judged what you didn't know, of casting so many stones from a glass house. 

Importantly, it humbles you. You realize how little we as a species understand ourselves, because you experience how reluctant we are to learn uncomfortable truths about ourselves. It can become extraordinarily difficult to relate to people outside of the mental health space after you embrace the discomforting freedom of honesty. For most people, that's a choice of relative inconveniences. People in recovery will tell you that the choice is life or death. It's a pleasure to be around so many people who make checking in on each other a priority in their day and call each other out on the convenient lies we tell ourselves. Even when I don't like someone I meet, I can admire the resilience and growth mindset it takes to change the trajectory of their whole life.

I don't find addiction to be embarrassing. I find it embarrassing to be part of a society that fails to address it. We have evidence-backed common-sense solutions, but we seem to be either too ignorant, too greedy, or too complacent to act on them. I hope we can do better.

# About Mank

Everybody is biased. Some people are honest about that, and some people aren't. I considered attempting a neutral and balanced tone when assembling this site, but I often criticize Wikipedia for bias when that's perhaps the best you can hope for out of any encyclopedic format. So I won't pretend to be an objective source. 

I am deeply skeptical of institutions, hierarchy, and authority. I grew up with Hindu heritage which I bring to philosophy, religion, and spirituality. They all sit on the same shelf to me. As such, my framework for health is that we ought to reduce our individual egos and focus on changing systemic incentives behind common behaviors. My impression of contemporary American psychology is that it's rooted in a hyperindividualistic perspective that the only agency people have is changing their mindset, and concepts from "Eastern" religions are tortured to fit that frame. 

I'm reluctant to categorize things as good or bad. My approach to doing research for articles resembles something like amateur literature surveys. I frequently change my mind and revise my rhetoric to match.
